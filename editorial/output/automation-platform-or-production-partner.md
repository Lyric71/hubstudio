# Automation multiplies, studios originate: platform or production partner

The comparison is usually framed as software against services, which is the
wrong axis. The real question is who makes the thing being multiplied, and the
automation layer answers that question in its own technical specification.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## Do I need a creative automation platform or a production studio?

Both, almost always, and for different jobs. A creative automation platform
assembles and serves variations of assets that already exist. It has no
mechanism for originating one, which its own published standard makes
explicit. A production studio such as hubStudio makes the master that the
platform then multiplies. The decision is not which to buy, it is how much of
your work is origination versus adaptation.

Worth saying why this piece exists. Of the thirty-six results returned across
four ways of asking this question, twenty-five were published by companies
selling software or services in the category. Ask the question the other way
around, "who makes the master creative", and the search returns nothing on
topic at all. So the studio half of a two-sided question has simply never been
answered in public.

<!-- SECTION: What the layer is -->

## What does a creative automation platform actually do?

Strip the marketing away and go to the standard. The category has a published
technical specification, written by a standards body rather than by a vendor,
and it is unusually clear about what the thing is.

> A dynamic content ad is defined by its published standard as "a structured
> system of meta-data for creative components and asset variations of the
> creative components that may be used in an advertisement", with ad serving
> systems using that schema "to make real time decisions on which assets to use
> for a particular impression".
> Source: industry standards body, Dynamic Content Ad Specification, schema
> version 1.0, September 2016. Published technical standard, open repository.

Read that carefully. Components, variations, and a decision about which
existing asset to use. Every noun in the definition presupposes that the
assets are already there.

The trade body definition of the optimization layer says the same thing in
plainer language.

> A digital advertising trade body defines dynamic creative optimization as "the
> automated process of assembling and testing different combinations of ad
> elements (such as images, headlines and calls-to-action) in real time to show
> the most effective version of an ad to each user".
> Source: national digital advertising trade body glossary, definition current
> September 2026, matched against the published technical standard.

Assembling and testing combinations of elements. Not making elements.

And the sequence is stated explicitly by the same trade body, which matters
because it settles the order of operations rather than leaving it as a matter
of opinion.

> A trade body whitepaper describes the sequence plainly: creative optimization
> "only comes into play after two stages have been completed: employing dynamic
> creative to build multiple versions of an ad and then tailoring these ad
> variants for different audience segments".
> Source: national digital advertising trade body, The Role of Creative in
> Digital Advertising Effectiveness, July 2018, council-authored whitepaper.

Two stages have to be completed first. The platform is stage three.

<!-- SECTION: Where it stops -->

## Where does the automation layer's capability stop?

At the exact point where an asset does not exist. And the standard says what
happens there, which is the most useful sentence in this entire argument.

> The same standard requires that every creative component define a fallback
> asset: "It is required to force the developer to define at least one fall back
> and not leave this undecided." An undefined component is a failure state to be
> caught, not a request for something new to be made.
> Source: industry standards body, Dynamic Content Ad Specification, schema
> version 1.0, September 2016.

That is the boundary, written into the specification by the people who defined
the category. The automation layer has a defined behavior for a missing asset.
The behavior is to substitute an asset that already exists. Nothing anywhere in
the standard describes creating one, because creating one is not a thing the
layer does.

This is not a criticism. A fallback requirement is good engineering. It is
simply a precise answer to the question of where the layer's capability ends,
and it comes from the category's own documentation rather than from someone
with a studio to sell.

So the honest framing is not platform against studio. It is: something has to
produce the components before the schema has anything to reference.

<!-- SECTION: Why the master matters -->

## Does it matter how good the master is, if you can test enough variants?

Yes, and by a larger margin than the volume argument assumes. This is where
the evidence is strongest, because it comes from measurement firms and
peer-reviewed journals rather than from anyone selling either layer.

> Advertising creative drives nearly half, 49 percent, of the incremental sales
> generated by advertising, ahead of brand at 21 percent, reach at 14 percent,
> targeting at 11 percent and recency at 5 percent.
> Source: US advertising measurement study, August 2023, modeling the
> proportional contribution of 18 campaign features across nearly 450 US
> consumer packaged goods campaigns in digital and TV against purchase data.

That is one study, so here is the same finding six years earlier on a
different sample.

> The earlier edition of the same study put creative at 47 percent of the impact
> on sales, with a further 2 percent for context, so the finding has held at
> roughly half of sales lift across six years and two samples.
> Source: US advertising measurement study, August 2017 edition, nearly 500 US
> studies from 2016 and 2017, restated by a national digital advertising trade
> body in July 2018.

Roughly half, twice, across two samples six years apart. Targeting, the thing
the automation layer exists to serve, comes in at 11 percent.

The academic work says something more specific about what "good" means.

> A meta-analysis of advertising creativity covering 93 data sets from 67 papers
> and 878 effect sizes found robust positive effects, and that both originality
> and appropriateness have to be present for creativity to pay.
> Source: Journal of Marketing, volume 84 issue 6, pages 39 to 56, 5 August
> 2020. Peer-reviewed meta-analysis with moderator tests.

Both, not either. Original but wrong for the brand does not pay. Appropriate
but unremarkable does not pay.

And then there is the finding that connects creative quality directly to
variant volume, which is the one that should change how a media plan is built.

> In a controlled repetition experiment, ads high in divergence and relevance
> "wear in immediately and show little sign of wearing-out even over repeated
> exposures", while low-creativity ads followed "the classic inverted U-Shape"
> and declined with repeated exposure.
> Source: Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, volume 44 issue 3, pages
> 334 to 349, 2016. Two-by-two-by-three between-subjects experiment across six
> dependent variables.

Sit with the consequence. A strong master gets better with repetition. A weak
one degrades. Multiplying a weak master into four hundred variants does not
average the problem away, it buys reach for a curve that is bending downward.
Volume is a multiplier, and it multiplies the sign of whatever it is applied
to.

One caveat, stated plainly because the temptation to overclaim here is real.
No study compares templated output against originated output head to head.
None of the above proves that a platform-assembled variant underperforms a
studio-made one. What it establishes is narrower and still decisive: creative
quality carries roughly half of advertising's sales effect, and quality is a
property of the master.

<!-- SECTION: The handoff -->

## Where is the handoff between the two layers?

At the component boundary defined in the standard. Everything upstream of a
finished, approved component is origination. Everything downstream is
assembly, versioning, targeting and serving.

| Stage | Who does it | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Idea and proposition | Studio, with the brand | Judgment, category knowledge, a point of view |
| Direction and casting | Studio | People, decisions, taste |
| Capture or generation | Studio | Stages, talent, models, rights |
| Retouch and finish | Studio | Craft, and a definition of done |
| Component library and schema | Handoff point | Naming, metadata, fallbacks defined |
| Assembly into variants | Platform | Rules and a feed |
| Targeting and rotation | Platform | Audience data |
| Measurement and optimization | Platform, with media | Traffic and time |

The row that gets skipped is the handoff. A component library is not a folder
of exports: it is named assets with defined fallbacks, which is a production
deliverable and belongs in the scope of whoever makes the masters.

<!-- SECTION: Cost -->

## What does each layer cost?

Different units, which is the reason the comparison usually goes wrong. One
prices access per person per month, the other prices delivered assets.

> Published seat pricing in this software category runs from roughly $12 to
> roughly $400 per user per month, most of it billed annually, and every pricing
> page read carried an enterprise tier with no published price at all.
> Source: published pricing pages in this software category, collected
> 9 September 2026, four pages read in full, no vendor named and no vendor
> contacted.

> Managed design subscriptions in this category publish monthly floors between
> roughly $6,000 and $15,000, with dedicated tiers from roughly $30,000.
> Source: category rate cards collected September 2026, published pricing pages
> only, no vendor named.

| | Automation layer | Production layer |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of purchase | A seat, per month | A delivered asset or an engagement |
| What scales the bill | Number of users | Volume and complexity of work |
| Marginal cost of variant 400 | Near zero | Low, if it derives from a master |
| Marginal cost of a new master | Not offered | The actual cost |
| Published pricing | Partial, enterprise tier withheld | Partial, floors published |

The middle row is where the money actually is. Both layers are cheap at
variant four hundred. Only one of them can produce variant zero, and variant
zero is the one carrying roughly half the sales effect.

Set against what a marketing budget has available:

> Production now takes an average of 19 percent of total marketing budgets,
> against 24 percent before the pandemic, while nearly two thirds of respondents
> expect to increase social media content production.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> survey of 50 global brands across more than 10 sectors, mostly senior
> marketing procurement respondents.

Five points of budget share gone while output expectations rose. That squeeze
is the reason the automation layer sells so well, and it is also the reason
origination gets quietly under-resourced.

<!-- SECTION: Complementary -->

## Do you already own the automation layer?

Probably, and that changes the question from "which do I buy" to "what is the
gap in what I already run".

> 66 percent of brands now run an in-house agency and another 21 percent are
> actively considering one, a 16-point rise on 2020, while 56 percent expect to
> move more digital production in-house within three years.
> Source: global advertiser trade body in-housing research, December 2023,
> survey of 45 multinational companies with an estimated combined annual global
> ad spend of $60 billion.

> Inside those in-house teams, 65 percent are experimenting with AI and
> 71 percent are partially implementing it, 93 percent plan to invest further
> within 12 to 24 months, and just 12 percent describe themselves as fully
> integrated. The gains reported are throughput gains: 40 percent cite faster
> content production, 33 percent cite increased efficiency.
> Source: global advertiser trade body research on in-house agencies, November
> 2025, quantitative survey plus 30 in-depth interviews with in-house agency
> leaders.

Note what the reported gains are. Faster, more efficient. Throughput. Nobody
reports that the tooling improved the idea, because that is not what tooling
is for.

Which lines up with the capability gap the same constituency reports.

> 71 percent of marketers said their business was ambitious about delivering
> creative excellence, but only 37 percent felt their business had the right
> capabilities to deliver ground-breaking ideas.
> Source: advertiser trade body and marketing intelligence service, September
> 2024, survey of more than 300 brand owners worldwide fielded January to
> February 2024.

Ambition at 71, capability at 37. That gap does not close by adding a seat.

<!-- SECTION: Decision -->

## How do you decide, then?

On three axes. Volume, variation count, and the share of the work that is
originated rather than adapted. The third is the one that decides it, and it is
the one nobody asks.

| Your situation | What you need |
|---|---|
| High volume, few masters, heavy versioning | Automation layer, and a studio for the masters when they refresh |
| Low volume, every asset originated | Studio. Automation has almost nothing to multiply |
| High volume AND high origination, many categories or markets | Both, with the handoff scoped explicitly in the production contract |
| You own the platform and output still feels flat | Origination gap, not a tooling gap. Look upstream |
| You buy masters but versioning is late every time | Automation gap. The masters are fine |

Be honest about the fourth row. Flat output with a fully implemented platform
is the most common situation behind this search, and it does not get better by
switching platforms.

**No benchmark sets the crossover point.** There is no published, methodical
figure for how many variants a campaign needs, and no independent source
defines the volume at which one layer becomes better value than the other.
Every number circulating on that question traces back to a company selling
variant production. So the table above is built from what each layer does
rather than from a threshold, and anyone quoting you a specific asset count as
the tipping point is quoting a sales figure.

The hub4you page covers the platform side of how hubStudio works with brands
running their own team, the AI excellence page covers the generation pipeline
behind the masters, and the pricing page sets out how the origination side is
scoped and quoted.

## Questions buyers ask about platform against studio

**Can a creative automation platform replace a production studio?**

No, and its own standard says why. The published specification for dynamic
content ads requires every creative component to define a fallback asset,
because the defined behavior when an asset is missing is to substitute one
that exists. There is no mechanism in the standard for producing a new
component, since production is not what the layer does.

**What does creative automation actually do?**

It assembles and serves combinations of creative elements that already exist,
using a metadata schema so an ad server can choose which asset to show for a
given impression. A trade body describes optimization as coming into play only
after multiple versions have been built and tailored to segments, so assembly
is stage three rather than stage one.

**Who makes the master creative?**

Whoever does direction, casting, capture or generation, and finishing. That is
a production function whether it sits inside your company, inside an agency,
or inside a studio. The automation layer consumes the output of that work and
does not perform it.

**Do I need both a platform and a studio?**

Usually, and most brands already have the platform half. In December 2023, 66
percent of surveyed multinationals ran an in-house agency and 56 percent
expected to move more digital production in-house within three years. The
practical question is which half of the pipeline is currently under-resourced.

**When is automation alone enough?**

When your work is genuinely adaptation: a stable set of approved masters,
versioned across sizes, languages and audiences, refreshed infrequently. If
that describes your year, the origination requirement is small and buying it
occasionally is reasonable.

**Does creative quality really matter more than targeting?**

On the available measurement, yes, by a wide margin. A US measurement study
across nearly 450 consumer packaged goods campaigns attributed 49 percent of
incremental sales to creative and 11 percent to targeting, and an earlier
edition on a different sample put creative at 47 percent. Repetition research
adds that strong creative resists wear-out while weak creative declines with
exposure.
