# Product photography cost per SKU, from 50 SKUs to 5,000

Every page answering this question quotes a day rate, which tells you what a
photographer costs and nothing about what your catalog costs. The number you
need is per image, then multiplied by a figure only you know.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How much does product photography cost per SKU?

Published studio cards put standard ecommerce capture between roughly $39 and
$50 an image in September 2026. Per SKU, multiply that by however many images
each of your SKUs actually needs.

That second number has no market answer. Nobody publishes an average number of
images per SKU, because it is a decision about your catalog rather than a fact
about the market: a t-shirt in four colorways is not a watch. So this page
prices the image, publishes the volume curve, and leaves that multiplier to you
rather than inventing one.

<!-- SECTION: the bands -->

## What published cards actually charge

Read one card at a time, not aggregated.

> Published studio rate cards put standard ecommerce capture between roughly $39
> and $50 an image, with complexity uplifts on one card to $75 for ghost
> mannequin or oversized work, $100 for jewelry and watches, $150 for a hand
> model image and $190 for a full-body model image.
> Source: published product photography pricing pages in this category, read
> directly on 10 September 2026, two independent cards, prices quoted against
> the unit each card states.

Note the spread inside one card. A jewelry image costs twice a plain product
image and a full-body model image costs nearly four times it, so a catalog mix
moves your average more than any discount you negotiate.

The same shape appears in a second market, which is worth knowing before anyone
tells you offshore capture is a different economics.

> A published Chinese ecommerce photography rate card, quoting its own executing
> rates for the Guangzhou and Pearl River Delta region, prices flat lay at 80 to
> 150 RMB an image, hanging shots at 100 to 180, in-studio model work at 200 to
> 300, scene images at 150 to 300 and retouch at 50 to 200, with a five-style
> minimum and 6 to 10 images per style.
> Source: a published Chinese ecommerce photography rate card, 20 July 2026, the
> publisher's own executing rates for one region, read in Chinese.

Different currency, same structure: flat lay cheapest, model work several times
more, retouch billed separately. The card also states a minimum, which is the
first hint that small runs are priced differently from large ones.

## Does cost per SKU fall at volume?

Yes, and then it stops, and where it stops is the useful part.

The discount in this category is usually not a lower list price. It is sold as a
membership you buy before you get the rate.

> The published volume discount in this category is sold as a membership, not as
> a lower list price. One card publishes annual memberships at $480, $2,340 and
> $7,500 a year buying 10, 20 and 30 percent off product work.
> Source: a published product photography pricing page in this category, read
> directly on 10 September 2026. The ladder appears on a single card.

A membership is a bet on your own volume, so the first question is where it pays
for itself. Divide each fee by what the discount saves on one image and the
answer falls out.

> Dividing each annual membership fee by the money it saves on one standard
> image gives the breakeven: 96 images a year on the 10 percent tier, 234 on the
> 20 percent tier and 500 on the 30 percent tier.
> Source: arithmetic on a published pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026. Fee divided by percentage saving on the card's own $50
> standard image, no other input.

Under 96 images a year, the cheapest tier costs you money. That is a
straightforward test you can run against last year's actuals before anyone
quotes you.

Now the ceiling, which is the finding.

> The published discount ladder stops at 30 percent off list. Past roughly 500
> images a year, buying more photography on a published card does not make
> photography meaningfully cheaper.
> Source: published product photography pricing pages in this category, read 10
> September 2026. The deepest published tier on any card read was 30 percent.

And it is not a quirk of one market.

> In both markets read for this piece, the published volume discount tops out at
> about 30 percent off list.
> Source: published pricing surfaces in this category in two markets, September
> 2026. Two independent ladders that agree, not a measured market average.

Two independent ladders, two currencies, the same ceiling. Whatever your
procurement team believes about leverage at scale, the published cards say the
leverage runs out at 500 images a year.

## The curve, with the arithmetic shown

Carrying that ladder across volumes gives the actual curve.

> Carried across the published ladder, effective capture falls from $50 an image
> at list to about $46.60 at 300 images, $41.25 at 1,200, $37.50 at 3,000,
> $35.63 at 12,000 and $35.25 at 30,000. A tenfold rise in volume above 3,000
> images moves the unit price about 6 percent.
> Source: arithmetic on a published pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026. List price times the card's own discount, plus the annual fee,
> divided by images. One card, one currency, no market average implied.

To turn that into per-SKU numbers you need images per SKU, and since no source
publishes an average, the table below uses six. Six is not a benchmark. It is a
legible number that makes the arithmetic checkable, and you should rerun it with
your own.

| Catalog size | Images at six per SKU | Effective per image | Per SKU |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 SKUs | 300 | about $46.60 | about $280 |
| 200 SKUs | 1,200 | about $41.25 | about $248 |
| 500 SKUs | 3,000 | about $37.50 | about $225 |
| 2,000 SKUs | 12,000 | about $35.63 | about $214 |
| 5,000 SKUs | 30,000 | about $35.25 | about $212 |

Read the last two rows together. Going from 2,000 SKUs to 5,000, more than
doubling the catalog, saves about two dollars a SKU. Everything worth winning on
this curve is won before you reach 500 SKUs.

That is the sentence the day-rate pages never print, and it changes what you
should be negotiating. Above the ceiling, arguing about the rate is close to
pointless. What is left to move is the number of images and the share of them
that need a camera at all.

## What does a photography day rate not cover?

Fixed costs, mostly, and they behave very differently at small and large runs.

> A separate published card charges $39 a photo plus a fixed $149 studio fee per
> booking. That fixed fee is about 62 percent of the unit price when six images
> come out of one booking, $63.83 an image, and under 1 percent at six hundred
> images, $39.25 an image. The list price never moved.
> Source: a published product photography pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026, with the fee amortized arithmetically across three run sizes.

The list price never moved. That is worth pausing on: two buyers quoted the same
$39 pay $63.83 and $39.25 for the identical image, purely on how many images
came out of one booking. Batching is a bigger lever than negotiating here, and
it costs nothing to pull.

Credit blocks work the same way in a different wrapper.

> Credit blocks on one published card run $1,500 for 25 credits, $2,500 for 50
> and $4,500 for 100, which is $60, $50 and $45 a credit. A fourfold larger block
> buys 25 percent off the unit.
> Source: a published product photography pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026, block price divided by block size.

Then there is what appears on no card at all.

> No card read for this piece prices sample logistics, reshoots or approval
> rounds. Eight published price surfaces across two markets, and getting the
> product to the studio, getting it back, and shooting it again after a failed
> approval appear on none of them.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, two markets, read directly
> in September 2026. Absence confirmed by reading each card in full.

Eight surfaces, two markets, and not one prices the three things most likely to
blow a catalog schedule. Sample movement in particular is the line that decides
whether a 2,000-SKU shoot takes six weeks or four months, and it is invisible in
every quote you will compare.

The useful way to hold all of this is as a fixed and variable split, because the
two behave in opposite directions. The per-image rate is variable and barely
moves once you clear the ladder. The booking fee, the platform fee and the
membership are fixed, and their weight per image collapses as the run grows.
Sample logistics and reshoots sit outside both, scaling with how many separate
sessions you run rather than with how many images come out of them.

Which points at a scheduling conclusion the rate sheet cannot give you. Fewer,
larger sessions beat more, smaller ones at every volume, and the saving from
consolidating bookings is usually larger than the saving from negotiating the
rate.

| What moves the number | Published on cards | Who absorbs it |
|---|---|---|
| Per-image rate | Yes | Priced |
| Volume discount | Yes, capping at 30 percent | Priced |
| Fixed booking or platform fee | Yes, as a flat line | You, and heavily at low volume |
| Catalog complexity mix | Yes, as uplifts | Priced, but only if scoped honestly |
| Sample logistics | No | You |
| Reshoots after failed approval | No | You |
| Approval rounds beyond the first | No | You |

## How much does ecommerce retouching cost per image?

Far less than capture, and confusing the two is how buyers end up comparing
quotes that are not comparable.

> Published ecommerce retouch cards put the floor between roughly $0.25 and
> $1.20 an image by operation, with one card adding a fixed platform fee of
> about $95 a month on top.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, two
> independent cards read directly and compared, no vendor named.

Put that against capture and the gap is not a negotiating range.

> Against published capture of $39 to $50 an image, a per-image retouch price of
> about $0.95 is between 1.9 and 2.4 percent of the capture line, and the full
> published retouch band runs from about 0.5 to about 3.1 percent of it. Retouch
> is not production.
> Source: two published pricing categories, both collected September 2026,
> divided one into the other. Capture from two studio cards, retouch from two
> editing cards, all four read directly.

Around two percent. When someone quotes you a per-image figure that sounds
impossibly low, check which of those two things they are selling, because the
answer is almost always the cheaper one.

Retouch has its own volume floor, and it is steeper than photography's.

> One published retouch card guarantees 200 edited images a day with
> next-morning delivery, and declines anyone under 1,000 images a year, sending
> them to a trial instead. Its $95 monthly platform fee doubles the unit price at
> 100 images a month and adds about 10 percent at 1,000.
> Source: a published image editing pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026. The access condition and the fee are stated on the card, the
> amortization is division.

A supplier that turns away small buyers is telling you its economics only work
above a threshold. At 100 images a month that platform fee doubles what you pay
per image, which makes the headline rate meaningless at your volume.

## When is generation cheaper than shooting?

At the point where the ladder stops helping, which the curve above puts at
around 500 images a year.

Be careful with the reasoning here, because this is where cost pages usually
overclaim. What the published evidence supports is narrow: capture prices stop
falling with volume, while the number of images you need does not stop rising.
Anything that reduces the count of images requiring a camera therefore attacks
the only variable still moving.

hubStudio publishes a mix figure for its own delivery, and it is worth reading
for what it is.

> hubStudio publishes that roughly 70 percent of its delivered imagery is
> generated and 30 percent captured. That is a published delivery mix on
> hubStudio's own pages, not a market ratio and not a measurement with a stated
> sample or period.
> Source: hubStudio site copy, September 2026, attributed to the page that
> publishes it.

No claim is made here about where that ratio moves with catalog size, because no
sample and no period sit behind it. It is one studio's published mix, offered so
you know the shape of the thing being described rather than as evidence for a
decision.

The decision itself is more usefully made per asset than per catalog.

| If this is true of the asset | Points to |
|---|---|
| The product physically exists and its material or fit must be literally accurate | Capture |
| It is a colorway, angle or background variant of something already captured | Generation from the captured master |
| It is a scene, context or lifestyle setting around an existing product plate | Generation, with the plate captured |
| The listing image is the legally operative one for the platform | Capture, then treat everything downstream as versioning |
| Nobody has ever photographed this product | Capture first, always |

The pattern in that table is that capture makes the master and generation
multiplies it. That is a production sequence rather than a choice between two
suppliers, and it is why the useful question is what share of your images need a
camera, not which vendor is cheaper per image.

rather than a quote.

## How this page was built

Every band was read directly from a published price surface in September 2026,
one card at a time, and is quoted against the unit that card states. Where a
figure is derived, the arithmetic is shown on the page and the inputs are named.
Bands built from two or more independent cards are given as ranges. A band drawn
from a single card is labeled as such. Nothing here comes from an aggregator, a
roundup or a vendor's estimate of somebody else's prices.

Two limits worth stating. The curve above is one card's published ladder carried
across volumes, not an average of the market, and it is labeled that way
wherever it appears. And there is no independent benchmark for any of this:
across the buyer phrasings searched, every page returned that published a price
was published by someone selling the service. That is not a reason to distrust
the cards, which are primary evidence of their own prices. It is a reason to
distrust any page claiming to know the market average, including one that
sounded confident.

## Questions buyers ask about per-SKU photography cost

**How much does product photography cost per SKU?**

Published studio cards put standard ecommerce capture at roughly $39 to $50 an
image in September 2026, with uplifts to about $190 for full-body model images.
Per SKU depends on how many images each SKU needs, and no source publishes an
average for that, because it is a decision about your catalog rather than a
market fact.

**Does cost per SKU fall at volume?**

Up to a point, then it flattens hard. Published ladders cap at about 30 percent
off list in both markets read, with the deepest tier reached around 500 images a
year. Carried across volumes, going from 12,000 to 30,000 images moves effective
capture from about $35.63 to about $35.25, roughly 1 percent.

**What does a photography day rate not cover?**

Sample logistics, reshoots and approval rounds beyond the first. Eight published
price surfaces were read across two markets and not one prices any of the three.
Fixed booking fees also hide inside day-rate framing: one card's $149 booking fee
is 62 percent of the unit price at six images from a booking and under 1 percent
at six hundred.

**When is generation cheaper than shooting?**

Once the published discount ladder stops helping, which is around 500 images a
year. Past that, the rate is nearly fixed and the only variable left is how many
images need a camera at all. Decide it per asset: capture makes the master where
material accuracy matters, generation multiplies it into variants.

**How much does ecommerce retouching cost per image?**

Published retouch cards run roughly $0.25 to $1.20 an image by operation, which
is about 0.5 to 3.1 percent of the published capture line. One card adds a fixed
platform fee of about $95 a month, which doubles the effective unit price at 100
images a month and adds about 10 percent at 1,000.
