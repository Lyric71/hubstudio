# Stock photos vs AI images for product content: cost, rights and speed

For product content, the choice isn't really stock or AI. It's whether the
picture has to show your product. A stock photo can't, and neither can an
image generated from a sentence. An AI edit of your own product photo can.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## Stock photos vs AI images: which should you use for product content?

Use licensed stock for real places, real events and documentary scenes. Use
text-to-image for props, moods and backgrounds that hold no product. When the
product itself must appear, edit your own product photo into the new scene,
in an app like hubStudio or any editor that takes a source picture. Stock
licenses cap use. Generated work carries thinner copyright.

| Criterion        | Licensed stock                                  | Generated from words                          | Generated from your product photo                |
|------------------|-------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Cost structure   | A license per image, or a monthly plan          | A price per run                               | A price per run, plus one good photo of the product |
| Rights           | A license; the library keeps the copyright      | Purely generated parts carry no US copyright  | Your photo stays protected where it shows through |
| Exclusivity      | Non-exclusive: anyone can license the same file | Unique in practice, with little to enforce    | Your product, so nobody else has it              |
| Speed            | As fast as the search                           | One run per attempt, several runs at once     | Same, once the source photo exists               |
| Product fidelity | Cannot show your product                        | Cannot show your product                      | Keeps the product, changes the scene             |

## Does the picture have to show your product?

Start here, because it settles most cases before price comes up.

A stock library can hold an enormous archive, and none of it shows your
SKU. A text-to-image engine has never seen your bottle, your label or your
exact shade of green. It renders something plausible. Plausible is fine for a
mood board and wrong for a product page.

The risk isn't only aesthetic. Advertising regulators judge a picture by what
a shopper takes from it.

> A stock photo with readable number plates added, shown as footage from the
> advertised dash cams, was "misleading, because it was not taken from
> footage from any of the advertised dash cams."
> Source: UK advertising regulator ruling, June 5, 2024.

The advertiser in that case had used a stock image and added detail to make
the product look more capable. The same logic reaches an AI image that
improves a product, as the regulator's own guidance says.

> "If using pre- or post-production techniques, images should not be altered
> in a way which exaggerates what the product can do."
> Source: UK advertising regulator guidance on misleading imagery, September
> 11, 2025.

So the first sort is simple. Pictures with no product in them (a kitchen, a
beach at dawn, hands holding a coffee cup with no brand) can come from stock
or from a prompt. Pictures of the product come from a photo of the product,
retouched or extended, and never invented.

<!-- SECTION: What a stock license buys -->

## What does a stock license actually buy?

Less than many buyers assume. A license is permission to use a file under
conditions, and the conditions matter more than the download button suggests.

**Royalty-free or rights-managed.** Royalty-free means you pay once and reuse
the image without further royalties, in any medium. It doesn't mean free.
Rights-managed images are priced and limited by use, size, placement,
duration and territory, so a second campaign means a second license.

**Standard or extended.** A standard license covers most marketing. It stops
at products for resale where the image is the main value (a printed mug, a
poster, a template) and at very large print runs. Both need an extended
license, at an extra fee.

Watch for the editorial-only tag, too. Those images carry no model or
property release, so they can illustrate news and commentary but can't sell
anything.

**Nobody's exclusive.** The library can license the same file to anyone,
including the brand next to yours on the shelf. Exclusivity, where a library
offers it at all, is a separate buy-out negotiated on request.

If a licensed image draws a claim, the library's protection runs out at a
ceiling. Some agreements also cover the file only in the form delivered, not
the version you retouched.

> Standard licenses cap print runs at 500,000 and cap the library's legal
> protection at $5,000 to $10,000; one library raises that to $250,000 per
> image with an extended license.
> Source: four published stock license agreements and three plan pages, read
> October 12, 2026, category level, no library named.

One more clause matters now that so much editing runs through AI. One large library
group's agreements allow AI for permitted editing of a licensed creative
image, but bar any training use, including uploading the file to an AI tool
that acquires rights in it. Read that clause before you put a stock photo
into any generator.

## What does each route cost, and when do you pay?

Stock is cheap per image and priced up front. For a picture with no product
in it, that is hard to beat.

> Entry stock subscriptions start at about $29 to $30 a month, which works
> out to roughly $3 to $10 a standard image.
> Source: lowest monthly plans on three stock libraries' public price pages,
> collected October 12, 2026; per-image figures as printed, or the monthly
> price divided by the images it includes.

Those are the published entry plans as of October 12, 2026. Extended licenses
are sold on top of the standard one, and video and premium collections cost
more. Rights-managed images are quoted per use, so there's
no published band to compare.

Generation is priced differently: per run, not per file you keep. You pay for
each attempt, including the ones you throw away, so cost follows how many
tries a picture takes. Starting from a real product photo changes what can go
wrong: the engine works from your product instead of inventing one, so the
review checks for drift, not for a different object.

| Route                      | What you pay for                         | When you pay                    | What is never charged              |
|----------------------------|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Licensed stock             | A license per image, or a plan           | At download, or monthly         | Re-use within the license terms    |
| Generated from words       | Each run                                 | Per run, before you keep anything | In hubStudio, a failed run       |
| Generated from your photo  | Each run, plus the photo once            | Per run; the photo when it's shot | In hubStudio, a failed run       |
| A new product shoot        | Studio, crew, styling, retouching        | Per shoot day or per SKU        | Depends on the contract            |

If the real comparison is a new shoot, the product photography cost per SKU
piece lays out how studio pricing scales. And if you already hold one clean
photo of each product, that photo is the asset the third route reuses.

## Who owns an AI-generated product image?

This section describes production practice, not legal advice. Ask counsel
before you register or enforce anything.

In the United States the starting point is settled. A machine can't be an
author, and a prompt on its own doesn't make you one.

> Copyright "does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material
> where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements,"
> and "prompts do not alone provide sufficient control."
> Source: US Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2:
> Copyrightability, January 29, 2025, executive summary.

The courts agree, and the Supreme Court has declined to revisit the point.

> The Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first
> instance by a human being." The Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal on
> March 2, 2026.
> Source: US Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, Thaler v. Perlmutter, No.
> 23-5233, March 18, 2025; Supreme Court docket No. 25-449.

That isn't the end of it for product work. The same report draws a line that
matters a great deal when the input is your own photograph.

> When "a human inputs their own copyrightable work and that work is
> perceptible in the output, they will be the author of at least that portion
> of the output." That copyright "would not extend to the AI-generated
> elements standing alone."
> Source: US Copyright Office, Part 2: Copyrightability, January 29, 2025,
> Section II.E, page 24.

Read that against the three routes. A text-to-image picture is mostly
unprotected, so a rival who copies it has little to answer for. An edit of
your own photo keeps protection over the photo where it shows through, but
not over the generated room around it. The photo has to stay clearly visible.
The Office's Review Board upheld a refusal where a photo went in and the
restyled result no longer clearly showed it.

Stock gives you no copyright at all, only a license. The library or the
photographer keeps it.

| Question                   | Licensed stock                       | Generated from words                    | Generated from your product photo           |
|----------------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Who holds the copyright    | The library or the photographer      | Nobody, for the purely generated parts  | You, for your photo where it shows through  |
| Exclusivity                | None unless bought out               | Unique, but little to enforce           | The product is yours alone                  |
| Indemnity                  | Capped by the license                | Whatever the tool's terms say           | Whatever the tool's terms say               |
| AI disclosure              | None for an unaltered photograph     | Possible, if it passes as a real product | Usually none when the product is shown as it is |

The copyright and AI page on this site covers what to document for each
asset, and where trademarks come in.

## Do you have to label AI-generated product images?

Sometimes, and Europe is where the rule is written down. Since August 2,
2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act has required whoever decides how AI is used
in a campaign to disclose deep fakes. And a deep fake isn't only a face: the
Commission reads the "objects" in the legal definition to include consumer
goods.

The Commission's guidelines draw the line where this comparison does.

> An AI image of a product that can mislead "as to the actual product
> appearance, characteristics or use" counts as a deep fake. A real product
> "shown in an advertisement against an AI-generated background" does not, as
> long as the ad does not mislead about the product.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50 of the AI Act,
> C(2026) 5054, July 20, 2026, examples after paragraph 116.

The guidelines also class aesthetic background replacements and compositions
of existing products in product ads as minor edits. So the third route, done
honestly, usually stays out of the label. An invented product shot that looks
better than the real one doesn't.

A label doesn't rescue a misleading picture, either. The UK regulator's
guidance says small print is "generally unlikely to prevent an ad from
misleading consumers." The EU AI Act labeling piece covers who applies the
label, where it sits and what the platforms add.

## Can an AI image keep your product accurate?

Only if it starts from the product. An edit begins with your photo and
changes what you name: the background, the light, the surface, the props.
The product's shape and label come from the source.

Even then, check. Engines re-render pixels, and the small things drift
first: the words on a label, a cap's color, the count of items in a pack.
Three habits help.

- Shoot or pick a sharp, evenly lit source photo with the label readable.
  hubStudio's Image studio resizes source pictures in your browser to 1,536
  pixels on the long side before sending them, so fine print needs to be
  legible at that size.
- Protect the product. On the ChatGPT Image engines in hubStudio, an optional
  mask marks what may change in the first image, and everything else stays as
  it is.
- Compare every result against the real item before it ships: label, color,
  proportions, and anything a shopper would hold you to.

The how-to on turning a product photo into a lifestyle image walks through
prompts and engine choice step by step.

## Which route is fastest?

That depends on what slows you down.

Stock is as fast as your search. When the right photo exists, it's yours in
minutes. When it doesn't, you can lose an afternoon to near-misses.

With generation, the wait turns into rounds. Each attempt is one run, and you
can start the next before the first finishes. The slow part moves to review:
checking that the scene reads right and that nothing in the product has
shifted.

The edit route adds one step at the start: a usable product photo. Once that
exists, it runs at the speed of generation, and every review starts from the
real item rather than an invented one.

## When should you still choose stock?

Whenever the picture has to be a photograph of the real world. A real city
skyline, a real event, a documentary scene, a news moment: generation can
imitate those, and the imitation is the problem. Stock also wins when you
need a generic photo in the next ten minutes and no product appears in it.

| If the picture needs                               | Choose                       | Because                                         |
|----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| A real place, event or documentary moment          | Licensed stock               | It has to be a photograph of the real world     |
| Your product, in a new setting                     | An edit of your product photo | It keeps the product and changes the scene     |
| A mood, texture or backdrop with no product        | Text to image or stock       | Neither has to match anything real              |
| Exclusivity you can enforce                        | Your own photo, edited       | Stock is shared and pure generation is thin on rights |
| Merchandise for resale or a huge print run         | Stock with an extended license, or your own work | Standard licenses stop there |

products still need a camera at all.

## FAQ

### Is it cheaper to use AI images or stock photos?

Per image, entry stock plans work out to roughly $3 to $10 a standard image on
the price pages we read in October 2026. Generation is priced per run, so the
cost depends on how many attempts a picture takes. For product content the
fair comparison is with a reshoot, because neither stock nor a text prompt can
show your actual product.

### Can I copyright an AI-generated image?

Not the purely generated parts, in the United States. The Copyright Office
says prompts alone don't give enough control, and the courts require a human
author. Your own photo used as the input stays protected where it is clearly
visible in the result, along with creative arrangement and edits. This is
production practice, not legal advice.

### Can I use AI-generated images commercially?

Usually, yes, subject to the terms of the tool you used and the rules where
the ad runs. Two limits matter for products. The picture must not mislead
about the product, whatever made it. In Europe, an AI image that could pass as
the real product, and mislead, is a deep fake that needs a visible label.

### Can AI show my actual product?

Only if you give it your product. A text prompt produces a plausible
stand-in, not your SKU. Editing your own product photo keeps the real item and
changes the scene around it. Check every result against the product, starting
with the small print and the colors, which drift first. A mask, where the engine supports one,
keeps the product untouched.

### Are stock photos exclusive to my brand?

No. Standard stock licenses are non-exclusive, so the library can license the
same file to anyone, rival brands included. At least one major library offers
exclusive rights to a royalty-free image only as a separately negotiated
buy-out. The library or the photographer keeps the copyright; you hold a
license with conditions and a capped warranty.

### Do I have to label AI-generated product images?

In the EU, a deep fake needs a visible label from August 2, 2026, and an AI
product image that misleads about the product's look or use counts as one.
A real product shown against an AI background, with nothing misrepresented,
does not, according to the Commission's guidelines. A label never makes a
misleading image acceptable.
