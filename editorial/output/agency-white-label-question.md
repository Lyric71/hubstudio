# White-label creative production for agencies: the NDA, contact rules and margin per asset

An agency that sells production under its own name signs two contracts, one with its
client and one with its supplier. The protections that matter have to be written into
both, because the one published US production standard read for this page assumes the
supplier knows who the advertiser is.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How does white-label creative production for agencies work?

It runs on two contracts: the agency's contract with its client and the agency's
contract with its supplier. The NDA, the contact rules, ownership of the work and any
trained model, the margin and AI disclosure all have to line up across both. hubStudio
works on the supply side of that arrangement.

This page describes production practice, not legal advice. A lawyer drafts both
contracts, and nothing here says what a court would enforce.

| Question | Client contract | Supplier contract | What breaks if they disagree |
|---|---|---|---|
| NDA | The client's material and plans | The same material, plus the client's identity and the arrangement itself | The supplier may share what the agency promised to protect |
| Client contact | Who speaks for the agency | What the supplier may do, may do with consent, and never does | A supplier on a client call, or in the client's inbox |
| Ownership | What the client receives, and when | What the agency receives, and when | The agency promises rights it does not yet hold |
| Margin | Whether markup and rebates are disclosed | Cost-plus or firm bid, and the overage rule | The agency absorbs overruns it never priced |
| AI disclosure | What the client is told about AI use | What the supplier reports to the agency, per asset | AI use reaches the client without the agreement it was promised |

The conversation that comes before this paper is covered in the questions to ask an AI
production partner article. This page starts where the talking stops.

## What should a white-label production NDA cover?

Sign it first. Guidance from WIPO and from the US commercial production trade body
both put the NDA at the very start of a supplier relationship.

> WIPO's trade secrets guide sets out the drafting points for an NDA: clearly
> identify which information is confidential, state the authorized use and the
> specific purposes, set out the exceptions, and regulate what happens to
> confidential documents once they have served their purpose, for example by
> requiring the recipient to "return or destroy the information and provide
> certification of the destruction." With partners, customers and suppliers, it
> says to enter into the NDA "at the very beginning of the business
> relationship, i.e., when starting negotiations."
> Source: World Intellectual Property Organization, WIPO Guide to Trade Secrets
> and Innovation, first published 2024, part IV on trade secret management;
> guide chapter read in full in September 2026.

> The US commercial production trade body's bidding guide puts the NDA on the
> first call. Its opening checklist asks: "Has a Mutual NDA been provided for
> this project? (if not, the production/post company should ask for one to
> protect its IP)". The same checklist asks: "May the production/post company
> use the finished spot on its reel/socials with full credit?"
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's bidding reference guide,
> 2026 edition; first-call checklist read in full.

That second question matters more in white-label work than anywhere else. A reel is
where an invisible arrangement becomes visible.

Start the agreement from its purpose. The UK Intellectual Property Office tells the
disclosing party to restrict use to a specific permitted purpose, stated as precisely
as it can, because a purpose can be widened later but never narrowed. In white-label
production the purpose is tight: named deliverables, for one agency, for one end client.

The office's own example agreements, one-way and mutual, run to nine short clauses.
They make a sound skeleton. The office is plain that an IP attorney or solicitor should
draw up the agreement you actually sign. The checklist below is production practice
built on that skeleton, not a signable document.

| Clause | What it settles | Where it comes from | White-label note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parties and direction | Who discloses to whom | UK IPO mutual template; US production guidelines | Make it mutual |
| Permitted purpose | The only use allowed | UK IPO guidance | Name the deliverables, the agency and the end client |
| What is confidential | The information covered | UK IPO templates; WIPO guide | Include the client's identity, the brief, unreleased work and the arrangement itself (practice) |
| Exclusions | Public and already-known information | UK IPO templates; WIPO guide | Keep them standard |
| Onward disclosure | Who else may see it | UK IPO templates | Freelancers and subcontractors only on equivalent written terms |
| Security | How the material is held | UK IPO templates | Separate storage per end client (practice) |
| Disclosure required by law | What happens when a law compels it | UK IPO templates | Notice to the agency first (practice) |
| Return or destruction | What happens to copies at the end | UK IPO templates; WIPO guide | Ask for certification of destruction |
| No license | The NDA grants no IP rights | UK IPO templates | Ownership sits in the production contract, not here |
| Reel, portfolio and credit | Whether the work can be shown | US production bidding guide, 2026 | Written as never, or as needing written consent |
| Training on client material | What the material may train | Production practice | Nothing shared or public |
| Duration | How long the duty lasts | UK IPO guidance and templates | A set term or indefinite, and say which |
| Governing law | Which law applies | UK IPO templates | Match the production contract (practice) |
| Timing | When it is signed | WIPO guide; US production bidding guide | Before the first brief |

Duration is a field the parties set, not an industry standard.

> On how long confidentiality should last, the same guidance says: "It's common
> to see it limited to 3 or 5 years. After that time they will be able to use
> and disclose your information."
> Source: UK Intellectual Property Office, non-disclosure agreements guidance,
> first published March 2015; a statement of common practice by the office, not
> a survey.

Confidentiality also runs both ways. The US production trade body's guidelines say it
should be mutual, and that it should never be so restrictive that the parties cannot do
their jobs. A supplier's methods deserve the same cover as an agency's client list.

## Can a white-label studio contact my client?

Only as far as the supplier contract allows. No trade body guideline, model contract or
government source read for this page publishes a rule that a supplier may not contact
the end client. The rule people repeat is a commercial term. The agency has to write it.

The one production standard read in full points the other way. Its standard agreement
names the advertiser, the agency and the production company on its first page, and
makes agency and advertiser jointly and severally liable. Its guidelines go further
when a bill is at risk.

> The trade body's guidelines point away from invisibility when payment is at
> risk. Where an agency keeps a sequential liability clause and the production
> company has neither a direct-payment provision nor full funding in advance,
> the guidelines say the production company should "Be provided with the
> advertiser billing and contact information," "Copy the advertiser on all
> invoices," and notify the advertiser of payment due as soon as the agency
> misses a payment date.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's national guidelines for live
> action production, revised April 2020; guideline document read in full.

That is the disclosed model. The advertiser is named on the supplier's paper, shares
liability for the bill, and becomes the supplier's fallback when an agency pays late.

White-label takes the advertiser out of the supplier's view. The fallback goes with it.

So whatever the disclosed model supplies by default now has to be written into the
supplier contract: payment terms, contact rules, and the two defaults in the same
standard form that bar assignment without written consent and give the client, the
production company and the director a revocable license to use finished work for
promotion until the client says otherwise in writing.

A studio's published policy can set the contact rule from its side. One example:

> hubStudio's agencies page describes its white-label arrangement this way:
> "Your clients never know we exist. You handle all client communication,
> strategy and relationship management. We execute the production work and
> deliver finished assets directly to you, under the agency brand. Clients see
> only your agency and your processes." That is the studio's published policy,
> not an industry standard.
> Source: hubStudio agencies solutions page, read September 2026; first-party
> statement of policy.

A published policy is not a clause. The table below sets out contact rules as terms the
agency writes into its supplier contract. It is production practice, not an industry
standard.

| Situation | Rule to write | Why it belongs in the supplier contract |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier works through the agency's named producer | Allowed | One line of instruction, one approver |
| Finished assets go to the agency, under the agency brand | Allowed | Delivery never touches the client |
| Supplier joins a client call, and in what role | Needs written consent | A call is where names change hands |
| Supplier receives client contact or billing details | Needs written consent | The disclosed model hands these over by default |
| Client reviewers log in to the supplier's review platform | Needs written consent | A login screen can name the supplier |
| Supplier shows the work on a reel, in credentials or a case study | Needs written consent | Standard forms can grant promotional use by default |
| Supplier subcontracts or assigns the work | Needs written consent | The US standard form bars assignment without consent |
| AI-generated content goes into materials for the client | Needs written consent | Extends the UK principle on agreement before AI content changes hands |
| Supplier approaches or pitches the client during the term and an agreed tail | Never | The non-solicitation term, see the caveat below |
| Supplier names the client, or the agency's client list | Never | Confidential under the NDA |
| Supplier discusses the agency's pricing or margin with the client | Never | Disclosure of margin is the agency's call |

A non-solicitation line is only as strong as the law that governs the contract. One
statute shows how far a restraint rule can reach.

> California's Business and Professions Code section 16600 states that "every
> contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession,
> trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void." The section, as last
> amended effective 1 January 2024, adds that it "shall not be limited to
> contracts where the person being restrained from engaging in a lawful
> profession, trade, or business is a party to the contract."
> Source: California Business and Professions Code section 16600, amended by
> Stats. 2023, Ch. 828 (AB 1076), effective January 2024; statute text read on
> the legislature's code display in September 2026.

How that section, or any other law, treats a clause between two businesses is a
question for counsel in the governing jurisdiction. This page does not answer it.

One more line to draw. A referral is not a white-label buy, and the contact rules
invert.

> hubStudio's partner program is a different arrangement. A partner makes an
> introduction, and hubStudio then handles "Initial discovery calls and needs
> assessment," "Custom proposals tailored to client needs," "Full project
> execution and delivery" and "Client relationship management."
> Source: hubStudio partner program page, read September 2026; first-party
> program description, commission terms excluded.

Under a referral, the studio holds the client. Under white-label, the agency does. The
partner program page and the agencies solutions page describe the two separately, and a
contract should never blend them. The hub4you platform page covers the briefing and
review side, which is where the login row above earns its place.

## Who owns work made under a white-label agreement?

Not simply whoever paid. Ownership passes along a chain, supplier to agency to client,
and each link is a separate contract with its own payment condition.

US law sets a narrow default. A commissioned work counts as a work made for hire only if
it falls in one of nine categories, among them part of a motion picture or other
audiovisual work, a compilation and a contribution to a collective work.

> The Office lists four criteria a commissioned work must meet: it falls within
> one of the nine categories, there is a written agreement, the agreement
> expressly says the work is made for hire, and all parties sign it. "If a work
> fails to satisfy any of these requirements, it is not a work made for hire."
> Ordinarily, the Office notes, the author is the person who actually created
> the work.
> Source: US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised August
> 2024; the Office's circular read in full.

China starts from the opposite end, which matters when the supplier works there.

> Under China's Copyright Law, ownership of a commissioned work is set by the
> contract, and where the contract is not explicit or there is no contract,
> copyright belongs to the commissioned party. A transfer of economic rights
> requires a written contract.
> Source: the Copyright Law of the People's Republic of China as amended
> 11 November 2020, in force 1 June 2021, Articles 19 and 27; statute text
> logged in the source ledger on 10 September 2026.

Two jurisdictions, opposite defaults, one practical answer: write it down at both links.
Neither instrument says what a court would make of a particular contract, and this page
does not either.

Generated material adds a layer. The US Copyright Office's 2023 guidance holds that
copyright protects only human creativity, so where a system produced the expressive
elements, the contract is what allocates them.

Then comes payment.

> The US standard production agreement vests "all rights, title and interest in
> and to the commercial(s) which are the subject of this Agreement, including
> all copyrights therein as well as in and to all the exposed negatives,
> positives, out-takes and clips" in the contracting client, and separately
> provides that "Ownership of the commercial does not transfer until full
> payment is made to Producer."
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's Standard Commercial
> Production Agreement, form revised December 2007, sections 5 and 12; standard
> form read in full.

Stack that condition across two contracts. The agency cannot hand its client rights it
has not yet paid its supplier for, so the transfer date in the client contract should
follow the one in the supplier contract, never run ahead of it.

Trained models are where the published paper runs out.

> That standard agreement has no field for a trained model, model weights, a
> training set or prompts. No published model agreement assigning ownership of
> a trained model between a brand, an agency and a supplier was found in this
> research.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's standard production
> agreement, revised December 2007, read in full for such fields on
> 10 September 2026, plus a review of four buyer searches the same day; none
> present.

> On training, the agreement is short: the producer may not use, or authorize
> any other party to use, the results and proceeds of services rendered under
> the contracts to train generative AI systems without the union's consent.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.1.A.4,
> agreement text, May 2025.

That clause binds only signatories to the US performers' union commercials agreement.
It is also the one published contract term on training found anywhere in this research,
which says how thin the record is.

Studio policy fills some of the gap, and shows where it stops.

> hubStudio publishes that "The custom models we train on your brand are your
> property, not ours," and that every asset is documented with "which model was
> used, which references trained it, which prompts produced it." In a
> white-label engagement the studio's client is the agency, so whether the end
> brand holds that model depends on the agency's own contract with the brand.
> Source: hubStudio AI excellence page, read September 2026; first-party
> statement of policy.

A model promise made to the agency reaches the brand only if the client contract
carries it forward. The table below maps each item across both contracts. Its last
column restates the instruments above and predicts no outcome.

| Item | Supplier contract should say | Client contract should say | If either is silent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finished deliverables | Assigned to the agency on full payment | Assigned to the client on the client's full payment | US: not a work made for hire unless all four criteria are met. China: the commissioned party holds copyright |
| Raw capture, out-takes and working files | Whether they are delivered, kept or destroyed, and when | Whether the client is entitled to them | The same defaults; the US standard form names out-takes and clips expressly |
| Prompts and generation records | Delivered with the asset as its provenance record | Whether the client receives them | No default found in the instruments read |
| Custom model trained on the brand, with its weights | Who owns it, whether it is handed over, whether it is deleted at exit | Whether the agency must pass it to the client | No published model agreement assigning it was found |
| Brand references used for training | Used for this client only, never for a shared model | The client licenses them for the job, including to the supplier | No default found; write it down |
| Supplier's own tools and methods | Stay with the supplier | The client takes no claim on them | No default found; write it down |

The AI excellence page and the terms of service set out the studio's own positions on
model ownership and on transfer after payment.

## How do agencies set margin on outsourced production?

Each company sets its own. Nobody publishes a figure with a method behind it.

> No method-stated figure for agency markup on outsourced production was found.
> Across four buyer searches and 31 results, every markup percentage in
> circulation was published by a seller of white-label services or agency
> software, with no sample and no method. The US commercial production trade
> body's own guidelines decline to set a percentage, and the US advertiser trade
> body's compensation reports could not be read at source.
> Source: search-results audit and document review run 10 September 2026, four
> buyer phrasings, publisher type recorded for every result, no domain named.

What the record does offer is structure. On the US bid form, the production company's
margin is a named line: a single markup percentage applied to the cost sections A to K,
producing a production fee that covers administrative and sales costs plus a fee for
services from prep through wrap. The trade body stops short of the number.

> The same guidelines decline to set the number: "The AICP stresses that the use
> of the AICP bid form (in whole or in part), fee or percentage amounts,
> business costs and components, and the terms of bid submissions or contract
> terms, are strictly a matter of independent determination by each production
> company."
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's national guidelines for live
> action production, revised April 2020; guideline document read in full.

The same guidelines add an overage rule. When an agency or advertiser changes the
specification and costs run over, the fee on those costs uses the markup percentage of
the awarded bid. Agree that rule with the supplier, then mirror it with the client.

Next, pick the buying method. It decides who carries an overrun.

> The standard agreement defines two buying methods. In a cost-plus job, "A
> fixed fee is added to the total of the costs of all the elements, which total
> becomes the contract price." In a firm bid, the producer submits "a bid
> identifying direct costs, production fee, and director's fee," and once the
> bid is accepted "it becomes the contract price for the job."
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's Standard Commercial
> Production Agreement, form revised December 2007, section 8; standard form
> read in full.

A markup and a commission can also describe the same money. The conversion below is
arithmetic, not a going rate.

> A markup and a commission can describe the same money from opposite ends. A
> joint advertiser and agency trade body compensation guide gives the
> conversion in its glossary, "17.65% markup is equivalent to a 15% commission,"
> because 15 is 17.65 percent of 85.
> Source: the joint US advertiser and agency trade body compensation guide,
> 2006, glossary entry for mark-up, read from a third-party-hosted copy in
> September 2026; shown as arithmetic only, never as a current or typical rate.

The build below is a structure for one asset or one job. Every line is the agency's to
price, and none carries a number, because none can be sourced.

| Line | What it covers | Why it exists in white-label |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier cost | The supplier's price per asset or per job, as quoted | The base every other line sits on |
| Agency handling | Brief translation, review against the client's standard, account time | The supplier cannot speak to the client, so the agency does all of it |
| Revision exposure | Rounds the client contract allows beyond those the supplier contract includes | A mismatch in round counts lands on the agency |
| Chain risk | Payment float, indemnity, insurance, rework after a client rejection | The supplier cannot see the client, so the agency carries it |
| Margin | What remains after the lines above | The agency's to set, and to disclose or not under the client contract |

The last decision sits in the client contract: whether the markup, and any rebate, must
be disclosed. Advertisers have not always asked.

> Fewer than half of the advertisers surveyed required their agency to disclose
> whether a production job was being bid to an in-house or affiliated production
> company.
> Source: US advertiser trade body production transparency study,
> August 2017, built on a task force of 30 member executives and 12
> subject-matter organizations plus a supporting member survey whose respondent
> count was not published.

> More than 60 percent of the advertisers surveyed either did not require, or
> did not know whether their agency contract required, production rebates and
> other incentives to be passed back to them.
> Source: US advertiser trade body
> production transparency study, August 2017, same survey, respondent count not
> published.

Both figures date from 2017. They show that disclosure is a term somebody writes, not a
default anyone can assume.

## Do my clients need to know AI was used?

Between agency and client, the trade bodies for both sides say yes. Toward consumers,
only where the AI use is material.

> The UK agency and advertiser trade bodies' principles for generative AI say:
> "Advertisers and agencies should be transparent with each other about their
> use of AI. Neither should include AI-generated content in materials provided
> to the other without the other's agreement."
> Source: the UK agency trade body and the UK advertiser trade body, advertising
> industry principles for the use of generative AI in creative advertising,
> October 2023, launched 1 November 2023; all twelve principles read in full.

Those principles address advertisers and agencies. They say nothing about a supplier
behind the agency. Applying the same rule one link down is the reader's extension, not
the principles' text. It is a reasonable one: an agency cannot be open about AI use it
was never told about.

> A US digital advertising trade body's disclosure framework takes a risk-based
> approach: "Disclosure is required only when AI materially affects
> authenticity, identity, or representation in ways that could mislead
> consumers." Under it, routine production tasks, background AI tools and
> clearly stylized creative can proceed without disclosure.
> Source: a US digital advertising trade body's AI transparency and disclosure
> framework, January 2026; the framework's public landing page read in full,
> with the full framework behind a member login.

Version 2 of that framework, announced in August 2026, says state and international
regulation takes precedence over it. In the UK, the advertising regulator's guidance
keeps the advertiser responsible for any AI output in its ads.

China is specific about the file itself. China's Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated
Synthetic Content, issued March 2025, set out two labels.

> The Measures require two labels on a generated image or video: an explicit
> label a person can perceive, and an implicit label written into the file
> metadata carrying the content attribute, the service provider's name or code
> and a content identifier. Where a provider offers download, copy or export, it
> must ensure the exported file still contains a conforming explicit label.
> Source: the instrument, Articles 4 and 5, in force 1 September 2025.

Who carries that duty between brand, agency and studio is generally treated as a
question for the contracts and for counsel. This page does not settle it. The China AI
labeling in the production workflow article follows labels through a pipeline, and the
disclosure audit trail per asset article covers the record behind them.

Lines to put in both contracts, as production practice:

- The supplier reports, per asset, where AI was used in making it.
- The supplier delivers the provenance record with the asset: model, references, prompts.
- The agency tells the client before AI-generated content enters the client's materials.
- Who applies each market's label is agreed in writing before delivery.
- Client material never trains a shared or public model.
- A named human reviewer signs off each asset before delivery.

## Questions agencies ask about white-label production

**What should a white-label production NDA cover?**

The permitted purpose, what counts as confidential (including the client's identity and
the arrangement itself), exclusions, onward disclosure only on equivalent terms, return
or destruction of copies, no license to intellectual property, reel and portfolio use, a
term and a governing law. Sign it before the first brief. A lawyer drafts it; this is
production practice, not legal advice.

**Can a white-label studio contact my client?**

Only if your supplier contract allows it. No trade body guideline or model contract
read for this page sets a rule against it, and US production trade body guidelines tell
suppliers to contact the advertiser when an agency's payment is at risk. Write the rules
in: what is allowed, what needs written consent, and what never happens.

**Who owns work made under a white-label agreement?**

Whoever the two contracts say, once the payments clear. In the US a commissioned work is
a work made for hire only in nine categories and only with a signed written agreement.
Under China's Copyright Law, a silent contract leaves copyright with the commissioned
party. No published agreement assigning a trained model was found, so write that down too.

**How do agencies set margin on outsourced production?**

Each company sets its own. No method-stated markup figure for outsourced production was
found, and the US production trade body leaves percentages to each company's independent
determination. Build the price from supplier cost, agency handling, revision exposure
and chain risk, then choose cost-plus or a firm bid and decide what the client contract
must disclose.

**Do my clients need to know AI was used?**

UK agency and advertiser trade bodies ask both sides to be transparent with each other
about AI use, and to agree before AI content goes into the other's materials. A US
digital advertising framework asks for consumer disclosure only where AI materially
affects authenticity, identity or representation. Who carries a label under China's
rules is generally treated as a question for the contracts.
