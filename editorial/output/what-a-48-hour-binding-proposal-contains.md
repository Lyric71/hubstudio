# What a binding creative production proposal should include

A proposal binds, if it binds at all, to the scope it names. So the scope has to
be on the page in units, with the exclusions, the revision rounds, the rights and
the rules for change written beside the price.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What should a creative production proposal include?

Deliverables written as countable units, what the price includes and excludes,
revision rounds, rights and usage, timeline and dependencies, acceptance, change
control, payment terms, validity, and whether the price is fixed or an estimate.
hubStudio publishes a binding proposal within 48 hours of a brief as studio
policy. Hold it, and every other proposal, to the same list.

This page describes production and procurement practice, not legal advice. Its
definitions come from US federal procurement rules and from the US commercial
production trade body's own bidding documents. Other jurisdictions were not
checked for it.

Start with the document type, because it decides how far the final invoice can
move.

| Document | What it commits the supplier to | How the price can move | When a buyer should want it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binding proposal or firm bid | The price for the scope it names | Only through an approved change to that scope | The deliverables can be specified now |
| Estimate or cost-plus | A forecast, with actual costs billed | With actual costs, up to any agreed ceiling | Scope depends on something still unknown |
| Time and materials | Hourly rates plus actual materials | With hours worked, up to a ceiling | The work cannot be estimated with confidence |
| Retainer | Whatever the retainer contract defines as the period's scope | Fixed per period; scope inside it is what moves | Continuous work across markets and channels |

No primary definition of a retainer was read for this page. The row describes
the model, and the contract has to define the rest.

## Is it a quote, an estimate or a binding proposal?

The words get used loosely. The one body of rules that defines them precisely is
US federal purchasing, and its definitions are useful even to a buyer who will
never sign a government contract.

> In US federal purchasing, a quotation is not an offer and cannot be accepted
> to form a binding contract. The contract forms when the supplier accepts the
> buyer's order.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 13.004, effective 13
> March 2026, regulation text read at the official host; it governs federal
> procurement, not private contracts.

An offer, in the same regulation, is the thing that binds: a response that, if
accepted, commits the supplier to perform. The pricing basis then decides what
that commitment is worth.

> A firm-fixed-price contract carries a price that is not adjusted for what the
> work actually cost the contractor, which puts the cost risk on the contractor.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.202-1, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> Cost-reimbursement contracts pay allowable costs and set an estimate of total
> cost, which works as a ceiling the contractor exceeds at its own risk unless
> the buyer approves.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.301-1, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> A time-and-materials contract pays fixed hourly rates plus actual materials,
> provides no positive profit incentive for efficiency, and may be used only
> when the extent or duration of the work cannot be estimated with any
> reasonable confidence.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.601, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

Commercial production asks the same question in its own vocabulary, and asks it
first.

> The 2026 bidding guide asks, before anything is priced, whether the project is
> firm bid, cost-plus, or a blend.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's bidding reference guide,
> 2026 edition, downloaded and read in full September 2026.

If a proposal does not answer that question on its face, ask it in writing
before comparing totals. Two totals on two different bases are not comparable.

## What does "binding" actually commit a supplier to?

Less than most buyers assume, and it depends on who is using the word. There is
no single legal meaning to rely on, and the page does not offer one.

The commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines are the
clearest published text on a firm bid, and they do not describe a cap.

> Under those guidelines an accepted firm bid becomes the minimum contract
> price. The final price adds hours actually used, never less than the time
> booked, plus any approved overages.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026.

> On a cost-plus job under the same guidelines, the bid is submitted the same
> way, but the final price carries the actual outside costs incurred or
> committed instead of the estimate.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026.

Set that beside the federal firm-fixed-price contract, which is not adjusted for
the supplier's cost experience at all. "Firm bid" and "firm fixed price" are two
different instruments. A buyer reading "firm" as "capped" is reading the wrong
document.

The one US federal rule read that defines a binding estimate comes from another
industry entirely. It is useful here only as an illustration of how a regulator
drew the line.

> The one US federal regulation read that defines a binding estimate, written
> for interstate household movers, requires it to say on its face that it binds
> both parties and covers only the services it names. Extra services reopen it:
> the mover reaffirms it, writes a new one the customer signs, or both agree in
> writing to treat it as non-binding.
> Source: 49 CFR 375.403, current eCFR text read 10 September 2026; a different
> industry, cited only to show what "binding" means where a regulator has
> defined it.

Read together, the primary texts agree on something narrower than "the price
cannot change." A price document binds, if at all, only to the scope it
identifies. A change of scope reopens the price through a written change.

That is why the scope sections matter more than the word on the cover.
hubStudio's own pages say its proposal is binding, is revised before signing if
the scope changes, and holds for 30 days. They define nothing further, so the
rest has to be asked for in writing, from any supplier.

## What goes in each section of a proposal?

Each section has a job. The last column shows where the practice is
written down, or says plainly where it is not.

| Section | What it must state | What goes wrong without it | Where it is written down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pricing basis | Firm bid, cost-plus or a blend | "Firm" gets read as "capped" | Trade body bidding guide; federal contract types |
| Deliverables | Count, length, version, aspect ratio and language of each unit | Two bids price two different jobs | Federal work statements; trade body guide |
| Inclusions | What the price covers, and whether the budget is all in | Stills, social or behind the scenes arrive as extra decks | Trade body bid form and bidding guide |
| Exclusions | Everything outside the price, by name | Usage, licensing and tariffs surface after award | Trade body bid form and sample language |
| Revision rounds | The count, what counts as a round, where a change starts | A change gets billed, or argued, as a revision | No published norm; trade body overage rule |
| Rights and usage | Who owns what, from when, for which media | The buyer assumes ownership it never received | US Copyright Office; China's Copyright Law |
| Timeline and dependencies | Dates, and what the buyer supplies by when | Delays nobody can assign | Production practice; no instrument read defines it |
| Acceptance | How the award is confirmed, and what starts the work | Work begins on an email nobody can find | Trade body bidding best practices |
| Change control | A written change, approved by a named person, before the work | Disputed extras on the final invoice | Trade body overage rule |
| Payment | Deposit, milestones and invoice terms, agreed while bidding | Work stalls on the first invoice | Trade body best practices and guidelines |
| Validity | How long the price holds | An old number applied to a new brief | Production practice |
| Cancellation | Charges by notice period | A late cancellation with no agreed cost | Trade body post-production guidelines |

The sections below take the ones that carry the most money.

## How should deliverables be written?

As results, in units someone can count and accept. The federal rule on service
contracts says it well.

> Federal service contracts are told to describe the work in terms of the
> required results, not how it will be done or how many hours it will take, and
> to measure performance against stated standards.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 37.602, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

The trade body's bidding guide pushes the same point into the detail that
decides a production budget.

> The guide also asks whether the deliverables match the creative that has to be
> filmed, giving the example of a 15-second deliverable written as a 60 or a
> 90, and whether every deliverable shares one aspect ratio or some run
> vertical for social.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's bidding reference guide,
> 2026 edition, read in full September 2026.

> The trade body's post-production guidelines call the bid an estimate based on
> the client's specifications, and say the final deliverables, including
> versions and aspect ratios, belong in those specifications.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026; informational guidance, not
> association policy.

> The same trade body's 2026 bidding guide tells bidders to establish whether a
> stated budget is all in, and whether stills, social elements and behind the
> scenes carry separate decks and separate budgets.
> Source: the trade body's published bidding reference guide, 2026 edition.

Versions and aspect ratios multiply fast once a campaign meets a marketplace.
The Tmall platform page describes the format range one storefront's buying
systems ask for, and the video production service page lists platform
reformatting and multi-language adaptation as post-production work. Each of
those is a unit, and each belongs in the specification. The what a finished
brand asset costs article sets out how to define one asset before pricing it.

## Who owns the work once it is paid for?

Whoever the written agreement says, in both jurisdictions read for this page.
Neither default hands the buyer ownership just for paying.

> In the United States, a commissioned work counts as a work made for hire only
> if it falls in one of nine listed categories, part of an audiovisual work
> among them, and both parties sign a written agreement saying so. Miss any
> condition and it is not a work made for hire.
> Source: US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised August
> 2024, read in full September 2026.

> In China, a commissioned work belongs to whoever the contract names, and to
> the commissioned party where the contract is silent or absent; transferring
> economic rights needs a written contract.
> Source: China's Copyright Law as amended 11 November 2020, Articles 19 and 27,
> statute text read September 2026.

Usage is a separate question from ownership, and talent makes it expensive.

> Under the US performers' union commercials agreement effective 1 April 2025,
> alternate scenes shot for one commercial in the same session are not treated
> as new commercials, while an edit or lift outside the defined parameters
> creates a new commercial and new fees.
> Source: the union commercials agreement effective April 2025, as read by two
> independent law firms. The union's own rate documents are not machine
> readable.

A proposal should state its usage assumption in one sentence. hubStudio's
published policy is one example of what that sentence looks like.

> Every hubStudio quote assumes unlimited commercial use across the client's
> owned and paid channels; broadcast TV, out-of-home at scale and talent-likeness
> licensing for AI brand ambassadors are quoted separately.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

> Ownership of final deliverables passes to the client once hubStudio has
> received full payment, unless the engagement agreement states otherwise; the
> proposal or statement of work prevails over the site terms where they
> conflict.
> Source: hubStudio's terms page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

The copyright and AI resource covers what changes when generated material is
part of the work, and the AI brand ambassadors article covers the likeness
consent a talent line needs. Both describe production practice, not legal
advice.

## What happens when the scope changes?

The price reopens, in writing, before the extra work starts. The trade body's
guidelines set out the mechanism.

> An overage arises when the specifications change, in scope or in schedule, and
> is billed as a change order. The client names a representative with authority
> to approve it, and it should be agreed before the work it covers begins.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026.

> If the client does not approve the cost of a specification change, the
> supplier is not obligated to provide the extra services or deliverables.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026.

That second rule cuts both ways. A buyer cannot get unpriced extras by asking
loudly, and a supplier cannot bill extras nobody approved. The proposal should
name the approver on the buyer's side before production starts.

Scope can also move before anything is signed. A binding proposal should say
what happens then.

> If the scope changes later, hubStudio revises the proposal before anything is
> signed.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

## How should payment, validity and cancellation be written?

Early, and before any work begins. The trade body's best practices put payment
in the bidding phase, not after award.

> The trade body's bidding best practices say suppliers should not be expected
> to begin work until a contract or purchase order commits the buyer to payment,
> and that payment requirements should be exchanged during the bidding phase.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's suggested best practices for
> bidding, 2026, read in full September 2026; not association policy or legal
> advice.

The same document asks for the award to be confirmed in writing. That
confirmation is the acceptance step most proposals leave out.

On the split itself, there is no norm to copy. The one printed example comes
with its own warning.

> The post-production guidelines print a payment plan of 75 percent on award and
> 25 percent on delivery, and state in the same document that the split is an
> example, not a recommendation.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026.

Published studio terms show the patterns a buyer will meet: deposits on unit
orders, milestones on projects, monthly billing on retainers.

> hubStudio takes a deposit upfront on asset orders with the balance on
> delivery, bills projects against milestones, invoices retainers monthly in
> advance, and offers Net 30 to established clients and listed companies on
> request.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

> hubStudio quotes are valid for 30 days and exclude tax where applicable.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

Cancellation is the clause buyers skip until they need it. The only published
schedule found covers post-production.

> The same guidelines scale cancellation charges by notice: 100 percent of the
> awarded bid inside five business days of the start date, 50 percent at six to
> ten days, 25 percent beyond ten, with committed outside costs and approved
> overages paid in every case.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read in full September 2026; guidance for post-production, not
> a rule and not association policy.

Treat it as a shape to negotiate, not a standard to accept.

## What is usually excluded from a production quote?

More than the total suggests. Start with the line that is missing from the
industry's own form.

> The US commercial production trade body's standard bid form itemizes a
> production across sections A through P, from prep crew to breakout talent, and
> carries no line at all for media usage payments.
> Source: the trade body's own free bid form, updated January 2023, downloaded
> and read in full September 2026.

The trade body's own documents then move three more things outside the price.

> The trade body's model language treats placeholder music, sound effects and
> other offline elements as illustrative only, to be licensed by the agency or
> advertiser before any use in the finished work.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's language for uncleared
> materials, November 2025, read in full September 2026.

> The trade body's post-production guidelines treat working files, project
> files, 3D data and the supplier's tools and methods as the supplier's own
> intellectual property, not included in the price of the project.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines,
> January 2026, read September 2026; the stated position of a suppliers' trade
> body, not a neutral standard.

> The trade body's sample bid language puts tariffs outside the production
> company's price, and asks the agency to approve an overage for tariff
> increases incurred after award.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's sample language for bids,
> dated May 2025, read in full September 2026.

Use this as an exclusions checklist. Every line should appear in the proposal
as either included or excluded, by name.

- Media usage and talent payments, which the standard bid form has no line for.
- Stills, social and behind the scenes, where they are bid as separate decks.
- Versions, lengths and aspect ratios missing from the specification.
- Stock footage and licensed materials, unless the proposal says they are in.
- Placeholder music, sound effects and other uncleared materials.
- Working files, project files, 3D data and the supplier's tools.
- Tariffs, duties and tax.
- Sample logistics, reshoots and approval rounds.
- New edits that create new talent fees.
- Broadcast TV, out-of-home at scale and talent likeness, where the quote
  assumes owned and paid channels only.

If a working file or a trained model matters to you, say so in the brief. A
suppliers' trade body has published its position that working files are not
part of the price, so silence will not get them for you.

## How many revision rounds should a proposal include?

There is no published number to benchmark against.

> No published benchmark with a stated method was found for how many revision
> rounds creative production includes as standard.
> Source: searches across trade body, analyst and vendor sources logged
> September 2026; proposal guides ranking for the query advise stating a round
> count without citing one.

No published definition of a round turned up either. So the proposal has to
supply three things: the count, what counts as one round, and the point where a
revision stops being a revision and becomes a change order under the overage
rule above.

hubStudio publishes its own count. It is policy, not a norm.

> hubStudio includes three rounds of modification on finalized work, more on
> retainer, and redoes work that misses the brief at no extra charge.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy.

Whatever the count, compare it with what triggers the count. Rounds counted on
finalized work and rounds counted from the first sketch are different offers.

## How fast should a production proposal come back?

No benchmark exists for this either, and the trade body puts the date in the
buyer's hands.

> No published benchmark was found for how fast a production proposal should
> come back. The commercial production trade body's bidding guide treats the
> bid due date as something the buyer states, and lists a bid forced into a
> compressed window, such as a weekend, as grounds for a pitch fee.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's bidding reference guide and
> suggested best practices, 2026 editions, read September 2026, plus a review of
> 31 search results on four buyer phrasings.

Speed matters when the number has to go into a budget cycle. It matters less
than whether the fast answer is binding.

> hubStudio sends a binding proposal within 48 hours of receiving a brief, and
> the quote reflects scope, complexity, volume and the deadline.
> Source: hubStudio's pricing page, read 10 September 2026, published studio
> policy quoted from the page, not a benchmark.

The pricing page sets out how that proposal is scoped. For a formal review with
several bidders, procurement's guide to buying AI content production covers the
request for proposal and the scoring. This page stays with the proposal that
comes back.

## What price bands should a proposal land inside?

Published category bands, attributed to the category and the date. They tell
you whether a proposal is in range. They cannot price your brief, and no
hubStudio figure appears among them.

> Published studio rate cards put a standard product image at $39 to $50, a
> model or lifestyle image at about $95, a short video clip at about $93 and a
> single UGC video at about $89, with editing add-ons around $49 each and a
> finished pro edit at about $199.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly, prices quoted as each card states them.

> Published rate cards in this category put the floor for ecommerce image
> editing between roughly $0.25 and $1.20 an image, with one card adding a fixed
> platform fee of about $95 a month on top.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly and compared, no vendor named.

> Published unlimited-request design subscription tiers run from roughly $700 to
> roughly $4,500 a month, month to month, with no capture, no talent and no
> usage included.
> Source: published subscription rate cards in this category, September 2026,
> two independent pricing pages read directly.

> Published managed design subscriptions state monthly floors between roughly
> $6,000 and $15,000, and the one card offering a named dedicated team starts at
> roughly $30,000 a month on a twelve-month term, plus a $1,000 monthly software
> fee.
> Source: published subscription rate cards in this category, September 2026,
> published pricing pages only, no vendor named. The $30,000 dedicated floor
> appears on a single card.

> Eight published price surfaces read across two markets price none of sample
> logistics, reshoots or approval rounds.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, two markets, read directly
> September 2026, each card read in full for those lines.

The bands price different units, so they only compare once a proposal is
broken down the same way.

| Band | Unit it prices | What sits outside it |
|---|---|---|
| Studio rate cards | One image, clip or edit | Usage, sample logistics, reshoots, approval rounds |
| Image editing cards | One edited image | Capture, and any platform fee |
| Unlimited-request subscription | A month of access | Capture, talent and usage |
| Managed subscription | A month above a stated floor | Anything the card leaves out of the month's scope |
| Binding proposal | The deliverables it names | Whatever its exclusions list says |

The production cost estimator returns a range built from published rate cards
for a scoped brief. It is not a quote, and it contains no hubStudio rate. A
range tells you whether a proposal is sane. The proposal tells you what the
work costs.

## Questions buyers ask about production proposals

**What should a creative production proposal include?**

Deliverables as countable units, what the price includes and excludes, revision
rounds and what counts as one, rights and usage, timeline and client
dependencies, acceptance, change control, payment terms, validity and
cancellation. It should also say whether the price is a firm bid, a cost-plus
estimate or a blend, because that decides how far the final invoice can move.

**What is the difference between a quote and an estimate?**

In US federal purchasing, a quotation is not an offer and cannot be accepted to
form a contract. An estimate forecasts a cost that moves with actual costs, up
to any agreed ceiling. A firm fixed price does not move with the supplier's
costs. Usage varies by instrument and by country, so read what the document
commits to, not what it is called.

**What does a binding proposal commit the studio to?**

The price for the scope it names, and a written change when that scope moves.
Under the US commercial production trade body's post-production guidelines, an
accepted firm bid is a minimum contract price, with approved overages on top.
hubStudio publishes that its proposal is binding, is revised before signing if
scope changes, and holds for 30 days. This is production practice, not legal
advice.

**How many revision rounds should a proposal include?**

No published benchmark with a stated method sets a number, and no published
definition of a round was found. Write the count into the proposal, define what
counts as one round, and say where a revision ends and a change order begins.
hubStudio publishes three rounds of modification on finalized work, more on
retainer, as studio policy.

**What is usually excluded from a production quote?**

Media usage and talent payments, which the standard US bid form has no line
for. Also placeholder music and uncleared materials, working and project files,
tariffs, duties and tax, and any version or aspect ratio missing from the
specification. Eight published price surfaces read in September 2026 priced
none of sample logistics, reshoots or approval rounds.

**How fast should a production proposal come back?**

No published benchmark exists. The US commercial production trade body's
bidding guide treats the due date as the buyer's to set, and lists a bid forced
into a compressed window, such as a weekend, as grounds for a pitch fee.
hubStudio publishes a binding proposal within 48 hours of a brief as studio
policy, not as a norm.
