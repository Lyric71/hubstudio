# The all-in cost of AI video, and what the dollar-an-ad math leaves out

The cheap figures circulating for AI video are not lies. They are one line of a
thirteen-line bill, published by people who sell that line. Here is the rest of
it, including the number nobody has measured.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How much does an AI video ad cost?

Generation costs between roughly $1.50 and $18.00 for thirty finished seconds
at published list rates, if nothing is retried. Nothing is ever not retried, and
the retry rate is the number nobody publishes.

That is the shape of this whole subject. One line of the bill is precisely
knowable because two surfaces publish it. The line that swings the total by more
than an order of magnitude has no benchmark anywhere, and most of the remaining
lines have no published price at all.

<!-- SECTION: generation cost -->

## What generation actually costs

Read off live surfaces, one at a time.

> Published list prices put a second of generated video between roughly $0.05
> and $0.60, depending on model tier and resolution, with audio included at the
> default on one of the two surfaces.
> Source: published generation price surfaces in this category, read directly
> on 10 September 2026, one credit card and one API price list, quoted per
> second of generated video, no vendor named.

Credits complicate that, so it is worth converting them.

> On the credit route the same second works out at about $0.10 to $0.23 once
> the monthly price is divided by the monthly credit allowance and multiplied
> by the card's stated 12 credits a second.
> Source: one published credit card in this category, read directly on 10
> September 2026, arithmetic shown on the page, single card.

Multiply either band out and thirty seconds looks trivially cheap.

> Thirty seconds of finished screen time, generated once with nothing retried,
> costs between about $1.50 and $18.00 in generation at published list rates.
> Source: arithmetic on published generation prices in this category, 10
> September 2026, shown step by step on this page.

Now the detail that makes those numbers behave differently in practice.

> Neither published price surface says whether a rejected or unusable
> generation is billed. Both bill the attempt, not the result.
> Source: the same two published price surfaces, read in full on 10 September
> 2026. The absence was confirmed at source, not inferred.

The attempt, not the result. Every generation you throw away is on the invoice,
which means the published per-second price is not a price per finished second.
It is a price per try.

## How many generations does one usable shot take?

Nobody knows, and this is the load-bearing gap in every cost estimate you will
read, including the cheap ones and the expensive ones.

> No independent benchmark of generations per usable shot exists. The only
> rate published by anyone selling generation tools comes from that seller's
> own unnamed internal case work, and the only figure from a named practitioner
> covers a single 2024 production. The two differ by roughly seventy-five
> times.
> Source: five buyer queries run in English and Chinese on 10 September 2026,
> top ten results assessed for each. Four of the top eight on the direct
> question measure detection of generated video, which is a different
> quantity on a different population.

Seventy-five times apart. That is not a range, it is two unrelated numbers, and
the honest thing to do with them is show what each does to the bill rather than
average them into a figure that describes nothing.

> Take the same 30 seconds at four generations for each usable one and the
> generation bill runs about $6 to $72. Take it at the roughly 300 to 1 ratio
> one documented production disclosed and the same 30 seconds runs about $450
> to $5,400.
> Source: arithmetic on published generation prices in this category, 10
> September 2026, applied to the two retry ratios that have been published
> anywhere, both shown with their limits on this page.

Look at what moves the total. Price per second varies about twelvefold across
every published rate. The retry rate, on the only two figures anyone has put in
public, varies about seventy-fivefold. The variable everybody quotes is the
smaller of the two.

The higher figure deserves its context, because a single production is not a
market.

> On one documented AI-assisted short film, the post-production lead put the
> ratio of generated source material to finished footage at about 300 to 1,
> with hundreds of generations of 10 to 20 seconds each behind roughly 90
> seconds of finished film.
> Source: a visual-effects trade publication's interview with the production
> team, April 2024. One production, the practitioner's own estimate, no
> counting log published, and the model generation is dated.

One production, a practitioner's estimate rather than a log, and models have
moved since. Treat it as the top of a plausible span rather than as a
measurement. What it does establish is that ratios in the hundreds are
achievable in real work, which is enough to make a four-to-one planning
assumption look optimistic.

That production also shows what generation does not finish.

> That same production still needed rotoscoping, artifact removal, retiming of
> clips that had rendered in unintended slow motion, manual cropping to create
> camera moves, color grading and an added grain treatment, and it ran at 480p
> before an external upscale.
> Source: the same trade-publication interview, April 2024, the team's own
> account of its post pipeline.

## The line the retry rate quietly creates

Retries cost more than their generation fee, and the extra cost lands in a place
no price surface touches. Somebody has to watch them.

Every generated attempt has to be viewed, judged and either kept or discarded,
and that is a person's time at a person's hourly rate. The arithmetic runs the
opposite way from the generation bill. At four generations per usable shot, a
thirty-second film means reviewing a couple of minutes of material to keep
thirty seconds, which is trivial. At the higher published ratio it means sitting
through a great deal of footage to keep the same thirty seconds, and every one
of those attempts needs a human decision.

So the two ratios do not just change the generation line by seventy-five times.
They change the shape of the job. One is a production task with a small review
step attached. The other is mostly a review task with generation attached, and
it is staffed, scheduled and budgeted differently.

This is why the generation price being close to free is less reassuring than it
sounds. Cheap attempts remove the friction that used to limit how many attempts
anyone made, and the cost migrates to the reviewing seat, which is not cheap and
does not scale by spending more on compute. No published source measures how
much time that takes, so this page puts no number on it. It belongs on your
estimate as a line with your own hours in it.

## Is AI video really a dollar per ad?

On generation alone, roughly yes. That concession matters, because the counter
argument is not that the number is invented.

> The circulating figure of roughly one to five dollars an AI ad is a market
> claim. No publisher in that chain states a sample, an instrument or a
> definition of a finished ad, and every one of them sells generation.
> Source: a circulating market claim in this category, observed again in
> September 2026. Quoted as a claim, never as evidence.

Run it yourself. At the cheapest published rate, a fifteen-second cut generated
once is well under a dollar. So the arithmetic holds, on one line, under
assumptions the claim does not state: no retries, no direction, no sound
design, no clearance, no versioning, and a definition of "an ad" that means a
file rather than something cleared to run.

The useful comparison is against a person's time rather than against another
tool's price. In the United States, published medians and the published
compensation load give an hourly figure for the roles that finish this work.

> In the United States, the median annual wage was $75,420 for film and video
> editors, $74,990 for camera operators in television, video and film, and
> $114,850 for art directors. Loaded at the published private-industry
> compensation multiplier of about 1.43 and divided by a 2,080-hour year, that
> is about $52, $52 and $79 an hour.
> Source: the national occupational employment and wage statistics program, May
> 2025 reference period, a full establishment survey, with the load taken from
> the national employer compensation cost series, March 2026 reference period.
> US data, and the 2,080-hour year is a stated convention.

At the cheapest published rate, two full minutes of generation costs less than a
sixth of one loaded editor hour, on US figures. Generation is genuinely close to
free next to the people around it. Which is exactly why pricing the whole film
on the generation line is the wrong instrument: you are optimizing the cheapest
input while the expensive ones stay unexamined.

## A 30-second brand film, line by line

Thirteen lines. Two have published prices. The empty cells are the point of the
table, and they are empty because no defensible published figure exists, not
because the work is free.

| Line | Published price available |
|---|---|
| Concept and direction | No |
| Script and storyboard | No |
| Shot plates, where anything real must appear | No |
| Generation, first pass | Yes, about $1.50 to $18.00 for 30 seconds |
| Generation, retries | Rate published, ratio not. Between about $6 and $5,400 |
| Selection and review time | No |
| Rotoscoping and artifact cleanup | No |
| Retiming and crop-based camera moves | No |
| Upscale and finishing | No |
| Color grade and grain | No |
| Sound design, music and mix | No |
| Rights clearance and talent releases | No |
| Versioning and cutdowns | No |

Two rows out of thirteen. Any figure presented as the cost of an AI video was
built by filling in eleven blanks from somewhere, and the page giving you that
figure is usually selling one of the two rows that are real.

## What AI video generation does not cover

Rights, most consistently, and the industry's own paperwork demonstrates it.

> The standard bid form of the US commercial production trade body itemizes a
> production across sections A through P, from prep crew to breakout talent,
> and carries no line at all for media usage payments.
> Source: the trade body's own free bid form, updated January 2023, downloaded
> and read in full September 2026.

Sixteen sections covering everything from prep to talent, and usage is not one
of them. What you pay to keep the film running sits outside the document the
industry uses to price making it, which means it sits outside any generation
quote too.

> The same trade body's 2026 bidding guide tells bidders to establish whether a
> stated budget is all in, and whether stills, social elements and behind the
> scenes carry separate decks and separate budgets.
> Source: the trade body's published bidding reference guide, 2026 edition.

Versioning has its own trap, and it is a rights trap rather than a production
one.

> Under the US performers' union commercials agreement effective 1 April 2025,
> alternate scenes shot for one commercial in the same session are not treated
> as new commercials, while an edit or lift outside the defined parameters
> creates a new commercial and new fees.
> Source: the union commercials agreement effective April 2025, as read by two
> independent law firms. The union's own rate documents are not machine
> readable.

A cutdown that steps outside those parameters is a new commercial with new fees
attached. Since cutdowns are precisely what volume video programs produce, this
is the line most likely to be discovered late, and generation cost has nothing
to do with it.

## When is AI video genuinely cheaper?

Often, and the conditions are specific enough to check against your own brief.

**When the shot would have required something expensive to stage.** A location,
a crowd, a weather condition, an impossible camera move. Here generation
replaces a real production line item, and the saving is the whole line rather
than a rate difference.

**When the output is a variant of something already made.** Recuts, aspect
ratios, background swaps and language versions built off an approved master.
The direction is already paid for, so generation is doing arithmetic rather than
authorship.

**When volume is the requirement and the bar is fitness rather than craft.**
Performance testing, where the point is to run many variants and let the data
choose.

> Nearly two thirds of brands expect to increase social and short-form video
> production.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> a survey of 50 global brands across more than ten sectors.

And where it is not cheaper: anything whose value is the direction. Generation
prices the making of frames. It does not price deciding what the frames should
be, and on a brand film that decision is most of the cost and all of the risk.

| If this is true | Generation economics |
|---|---|
| The shot needs a location, crowd or condition that must be staged | Strongly favorable, it replaces a whole line |
| It is a variant of an approved master | Favorable, direction is already paid for |
| Volume matters more than craft on this asset | Favorable, if selection time is budgeted |
| A real product must appear and read accurately | Mixed, plate capture still needed |
| The asset carries the campaign idea | Unfavorable on cost logic, direction dominates |
| Recognizable people appear | Unfavorable until releases and usage are settled |

The AI video production page covers how generation and capture run in one
pipeline, the video production service page covers the direction and finishing
lines that dominate this table, and the production cost estimator will return a
market range against your own volume rather than a quote.

## How this page was built

Every generation price was read directly from a live published price surface on
10 September 2026, one surface at a time, and is quoted per second of generated
video as that surface states it. Bands built from two independent surfaces are
given as ranges. Arithmetic is shown in full so you can rerun it on your own
rates. Where no figure exists, this page says so and prints nothing in its
place.

One thing this page deliberately does not do is give you a single percentage for
generation as a share of total cost. The numerator is publishable and the
denominator is not, so any such percentage would be an invention wearing a
decimal point.

## Questions buyers ask about AI video cost

**How much does an AI video ad cost?**

Generation for thirty finished seconds runs about $1.50 to $18.00 at published
list rates with nothing retried, and about $6 to $72 at four generations per
usable shot. Everything else on a thirteen-line bill, including direction,
cleanup, sound, clearance and versioning, has no published price, so no
trustworthy all-in figure exists.

**Is AI video really a dollar per ad?**

On the generation line alone, roughly yes, and that concession is fair. The
circulating one-to-five-dollar claim states no sample, no instrument and no
definition of a finished ad, and every publisher in that chain sells generation.
It assumes no retries, no direction, no sound, no clearance and no versioning.

**What does AI video generation not cover?**

Direction, shot plates, selection time, rotoscoping and artifact cleanup,
retiming, upscaling, grade, sound, rights clearance and versioning. Usage rights
in particular sit outside the industry's own standard bid form, which itemizes a
production across sections A through P and carries no line for media usage
payments at all.

**How many generations does one usable shot take?**

No independent benchmark exists. The only two published figures are a
generation seller's own unnamed internal case work and one named practitioner's
estimate of roughly 300 to 1 on a single 2024 production. They differ by about
seventy-five times, which is why this page shows the bill at both rather than
averaging them.

**When is AI video genuinely cheaper?**

When the shot would have needed an expensive location, crowd or condition
staged; when the asset is a variant of an approved master, so the direction is
already paid for; and when volume matters more than craft, provided selection
time is budgeted. It is not cheaper when the asset carries the campaign idea,
because direction dominates that cost.
