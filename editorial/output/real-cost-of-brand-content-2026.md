# The real cost of brand content in 2026, from published figures

There is no single number, and this page will not invent one. What follows is
every absolute figure for producing brand content that could be read directly
off the surface publishing it, each one dated, with its method beside it and
the disagreements left in rather than averaged out.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What does brand content production cost in 2026?

Between about $0.25 for one bulk image edit and $372,000 committed for a year
of a dedicated creative team. The spread is that wide because those are
different units, not different prices for the same thing. hubStudio compiled
the bands below from published surfaces, one card at a time, and averaged none
of them.

One scope note before the table. This page prices the making of an asset:
capture, retouch, layout, edit, generation, the words. It does not price media,
creator sponsorship fees, or the monthly retainers sold under the name content
marketing, all three of which dominate the search results for this question and
measure something else entirely. Every figure below was re-read at source on 17
September 2026.

## How was this page compiled?

It is a compilation, not a survey. Nobody was polled, nothing was modeled, and
no figure here originates with hubStudio. Every band was read off the surface
that publishes it, and the rule for getting onto the page was fixed before the
reading started.

A figure qualifies only if it clears four conditions at once. It has to be an
absolute amount, in the currency its own source publishes, against a unit that
source states. It has to have been read directly on the publishing surface: a
seller's own pricing page, a national statistical release, or a trade body's
own instrument. It has to carry a date. And the way it was produced has to be
statable in one sentence.

That last condition does most of the work. It is why a rate card counts as
evidence of what one seller publicly charges on the day it was read, and never
as a market average. Where two or more independent cards agree, the band is a
range. Where one card carries the figure alone, the table says single-source,
because one card is not a market.

The collection window: pricing pages were read on 9 and 10 September 2026 and
every load-bearing one was re-read on 17 September 2026. Three Chinese rate
cards carry their own dates, from June 2023 to July 2026. The statistical
series carry their own reference periods, which are not the same as the date
they were read. Trade body instruments date from January 2023, 2026 and March
2025.

Here is what was thrown out, and why.

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| Savings percentages: "cut costs 30 to 40 percent", "up to 80 percent", "about 40 percent offshore" | A percentage of an unstated baseline cannot be checked, and every instance found was published by a party selling the cheaper option |
| Per-ad claims, the circulating one to five dollars an AI ad | No publisher in the chain states a sample, an instrument, or what a finished ad includes, and each one sells generation |
| Figures published by the party they flatter, with no method | A seller pricing both sides of its own comparison wins it by construction |
| Aggregator and roundup figures | They restate unnamed sources, so no figure traces back to the surface that set it |
| Gated figures | A reader cannot check a number sitting behind a membership, an email form, or a priced book |
| Estimates of a price a seller does not publish | An estimate is not a price |
| Survey figures with no sample, instrument or fielding date | There is nothing to test them against |
| Figures found in a search summary but absent from the source they cite | Two of these were "2025" commercial production figures citing a survey discontinued in 2014 |
| Monthly content marketing retainers and creator sponsorship fees | They price management, media or audience reach, not the making of an asset |
| hubStudio's own rates | This page prices a market, not a studio |

The production cost estimator on this site runs on the same discipline and is
also ungated.

## Does this page pass the tests it applies to everyone else?

Partly. A cost benchmark in this category should pass four tests: the sample is
stated, the figures are absolute, the data is ungated, and the author does not
sell the thing being priced. Nothing examined for this page passes all four.

> Nothing examined for this page that prices a brand content asset passes all
> four tests of a usable benchmark: a stated sample, absolute figures, open
> access and an author who does not sell the work. The only sources that pass
> all four price an hour of a person's time.
> Source: ranking pages, trade body instruments, a creative dataset and three
> national statistical series assessed against the four tests, September 2026.

So here is this page scored against its own instrument, including the test it
fails.

| Test | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sample stated | Partly | Every band says how many cards or which series stands behind it. That is a count of sources, not a sample of the market, and published cards lean toward productized work because that is the end of the market that publishes prices at all |
| Absolute figures | Meets | Every figure is an amount against a unit. No saving is expressed as a percentage |
| Ungated | Meets | Nothing here or behind its links asks for an email |
| Independent author | Misses | hubStudio produces assets in several of the categories priced here |

That last row is not a technicality and it will not be softened. What can be
offered instead of independence: no hubStudio figure appears anywhere on this

## What happened when these figures were re-checked a week later?

Two of them moved. This is the part worth reading before you use any cost band
from anywhere, including this one.

Every source here was read at the start of September and re-fetched on 17
September 2026, seven days later. One studio pricing page had rebuilt how it
expresses price. At the first reading it published complexity uplifts as dollar
figures: $75 for ghost mannequin or oversized work, $100 for jewelry and
watches, $150 for a hand model image, $190 for a full-body model image. Seven
days on, not one of those four numbers appears anywhere on the card. The same
ladder is now published as credit multipliers, one credit for a standard image
rising to four for a full-body model, against headline rates of "from $50 per
image" and "from $95 per image".

Those four figures are therefore cut from this page. They could be reconstructed
by multiplying the multipliers against the $50 floor, and three of the four old
numbers would reappear, which is exactly why it is not done here: "from $50" is
a floor, not a rate, and the arithmetic would publish a price the card does not.

The second change was quieter. The national employer compensation series
advanced a reference period, and the newer release moved the benefits load from
1.4294 to 1.4287. Both round to 1.43, so nothing derived from it on this page
shifts by a printed digit. It is recorded anyway, because a figure that was
checked and a figure that was remembered look identical on the page, right up
to the moment one of them is wrong.

The practical rule: treat any cost band published without a date as a
historical artifact, because in this category it usually is one.

## What does a product or model image cost?

Every band below is quoted against the unit its own source states, and where two
sources use different units they get two rows, never one merged range. That rule
holds for the rest of this page.

> Published studio rate cards put standard ecommerce capture between roughly
> $39 and $50 an image. One card prices a photo at $39 and adds a $149 studio
> fee per booking; the other starts product work at $50 an image against a $300
> project minimum.
> Source: published product photography pricing pages in this category, two
> independent cards read directly on 17 September 2026, prices quoted against
> the unit each card states.

Model work is priced on one card only, so it is labeled single-source in the
table and here.

> Published studio rate cards put a standard product image at $39 to $50, a
> model or lifestyle image at about $95, a short video clip at about $93 and a
> single UGC video at about $89, with editing add-ons around $49 each and a
> finished pro edit at about $199.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly, prices quoted as each card states them.

Read that one carefully. Only the $39 to $50 figure sits on two cards. Every
other number in it comes from a single card, which is why the master table
labels each of them single-source rather than letting them inherit the
triangulated label from the row above. Quoted whole, without that caveat, the
band reads far more solid than the evidence under it.

The Chinese side of the same work prices differently and publishes more of its
conditions.

> A Chinese commercial photography studio's published 2026 rate card lists
> flat-lay shots at 80 to 150 RMB an image, in-studio model shots at 200 to
> 300 RMB, retouching at 50 to 200 RMB an image and short video at 3,000 to
> 15,000 RMB a piece, on a five-style minimum.
> Source: a published Chinese ecommerce photography rate card, dated 20 July
> 2026, described by its publisher as its own executing rates for the
> Guangzhou and Pearl River Delta region. One studio's book, no survey behind
> it.

No conversion is printed between those two sets of figures, deliberately.
Applying one day's exchange rate to one regional card would imply a precision
the card does not have, and the minimums and inclusions differ on both sides.
Read each in its own currency. The ecommerce design service pages carry the
same discipline for the deliverables themselves.

## What does retouch cost?

Four different services share this one word, and the published prices sit more
than a hundredfold apart because of it.

> Published rate cards in this category put the floor for ecommerce image
> editing between roughly $0.25 and $1.20 an image, with one card adding a
> fixed platform fee of about $95 a month on top.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly and compared, no vendor named.

On the 17 September re-read the top of that band reads $1.19, for image masking
and multi-clipping, which is the figure the master table carries. Two specialty
operations sit above it on the same card, at $2.99 and $3.99, and they were
outside the band at both readings.

Against capture, that is almost nothing.

> Against published capture of $39 to $50 an image, a per-image retouch price
> of about $0.95 is between 1.9 and 2.4 percent of the capture line, and the
> full published retouch band runs from about 0.5 to about 3.1 percent of it.
> Retouch is not production.
> Source: two published pricing categories, both collected September 2026,
> divided one into the other. Capture from two studio cards, retouch from two
> editing cards, all four read directly.

The access conditions matter more than the unit price at low volume.

> One published retouch card guarantees 200 edited images a day with
> next-morning delivery, and declines anyone under 1,000 images a year, sending
> them to a trial instead. Its $95 monthly platform fee doubles the unit price
> at 100 images a month and adds about 10 percent at 1,000.
> Source: a published image editing pricing page in this category, read 10
> September 2026. The access condition and the fee are stated on the card, the
> amortization is division.

## What does a detail page cost?

This is the row where the unit problem stops being theoretical.

> Published Chinese rate cards for ecommerce detail-page design disagree by
> roughly an order of magnitude, from 200 to 300 RMB a page at the low end of
> a 2023 page to 1,500 to 5,000 RMB a set on a 2026 page, where a set is
> defined as twenty pieces: main images, the detail-page cover, scene images
> and detail shots.
> Source: published Chinese design rate cards, dated 12 June 2023 and 30 April
> 2026, both re-read 17 September 2026, no company named. Published prices
> only, and the conflict is stated rather than averaged.

Now divide. Fifteen hundred to five thousand RMB across twenty pieces is 75 to
250 RMB an asset, which overlaps the older page's 200 to 300 RMB almost
completely. Most of that order-of-magnitude gap was never a price difference at
all. It was a unit difference, and it survived three years of being quoted
because neither page made the reader check.

Two limits travel with that arithmetic. The 2023 figure prices one detail page
while the 2026 set bundles main images, a cover and scene shots alongside detail
shots, so the two still are not the same thing. And the 2026 page is sponsored
content. That is precisely why it is published as a conflict rather than as a
reading of the market, and why the far lower figure the same page quotes for its
own sponsor's tool appears nowhere here.

For the English-speaking markets there is no band at all. Searched in September
2026, every result was a listicle, an agency roundup or a tool vendor
comparison, and not one price was read on a seller's own card. The absence is
the finding. What it costs to localize a campaign for China covers the
neighboring ground, and the Tmall platform page describes what the deliverable
has to be before anyone can price it.

A clip out of a studio, a finished short, and a re-version of a film that

For originating a brand film there is no current figure to give you, and the
reason is a small piece of category history worth knowing.

> The US advertising agency trade body ran an annual survey of TV commercial
> production costs for 25 years and dropped it in February 2014. Its last data
> year was 2011, when a 30-second spot averaged $354,000, and the reason given
> was that almost nobody shoots a single 30-second commercial on its own
> anymore.
> Source: trade press report of the trade body's decision, February 2014. A
> historical figure from a discontinued survey, not a 2026 price.

Two figures circulating on this query in 2026 cite that survey's "2025 edition"
and a trade body guideline's "average bid". Neither exists on any document that
could be read. They are not printed here, and the reason they are worth
mentioning at all is that they sit on a page ranking for exactly the question
you typed.

## What does subscription access cost?

What a subscription prices is capacity, not assets, and the cards say so once
you read the tier labels rather than the headline.

> Unlimited-request design subscriptions in this category publish tiers
> between roughly $700 and $4,500 a month, billed month to month.
> Source: category rate cards collected September 2026, published pricing
> pages read directly, no vendor named.

> "Unlimited requests" is published alongside a cap on how many requests can be
> worked at once. Across four published cards read in September 2026, that cap
> runs from one to five active items at a time, rising with the tier.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, tier
> labels read directly off the cards, no vendor named.

Both cards re-read on 17 September 2026 still publish that cap in the tier
label itself, in wording like "two active workstreams" and "one daily output".
Nobody is hiding it. It is simply not in the headline.

Above that sits the managed end, where the unit becomes an hour.

> Managed design subscriptions in this category publish monthly floors between
> roughly $6,000 and $15,000, with dedicated tiers from roughly $30,000 a
> month on a twelve-month commitment.
> Source: category rate cards collected September 2026, published pricing
> pages read directly, no vendor named.

> Divide the published managed tiers by their published hours and the category
> prices access at almost exactly $50 a creative hour: $5,995 for 120 hours and
> $8,995 for 180 hours.
> Source: arithmetic on one published rate card in this category, collected

Those hours expire monthly. And the commitment, once you carry the published

> At the dedicated end the same card starts at $30,000 a month plus the $1,000

## What does an in-house team cost?

The only sources in this entire compilation that pass all four tests are the
national wage series. They price an hour of a person, never an asset.

> Median annual wages in the national occupational wage series for May 2025
> run $62,960 for graphic designers, $75,420 for film and video editors,
> $74,990 for camera operators and $114,850 for art directors.
> Source: national occupational employment and wage statistics program, May
> 2025 reference period, full establishment survey.

> US photographers earned a median $44,660 a year, $21.47 an hour, in May 2025,
> with the lowest tenth under $14.82 an hour and the highest tenth over $44.14.
> At the published private-industry load of about 1.43, the median hour costs an
> employer about $31.

Salary is not the cost, which is the mistake that sinks most in-house models

> Turn that into the multiplier a budget holder actually needs and salary

> Source: arithmetic on the national employer compensation cost series, March

## Why do content cost benchmarks disagree with each other?

Because almost nobody publishing one is a disinterested party, and almost
nobody defines the unit before pricing it.

> Across four phrasings a buyer would type about brand content cost, 28 of the
> 34 results were published by a party selling the work, tool or talent being
> priced, and none by a statistical agency or a trade body. None of the six
> pages read in full stated how its own figures were collected.
> Source: search-results audit run 10 September 2026, publisher type recorded
> for every result and six pages read in full, no domain named.

Under that, the same handful of figures recirculates from page to page with the
instrument stripped off. Once a number has been relayed three times it reads
like consensus, and it is the same unsourced number wearing three coats.

Here are the conflicts this page carries rather than resolves, because resolving
them would mean inventing something.

| Where sources disagree | The competing figures | Why they disagree |
|---|---|---|
| Retouch | $0.25 to $1.19 an image, from $9 a premium edit, bundled into a $50 capture, 50 to 200 RMB in China | Four different services share the word: one automated operation, a premium edit add-on, retouch folded into capture, and fine beauty retouch billed separately |
| Detail page | 200 to 300 RMB a page against 1,500 to 5,000 RMB a 20-piece set | Different units three years apart, one of them sponsored content. Matched per asset they nearly overlap |
| Short video | About $93 a studio clip, 3,000 to 15,000 RMB a finished short, from 1,350 EUR a 15-second adaptation | Raw capture, a finished piece, and a re-version of an existing film, in three markets and three currencies |
| Generated video yield | About four generations per usable clip against about 300 to 1 | A tool seller's own case work against one practitioner on one 2024 production, two model generations and two quality bars apart |
| Benefits load | 1.25 to 1.4, circulating, against 1.43 from the national series | The circulating figure misreads benefits as a share of total compensation and applies it as a markup on salary |
| A bought hour against an employed hour | About $50 a creative hour against $54.32 a paid hour for four US seats | Not the same unit. An access hour comes with a concurrency cap and no capture; an employed hour has a productive share nobody publishes |
| Commercial production | $354,000 for a 30-second spot in 2011 against two higher figures labeled 2025, neither printed here | The 2025 figures cite a survey discontinued in 2014 and a trade body guideline that publishes no average, so neither traces to a document that can be read |

That second-to-last row is worth dwelling on, because it is the comparison every
budget holder wants and the one this page will not make. Fifty dollars an hour
and $54.32 an hour are close enough to look like a verdict. They are not the
same hour. One of them is sold with a cap on concurrent work and no camera in
the building; the other is employed, and what share of it is productive is a
number no source publishes in any market.

## Which buying model fits which volume?

Built from the published figures above, not from a crossover point somebody
asserted. No published crossover threshold exists in this category. The only
one found anywhere was seller-published with no method, and it is not here.

| Volume or situation | What the published figures point to | Basis | The caveat that travels with it |

## What does no published band include?

The things that reliably blow up a production budget are absent from every card
read for this page, across two markets.

> No card read for this piece prices sample logistics, reshoots or approval
> rounds. Eight published price surfaces across two markets, and getting the
> product to the studio, getting it back, and shooting it again after a failed
> approval appear on none of them.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, two markets, read directly
> in September 2026. Absence confirmed by reading each card in full.

Rights are the other silence, and it runs all the way to the top of the
category.

> The US commercial production trade body's standard bid form itemizes a
> production across sections A through P, from prep crew to breakout talent, and
> carries no line at all for media usage payments.
> Source: the trade body's own free bid form, updated January 2023, downloaded
> and read in full September 2026.

So the instrument the category built to cost a production in full carries no
line for the payments that running the work then triggers.

Where the better data might have been, there are locked doors and missing
columns.

> The UK advertiser trade body's 2025 creative and media agency hourly rate
> benchmarks are released only to members who contribute their own rates, in
> aggregated form.
> Source: the trade body's own published access condition, read September 2026.

> The largest creative dataset published in this category, 578,750 creatives
> across 6,015 brands and $1.29 billion of ad spend, reports weekly creative
> volume and expected hit rate. It reports no cost per asset in any currency.
> Source: a creative analytics benchmark in this category, 2026, platform data,
> period covered not stated by the publisher.

Six hundred thousand creatives, more than a billion dollars of spend behind
them, and not one currency figure per asset. That is not an oversight. It is
what the category measures.

For scale, production is a smaller slice of the budget than most people
assume, and shrinking.

> Production accounted for an average of 19 percent of total marketing budgets,
> against 24 percent before the pandemic.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> a survey of 50 global brands across more than ten sectors. It publishes no
> absolute cost per asset.

## Questions buyers ask about content cost

**How much does brand content cost in 2026?**

There is no single figure, and any page giving you one has not told you what it
is pricing. Published bands run from about $0.25 for a bulk image edit to
$372,000 committed for a year of a dedicated team. The master table above gives
each band against the unit its own source states, with the date and confidence
attached.

**What does a product image cost to produce?**

Two published studio cards read in September 2026 put standard ecommerce
capture at $39 to $50 an image. One adds a $149 studio fee per booking, the
other a $300 project minimum. A published Chinese rate card dated July 2026
prices flat lay at 80 to 150 RMB an image on a five-set minimum. Neither card
prices sample logistics or reshoots.

**What does AI video really cost per usable shot?**

Nobody can tell you honestly, and the gap is the answer. Generation lists at
$0.05 to $0.60 a second, so 30 finished seconds costs $1.50 to $18.00 if
nothing is retried. At four generations per usable one it is $6 to $72. At the
one disclosed ratio of roughly 300 to 1 it is $450 to $5,400. No independent
benchmark of that ratio exists.

**Is a design subscription cheaper than a studio?**

Wrong question, because they sell different units. A subscription buys capacity
with a published cap of one to five items in progress at once, and no card read
includes capture, talent booking or rights clearance. A studio card prices a
delivered asset. Until you fix the unit, the comparison has no meaning in either
direction.

**Why do content cost benchmarks disagree?**

Because 28 of 34 results across four buyer phrasings were published by a party
selling the work being priced, none by a statistical agency, and not one of the
six pages read in full stated how its own figures were collected. Under that,
undefined units do the rest: one word, retouch, covers four different services
priced more than a hundredfold apart.

**What does it cost to translate a campaign for China?**

Published translation rate cards put English to Simplified Chinese at $0.08 to
$0.18 a source word, with no method behind the band. Transcreation is priced
per hour or per project instead, and no absolute figure for it is published
anywhere. The words are also the cheapest part: what it costs to localize a
campaign for China covers the visual production the word rate never touches.
