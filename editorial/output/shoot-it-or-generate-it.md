# Shoot it or generate it: the decision rule, asset by asset

Most advice on this splits the world into believers and holdouts. The useful
version is narrower. There are four conditions that force a camera, and once
you can name them the rest of the catalog stops being an argument.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## When should you shoot a product and when should you generate it?

Shoot when the asset has to prove something physical, when a regulator or a
marketplace requires the real item, when a real person appears, or when scale
is the point. Generate the variants, the environments, and the repetitions
around those anchors. hubStudio runs most ecommerce catalogs at about 70
percent generated and 30 percent shot, and the interesting question is which
30 percent stays in front of a camera.

| Condition | What forces it | How firm |
|---|---|---|
| Material truth | Reflection, contact and material response are where generation measurably breaks | Physical, and improving |
| Regulated categories | Marketplace image rules and misleading-advertising codes | Written down, enforced |
| Talent | Synthetic performer disclosure statutes | Written down, expanding |
| Physical scale | Size cues the viewer reads and a generator has no reason to respect | Softest, test per category |

<!-- SECTION: The four conditions -->

## What are the four conditions that force real capture?

These are not preferences. Each one has something behind it: a physical limit,
a marketplace rule, or a statute.

**Material truth.** Anything whose selling point is how a surface behaves.
Fabric weave, brushed metal, glass, liquid, anything transparent or reflective.
This is the one place where the limitation is measurable rather than
aesthetic.

> Physical realism, not semantic accuracy, is where current image generation
> still breaks: a 900-case benchmark covering optics, mechanics and state
> transition scored eleven current image editing models against
> human-annotated reflection surfaces and contact points, and every
> open-source model scored below 60, with only two closed systems slightly
> above it.
> Source: PICABench, image editing physical-realism benchmark, October 2025.

There is a structural reason underneath the scores. A generated image is
perceptually convincing without being physically plausible, because it never
originated from a three-dimensional scene and so cannot be tied back to
physical parameters, a point made in the Journal of Vision in March 2026 on
the basis of tests run against earlier generators. Read that for the structure
rather than as a verdict on this quarter's models. It holds either way. An
image that never came from a scene has nothing to be checked against.

**Regulated categories.** Where the law or the platform says the picture has to
be the product. Marketplaces moved first and moved specifically.

> A Chinese marketplace became the first to publish a rule aimed specifically
> at AI fake product images, naming four failure categories: product material
> or style that does not match the real item, distorted or exaggerated product
> effects, an obvious cutout and paste look, and bodies or scenes that break
> physical laws.
> Source: Economic Information Daily, March 2025, reporting the
> platform's own rule announcement and its detection-model enforcement.

> A general marketplace's seller image guidelines state that images generated
> by artificial intelligence must be truthful, accurate and not misleading, and
> that sellers must use actual product images that accurately reflect the
> product being sold.
> Source: marketplace seller image guidelines, page dated May 2026, the
> platform's own published rules.

Advertising regulators are enforcing the same principle without needing a
special AI rule.

> A UK advertising regulator upheld 22 complaints against an ad for a robot
> toy, finding that footage which appeared to show real or AI-generated
> puppies gave the impression the product was highly realistic, and ruled that
> the advertiser must not misleadingly represent or exaggerate the
> functionality and appearance of its products.
> Source: UK advertising regulator ruling, 25 March 2026, an upheld
> investigation under the code's misleading advertising, substantiation and
> exaggeration rules.

> The same regulator's guidance holds that the advertising code is
> media-neutral: the rules apply regardless of how the content was created, and
> the advertiser remains responsible for any AI output used in its ads.
> Source: UK advertising regulator guidance, June 2026.

Media-neutral is the sentence to take away. Nobody is going to ask how the
image was made. They are going to ask whether it overstated the product.

Food and cosmetics carry their own version of the same duty, and both predate
the AI question entirely.

> EU food law bars misleading a consumer by suggesting, through the appearance,
> the description or pictorial representations, the presence of an ingredient
> that has in fact been substituted, and applies the same rule to the
> advertising and presentation of the food, including its shape and appearance.
> Source: Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 7(1)(d) and Article 7(4),
> adopted 25 October 2011, as summarized on the European Commission's own fair
> information practices page.

EU cosmetics rules do something similar through their honesty criterion, where
a presentation of a product's performance may not go beyond the supporting
evidence, under Commission Regulation (EU) No 655/2013, in application since
July 2013.

**Talent.** Any real person, and increasingly any person-shaped asset at all.

> A New York statute signed in December 2025 and in force since 9 June 2026
> requires an advertisement using a synthetic performer to conspicuously
> disclose it, defining a synthetic performer as a digitally created asset
> intended to create the impression of a visual performance by a human
> performer who is not any identifiable real person.
> Source: New York Senate Bill S8420A, signed 11 December 2025, effective 180
> days later.

> EU transparency obligations for synthetic content apply from 2 August 2026,
> with a limited grace period to 2 December 2026 for marking obligations on
> systems already on the market, and require deployers of deep fakes to
> disclose them on first exposure in a clear and distinguishable manner.
> Source: European Commission FAQ on Article 50 transparency obligations, page
> last updated 24 July 2026.

Generating a person does not remove the paperwork. It changes which paperwork.

**Physical scale.** Vehicles, architecture, furniture in a real room, anything
where the viewer's sense of size comes from cues a generator has no reason to
respect. This is the softest of the four and the one that moves fastest, so
test it per category rather than assuming.

<!-- SECTION: What favors generation -->

## What should you generate instead?

Everything that repeats. Colorways of an item already shot, environments around
a fixed hero, crops for six platforms, localized set dressing, background
extension, the forty-third lifestyle scene for a product you photographed
properly once.

The economics run backwards from how people usually describe them. The
expensive part of a shoot is not the shutter. It is scheduling, sample
logistics, styling, the crew day, and the reshoot when a sample turns up in the
wrong finish. Generation removes almost none of the cost of the first image and
almost all of the cost of the two hundredth.

<!-- SECTION: The decision table -->

## The rule, by asset type

| Asset | Default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hero product, primary listing image | Shoot | Material truth, and marketplace rules that require the actual product |
| Colorway and material variants | Generate from the shot hero | Same geometry, same lighting, no new physical information |
| Texture and detail crops | Shoot | This is the claim being made |
| Lifestyle and in-context scenes | Generate, around a shot anchor | Repetition is the cost driver |
| Model and talent imagery | Shoot, or generate with disclosure and a release | Statute, not preference |
| Food and cosmetics performance shots | Shoot | Pictorial representation rules bind the appearance |
| Platform crops and aspect variants | Generate | No new capture, purely derivative |
| Scale-dependent categories | Shoot the anchor, test the rest | The softest condition, so verify per category |

Read down the "why" column rather than the "default" column. The defaults will
move. The reasons will not, because three of the four conditions are written
into rules rather than into model capability.

<!-- SECTION: What goes wrong -->

## What goes wrong in each direction?

Generating what you should have shot fails publicly and slowly. The image
passes internal review because it looks right on a monitor, then returns data
or a complaint tells you the product did not match the picture. The regulatory
version of that failure is the ruling above.

Shooting what you should have generated fails privately and constantly, and it
looks like a calendar problem rather than a creative one. The catalog ships
late because eight hundred variants queued behind a studio day. A market gets
skipped because a reshoot could not be scheduled. Nobody writes that up as a
failure. It shows up as a slower launch every season.

The asymmetry is worth naming. Over-generating produces a small number of
severe failures. Over-shooting produces a large number of invisible ones.
Teams reliably fear the first and absorb the second, which is how catalogs end
up costing more than anyone budgeted for a reason nobody can point at.

The cost of getting it wrong sits in rework, not in the split itself. A hero
generated when it should have been captured has usually propagated into every
variant derived from it by the time anyone notices, so the fix is the anchor
plus everything downstream. A variant shot when it could have been generated
costs a studio slot that something else needed, normally the hero of a
different product. Both errors push work later in the calendar, which is where
it gets expensive.

practice, the AI image production page covers the generation side, and the

## Questions teams ask about shooting against generating

**When should a product be photographed instead of generated?**

When the asset has to carry material truth, when a marketplace or a regulator
requires the actual product, when a real person is in it, or when physical
scale is the message. Those four conditions cover most of what still belongs
in front of a camera. Everything else is a judgment call about volume.

**What can current image generation not do convincingly?**

Physical behavior rather than subject matter. An October 2025 benchmark
covering optics, mechanics and state transitions scored eleven current editing
models against human-annotated reflection surfaces and contact points, and
every open-source model scored below 60. Reflections, contact shadows and
material response are where it shows.

**Do marketplaces allow AI-generated product images?**

It varies, and the direction is toward requiring the image to represent the
real item. One Chinese marketplace published a rule in March 2025 naming four
AI failure categories including mismatched material and exaggerated effects. A
general marketplace's published seller guidelines require AI images to be
truthful and accurate and require sellers to use actual product images. Check
the specific platform rules before a catalog runs.

**Does an advertising regulator treat AI imagery differently?**

Not as a separate category. UK regulator guidance from June 2026 states the
advertising code is media-neutral: the rules apply regardless of how content
was created, and the advertiser remains responsible for AI output used in its
ads. In March 2026 the same regulator upheld 22 complaints where footage gave
a misleading impression of how realistic a product was.

**Do I need a disclosure for a generated model or presenter?**

In some markets, yes. A New York statute in force since 9 June 2026 requires an
advertisement using a synthetic performer to conspicuously disclose it. EU
transparency obligations for synthetic content apply from 2 August 2026 and
require deployers of deep fakes to disclose on first exposure. Treat a
generated presenter as a paperwork question, not a shortcut around one.

**What is a sensible starting ratio?**

Most catalogs land near 70 percent generated once a proper set of anchors
exists, but the ratio is an output, not a target. Work out which assets meet
the four conditions, shoot those, generate what derives from them, and let the
percentage fall where it falls. A team that starts from the percentage ends up
arguing about the wrong thing.
