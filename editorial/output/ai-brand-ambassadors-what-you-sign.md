# AI brand ambassadors: what you actually sign

The pages ranking for this question are risk explainers written by people who
have never run a scan session. They tell you a digital replica creates exposure.
They do not tell you which clauses carry it, or what the one published standard
form actually asks you to fill in.

This is a clause map, not a template. It describes production practice rather
than legal advice, it is written from the capture side, and the final document
gets signed off by a lawyer. Nothing here replaces that.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What goes in an AI avatar talent release?

Ten things, and the published standard form collects three of them. Scope, term,
territory, permitted uses, retraining rights, derivative rights, revocation,
compensation, sunset and deletion.

The gap between that list and the form is the whole subject. A model exists as a
capability long after a campaign stops running, and the paperwork most
productions sign was designed for a photograph.

<!-- SECTION: talent types -->

## Three kinds of talent, three kinds of paperwork

Get this wrong at the top and every clause below is aimed at the wrong risk.

| Type | What it is | What the paperwork has to carry | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scanned real person | A model built from a named individual's capture or prior performance | The full clause map: the specificity test, the representation condition, retention, deletion, survival after death | Treating the scan session as part of the shoot rather than as its own consent event with its own description |
| Composite | An output blended from more than one person's material, or a person plus generated material | Every contributing person's consent, each with its own description, plus a record of which inputs went in | A thin blend is still readily identifiable as someone, and the statutory definitions turn on identifiability rather than on intent |
| Fully synthetic character | An asset creating the impression of a human who is not recognizable as any identifiable person | No talent release for a person who does not exist. Instead, proof of how it was made and the disclosure duty attaching to the ad | Prompting with a real person's name, image or recording, which is the mechanism by which a synthetic character stops being synthetic |

That third trap deserves its own line, because it is the one that catches
productions who thought they had avoided the problem entirely.

> Where a producer creates a synthetic performer using the name of an identified
> performer, or any image, photograph or recording of that performer in the
> prompt to a generative system, the producer must obtain that performer's
> consent and bargain for payment at no less than the minimum session fee then
> in effect.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.1.A.3,
> agreement text, May 2025.

A prompt is an input. Type a famous name into it and you have cast that person,
as far as this agreement is concerned.

## The specificity test, stated accurately

This gets reported as "California requires a reasonably specific description".
It does not. The statute withdraws enforceability from a clause that fails a
three-part test, all parts at once, and only as to performances fixed from the
start of 2025.

> California Labor Code section 927, added by Chapter 259 of the Statutes of
> 2024 and effective 1 January 2025, makes a personal or professional services
> provision unenforceable, but only as it relates to a new performance fixed on
> or after 1 January 2025 by a digital replica of the individual, and only where
> the provision meets all of the listed conditions at once.
> Source: California Labor Code section 927, code text retrieved 9 September
> 2026.

The practical consequence of "all at once" is worth sitting with. A clause with
a strong description survives an unrepresented signer. A thin description
survives if the person's own counsel negotiated it.

> The third condition is that the individual was not represented, either by
> "legal counsel who negotiated on behalf of the individual licensing the
> individual's digital replica rights, and the commercial terms are stated
> clearly and conspicuously in a contract or other writing signed or initialed
> by the individual", or by "a labor union representing workers who do the
> proposed work, and the terms of their collective bargaining agreement
> expressly addresses uses of digital replicas".
> Source: California Labor Code section 927(a)(3)(A) and (B), code text
> retrieved 9 September 2026.

There is also a carve-out most summaries drop.

> The second condition is that the provision "does not include a reasonably
> specific description of the intended uses of the digital replica". The statute
> then adds that a missing description does not render the provision
> unenforceable "if the uses are consistent with the terms of the contract for
> the performance of personal or professional services and the fundamental
> character of the photography or soundtrack as recorded or performed".
> Source: California Labor Code section 927(a)(2)(A) and (a)(2)(B), code text
> retrieved 9 September 2026.

So what does "reasonably specific" look like in practice? No court has told us.
The best available answer comes from the parties who bargained the language,
who supplied a worked example.

> The agreement supplies its own worked example of a reasonably specific
> description: "Performer agrees that Producer may create and use Performer's
> Digital Replica to generate a future performance in which Performer's
> character floats in order to represent the lightness of advertiser's yogurt."
> It adds that a description is reasonably specific if it conveys information
> consistent with what a performer would receive at casting and audition,
> including scripts and storyboards.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, digital replica
> examples, agreement text, May 2025.

That is the usable standard: describe the use as concretely as you would brief
it at casting. If your description would not let a performer picture the
finished spot, it is not specific enough.

One caution about everything drawn from that agreement, on this page and
anywhere else. It binds signatories to it, in one market, for one kind of work.
It is not law. It is used here because it is the most detailed published
standard anybody has written, which makes it a good instrument even where it
does not apply to you.

## The one published standard form, and what it leaves out

A model release for this exists, exactly one, and reading it is instructive.

> A standard-form Digital Replica Rider exists and is published. Exhibit 3 to
> the 2025 commercials contract was prepared jointly by the advertiser and
> agency bargaining body and the performers' union and posted on 17 November
> 2025. It is three pages. It collects consent to create a replica, consent to
> use a pre-existing one, and the permitted uses, in a table headed "Consented
> to Uses of the Digital Replica" whose only columns are the agency, producer
> and product, the reasonably specific description of uses, and the date the
> performer was engaged.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Exhibit 3 Digital Replica Rider, form text,
> published November 2025.

Three columns, one of which is a date. Now the absence.

> The published rider carries no field for term, territory, media, exclusivity,
> compensation, revocation, retraining, deletion or sunset. Those live in the
> collective agreement the rider hangs off, not on the page the performer signs.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Exhibit 3 Digital Replica Rider, read in
> full on 9 September 2026 for such fields. None is present.

Nine of the ten things in the clause map are not on the form. They are not
missing, they sit in a collective agreement that only exists if your production
is covered by one. If it is not, nobody has written those clauses for you, and a
production borrowing this rider outside its own context inherits the blanks
without the backstop.

## The clause map

Ten clauses. For each, what it has to settle and where the published standard
puts it.

| Clause | What it has to settle | Where the published standard puts it |
|---|---|---|
| Scope and identification | Which person, which capture, which model built from it | The rider, as consent to create or to use a pre-existing replica |
| Permitted uses | The reasonably specific description, concrete enough to picture | The rider's only substantive column |
| Term and maximum period of use | How long the replica may generate performances | The collective agreement, not the form |
| Territory, media and exclusivity | Where it runs, on what, and against whom | Neither. You write these |
| Forward consent | Whether it reaches commercials not yet engaged, and on what conditions | The agreement, tightly bounded |
| Alteration limits | What may not be done to the likeness without explicit consent | The agreement, as a default prohibition |
| Compensation | Session, use and holding treatment for replica-generated work | The agreement, as a multiple |
| Retention | How long the model may be held, and on what consent | The agreement, with two clocks |
| Deletion and certification | What happens when consent to retain is absent | The agreement, with a written certificate |
| Survival and succession | What happens after death, and who consents then | The agreement, plus the statutes |

The rows saying "neither" are the ones to look at first. Territory, media and
exclusivity are standard talent-deal terms that the replica paperwork simply
does not reach, so they carry over from your normal release or they do not exist
at all.

Three clauses have published detail worth quoting, because they are the ones
productions most often assume rather than negotiate.

**Notice runs before capture, not after.**

> Notice runs ahead of the capture, not after it. The producer must use
> commercially reasonable efforts to notify the performer and obtain consent at
> least 48 hours before the performance used to create the replica, or at least
> 24 hours where the performer is engaged inside that window, and must state in
> the casting notice that a digital replica will be created when that is known.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.C.1,
> agreement text, May 2025.

The casting notice, not the call sheet. If a scan is planned, it belongs in the
document people read before they decide whether to audition.

**Alteration is prohibited by default.**

> Unless the performer has consented to it explicitly inside the reasonably
> specific description, a replica may not be used to change the performer's
> physical traits, to make them appear nude or partially nude, or to create a
> performance that could be offensive to a reasonable person.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.C.3,
> agreement text, May 2025.

**Training is a separate permission.**

> On training, the agreement is short: the producer may not use, or authorize
> any other party to use, the results and proceeds of services rendered under
> the contracts to train generative AI systems without the union's consent.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.1.A.4,
> agreement text, May 2025.

Consent to build one person's replica is not consent to feed the footage into a
general model. Those are different asks and they need different clauses.

## What happens when the campaign ends and the model persists

This is the part almost nobody drafts, and the published standard is unusually
concrete about it. Two clocks run.

> Retention is bounded. A producer may hold the replica during the maximum
> period of use of the commercial it generated a performance for, and holding it
> longer requires written consent bargained with the performer. Where no
> commercial is in fact made, the producer may hold the replica for 24 months
> from the date consent to creation was given, and any further retention is
> limited to periods of not more than 24 months each.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.D.6.b,
> agreement text, May 2025.

Note the second clock. A scan that never became anything still has an expiry,
which means an abandoned project leaves a deletion obligation behind it.

> Where consent to retain has not been obtained, the producer must "permanently
> and irrevocably delete all copies of the Digital Replica and certify in
> writing to the Performer within ninety (90) days that such deletion has
> occurred". Deleting the model does not require destroying commercials already
> produced with it.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.D.7,
> agreement text, May 2025.

Read both halves of that. Ninety days and a written certificate is a contract
term under one agreement, not a general legal requirement anybody owes by
default. And the work already made stays made: deleting the model does not
unmake the commercials.

Access and transfer are handled too, and the transfer clause is the one that
survives a change of agency.

> Access to the replica must be limited to persons with a legitimate business
> need, with commercially reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access, use
> or copying, and a transfer of rights to a retained replica must be recorded on
> the transfer of rights assumption agreement, with the transferee bound by the
> same replica provisions.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Sections XX.D.6.a
> and XX.D.6.c, agreement text, May 2025.

Death does not end the question either, and the clocks here are statutory rather
than contractual.

> Consent granted during a performer's lifetime continues to be valid after
> death unless it was explicitly limited when it was granted or by later mutual
> agreement. Where no valid consent exists, the producer obtains consent from
> the deceased performer's authorized representative, or from the union where no
> representative can be identified or located.
> Source: 2025 Commercials Contract Memorandum of Agreement, Section XX.C.4,
> agreement text, May 2025.

The statutory picture differs by state, and by a wide margin.

> A deceased personality, in the California statute, is "any natural person
> whose name, voice, signature, photograph, or likeness has commercial value at
> the time of that person's death", and no action lies for a use occurring after
> the expiration of 70 years after that person's death.
> Source: California Civil Code section 3344.1(h) and (g), code text retrieved
> 9 September 2026.

> New York runs a shorter clock and adds two conditions. Its postmortem right
> lasts 40 years after death, it reaches only a person "domiciled in this state
> at the time of death whose name, voice, signature, photograph, or likeness has
> commercial value", and a successor in interest has no cause of action for uses
> occurring before the claim is registered with the Secretary of State.
> Source: New York Civil Rights Law section 50-f, statute text retrieved
> 9 September 2026.

Seventy years against forty, with a registration precondition on one of them.
Other states differ again, and this page names only the two it verified rather
than publishing a span it cannot stand behind.

## Does a digital human have to be labeled in China?

Two separate duties attach, and the first one is about consent rather than
labels.

> Article 14 of those Provisions says that where a deep synthesis service
> provider or technical supporter offers a function that edits biometric
> information such as a face or a voice, it must prompt the user to inform the
> individual being edited in accordance with law and to obtain that individual's
> separate consent.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, Provisions on the Administration
> of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services, Article 14, instrument
> text retrieved 9 September 2026.

Separate consent. Not consent bundled into a general release, which is how most
talent paperwork handles it.

> Article 17 requires a conspicuous label in a reasonable position or area of
> the generated or edited content where a deep synthesis service may cause
> public confusion or misrecognition, and it lists synthetic voice and voice
> imitation, and face generation, face replacement, face manipulation and
> posture manipulation, among the services that trigger it. Article 18 forbids
> any organization or individual from using technical means to delete, alter or
> conceal those labels.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, Provisions on the Administration
> of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services, Articles 17 and 18,
> instrument text retrieved 9 September 2026.

A digital human is squarely inside that list. In the United States a separate
disclosure attaches to the advertisement itself.

> The New York statute requires a covered person to "conspicuously disclose in
> such advertisement that a synthetic performer is in such advertisement, where
> such person has actual knowledge", and sets a civil penalty of 1,000 US
> dollars for a first violation and 5,000 US dollars for each subsequent
> violation.
> Source: New York State Senate, bill text, December 2025.

copyright and AI resource handles the ownership questions upstream of consent,
and the AI excellence page sets out the per-asset record that makes any of this
auditable later.

## Questions about AI ambassador releases

**What goes in an AI avatar talent release?**

Scope, term, territory, permitted uses, retraining rights, derivative rights,
revocation, compensation, sunset and deletion. The one published standard form,
a three-page rider posted in November 2025, collects consent and a description
of permitted uses. It carries no field for the other seven, which live in a
collective agreement rather than on the signed page.

**Do I need a release for a fully synthetic character?**

Not for a person who does not exist. What it needs instead is a record of how it
was made, plus whatever disclosure duty attaches to the advertisement. The
exception matters: prompting a generative system with a real performer's name,
image or recording pulls the character back into consent and payment territory
under the 2025 commercials agreement.

**Can a model be retrained after the campaign ends?**

Not on the strength of the original consent. The 2025 commercials agreement
treats training as a separate permission and bars using the results and proceeds
of services to train generative systems without union consent. Consent to build
one person's replica is not consent to feed the footage into a general model, so
it needs its own clause.

**What happens to a digital human when the contract lapses?**

Under that agreement, retention is bounded by the commercial's maximum period of
use, or by 24 months from consent where no commercial was made, with further
retention in blocks of no more than 24 months. Absent consent to retain, the
producer deletes all copies and certifies in writing within ninety days. Work
already produced stays intact.

**Does a digital human have to be labeled in China?**

The deep synthesis Provisions, in force since 10 January 2023, require a
conspicuous label where the service may cause public confusion, and list face
generation, face replacement, face manipulation and synthetic voice among the
triggers. Article 14 separately requires the individual's separate consent where
a face or voice is edited, not consent bundled into a general release.
