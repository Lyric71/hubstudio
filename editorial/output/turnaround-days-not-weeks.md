# Content production turnaround time, stage by stage

A production calendar runs two clocks at once: the time the work takes, and the time
it waits for a decision. Faster production shortens only the first one.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How long does content production take?

No method-stated benchmark answers that. The windows in circulation are published by
companies selling the work, and each one starts its clock somewhere different. What
can be shown is where the days go: making compresses, waiting for decisions does not.
hubStudio publishes its own windows, and they are quoted below with their pages.

> Across four buyer searches on content production turnaround, 35 results came
> back. Every one was published by a company selling production, editing,
> photography, software, tools or training, or by a practitioner, a content farm
> or an off-topic manufacturer. None came from a trade body, a statistical agency
> or a peer-reviewed journal, and none of the stage timelines read in full stated
> a sample or a method.
> Source: search-results audit run 10 September 2026 across four English buyer
> phrasings, publisher type recorded for every result, three stage-timeline pages
> read in full, no domain named.

So this page does not print an industry number. It prints the stages, what each one
lets you compress, and the arithmetic that decides the rest.

<!-- SECTION: stages -->

## Where do the days go, stage by stage?

The table below is production practice: a description of how an engagement runs,
not a measurement. Where a figure exists for a stage, it is quoted further down with
its source.

| Stage | What compresses | What does not | Who owns the wait |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brief | The length of the document | Agreement on what the brief means | The buyer |
| Proposal | The studio's response time | The buyer's internal budget sign-off | The studio, then the buyer |
| Scope lock | Very little, because it is a decision | Adding a market or format later reopens it | Both sides |
| Capture | Shoot days on standard set-ups | Sample shipping, customs and public holidays | The buyer for samples, the studio for the shoot |
| Model training | Compute time | Clearing the training set, and the depth of the brand library | The studio, working on the buyer's library |
| Generation | The generation call itself | Selecting and finishing what comes back | The studio |
| Review rounds | The revision work between rounds | Waiting for one consolidated set of comments | The buyer's reviewers |
| Retouch | Turnaround, if you pay for a faster tier | The daily volume cap on that tier | The studio or retouch supplier |
| Delivery | Export and formatting | Platform gates and holiday closures | The studio for export, the platform for gates |

Read down the third column and a pattern shows. Almost everything that refuses to
compress is a decision, a queue or a calendar someone else controls.

The first loop usually starts at the brief. The one trade-body release on the subject
measures perception, not days, and it is five years old, but it is consistent.

> In a survey of more than 1,700 marketers and agency staff across more than 70
> countries, 69 percent of marketers and 73 percent of agency respondents agreed
> that rebriefs happen too often. The same survey found 78 percent of marketers
> believed their briefs gave clear strategic direction, against 5 percent of
> creative agency respondents.
> Source: global briefing survey released by the UK advertising agency trade
> body, 13 October 2021; self-reported agreement, fielding window and per-group
> counts not published, and it measures perception, not time.

## Where does a quoted turnaround start?

Before comparing two quoted windows, ask where each clock starts and what it leaves
out. Published schedules tend to start late and stop before your review.

> One Chinese ecommerce photography studio's published process, dated July 2026,
> gives 5 to 7 working days for white-background and scene images and 2 to 4
> weeks for video. Its own step list shows where that clock starts: a shoot of one
> to two days, a day of image selection, two to three days of retouch and a day to
> deliver. Contract signature and one to three days of sample shipping come before
> it, and the two free revisions it offers carry no time at all.
> Source: a Chinese ecommerce photography studio's published eight-step process
> page, dated 20 July 2026, read in Chinese 10 September 2026, one studio's own
> terms; the clock-start reading is arithmetic on its stated steps.

> A Chinese video production company's published schedule runs about 20 to 24
> working days from first client conversation to delivery, including three
> working days to adjust the cut after feedback, and states that its post-production
> estimate does not include the client's review and revisions.
> Source: a Chinese video production company's published schedule, dated 7 August
> 2019, read in Chinese 10 September 2026, one company's terms and seven years
> old; the day total is arithmetic on its six stated stages.

The fastest promises attach to the smallest jobs. Design subscriptions are the
clearest case.

> Turnaround promises in this category are published against small or minor
> requests, not against whole jobs. Cards read in September 2026 publish
> next-day, same-day, 24-hour and 12-hour promises, each qualified by request
> size or tier.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, read
> directly, no vendor named.

And the stages that most often stretch a calendar are the ones nobody prices.

> None of eight published price surfaces read across two markets in September
> 2026 prices sample logistics, reshoots or approval rounds.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, two markets, read directly
> September 2026, absence confirmed by reading each card in full, no vendor named.

Retouch is the exception, because it is the one stage you can buy speed on.

> Retouch is the stage a buyer can speed up by paying. One published image editing
> card read in September 2026 sets a 24-hour default and sells turnaround from 6
> to 96 hours; another guarantees 200 edited images a day with next-morning
> delivery, and quotes a 600-a-day tier from a 3-hour turnaround on custom pricing.
> Source: two published image editing pricing pages in this category, read
> directly 9 and 10 September 2026, turnaround terms as each card states them, no
> vendor named.

## How fast can AI content production turn around?

Generation is fast. Finishing is not, and the published record says so.

> Published documentation for one image generation model states that complex
> prompts "may take up to 2 minutes to process." Two published documentation
> pages for video generation models, read the same day, state no generation time
> at all.
> Source: published model documentation in this category, read 10 September
> 2026, pages undated or last updated June 2026, no vendor named; a stated ceiling
> for complex prompts, not a measured average.

> On one documented AI-assisted short film, a three-person team took about a week
> and a half to two weeks to finish roughly 90 seconds of footage. Its
> post-production lead put generated material to finished footage at about 300 to
> 1, with rotoscoping, artifact removal, retiming, grading and grain still
> required.
> Source: a visual-effects trade publication's interview with the production
> team, April 2024; one production, the practitioner's own estimate, no counting
> log, and a dated model generation.

Two minutes of generation against two weeks of finishing is one production, not a
rule. It does show where the calendar goes once the model is no longer the slow part:
selection, cleanup and the decisions around them.

> Forty percent of in-house agency leads told a global advertiser trade body they
> had seen faster content production from AI. The research reports speed as a
> share of respondents saying so, not as days saved.
> Source: global advertiser trade body research on AI in in-house agencies, 4
> November 2025; a quantitative survey plus 30 in-depth interviews, respondent
> count not published on the page.

## What do review rounds add to the calendar?

More than most schedules admit, and it can be worked out without a benchmark.

> No method-stated benchmark for how long creative approval takes, or for how
> many review rounds a project runs, turned up in searches run in September 2026.
> The one numeric approval benchmark located disclosed no sample size.
> Source: evidence searches run 10 September 2026 in English and Chinese, every
> result classified by publisher type and the one numeric benchmark read in full;
> a scoped observation, not proof that none exists.

In place of a benchmark, use the arithmetic. Review time added to one deliverable
equals the number of rounds multiplied by the wait for consolidated feedback plus the
revision work. The inputs below are round numbers chosen for illustration, not
measurements. Put your own in.

| Scenario | Rounds | Wait per round | Revision per round | Review time added |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Next-day feedback | 3 | 1 day | 1 day | 6 working days |
| Feedback in three days | 3 | 3 days | 1 day | 12 working days |
| Weekly review meeting | 3 | 5 days | 1 day | 18 working days |
| Weekly meeting, one extra round | 4 | 5 days | 1 day | 24 working days |
| Weekly meeting, revision twice as fast | 3 | 5 days | 0.5 day | 16.5 working days |

> Review time grows with the number of rounds multiplied by the wait for
> consolidated feedback plus the revision work. At three rounds and a day of
> revision each, next-day feedback adds six working days, three-day feedback adds
> twelve, and a weekly review meeting adds eighteen. A fourth round at the weekly
> pace takes it to twenty-four, while halving revision time in that case saves a
> day and a half.
> Source: arithmetic with illustrative inputs, not measurements, 10 September
> 2026, shown so a reader can substitute their own.

The last row is the one to show whoever wants a faster studio. Halving the studio's
revision time saves a day and a half. Moving from a weekly meeting to next-day
feedback saves twelve.

Time zones decide how long that wait is before anyone has an opinion.

> China runs on a single time zone, UTC+8. A nine-to-six working day in Shanghai
> overlaps a nine-to-six day in London by about one hour in winter and two in
> summer, in Paris or Berlin by two and three, and in Sydney by about seven
> hours on standard time and six on daylight time. It overlaps Los Angeles by
> one hour in the northern winter and not at all in summer. It never overlaps a
> New York working day.
> Source: published civil time offsets, arithmetic shown so the reader can check
> it, September 2026.

With no overlap, comments sent at the end of a New York day land at the start of the
Shanghai day, which works well for written feedback. A round that needs a live
conversation has to wait for a call booked outside one side's working hours.

## What closes the calendar that nobody on the project controls?

Public holidays, and platform deadlines that publish no lead time.

> China's 2026 holiday schedule stopped work for nine days over the Spring
> Festival, 15 to 23 February, for five days at Labor Day, 1 to 5 May, and for
> seven days at National Day, 1 to 7 October. Weekend days were worked in
> exchange: 14 and 28 February, 9 May, 20 September and 10 October.
> Source: the State Council General Office notice on 2026 holiday arrangements,
> issued 4 November 2025.

National Day falls on 1 to 7 October 2026, so a project briefed into China in late
September loses that week before its first review.

> No platform rule, government notice or survey found in September 2026
> publishes how long creative production should take before a Singles Day or
> 618 deadline. The lead times in circulation come from companies selling
> preparation services, with no sample and no method behind them.
> Source: search of Chinese- and English-language sources, 10 September 2026,
> covering reporting of platform rules and the pages ranking for four buyer
> phrasings. An observation about the published record, not a measurement.

## A turnaround checklist to use on the next brief

Production practice, not a standard. None of these lines carries a figure, and each
one removes waiting rather than work.

- Name one approver per deliverable, and one person who consolidates comments.
- Write the number of rounds into the scope, and define a round as one consolidated
  set of comments, not a thread.
- Book review slots in both calendars before kickoff, and put the feedback turnaround
  in working days into the scope.
- Check the working-hour overlap between your reviewers and the studio, then choose
  written or call-based review.
- Confirm the sample ship date, and whether the samples cross a border.
- Check public holidays in the production country across the whole window.
- Decide at the brief whether a trained model is needed. If it is, schedule it before
  generation.
- Ask where each quoted window starts: brief, signature, sample receipt or shoot.
- Ask whether a quoted schedule includes your own review time.
- Ask the price and the daily volume cap of any faster retouch tier.
- Lock formats and markets before the first review.
- Put a date on final approval, not only on final delivery.

## Questions about production turnaround

**How long does content production take?**

No method-stated benchmark exists in the published record, and quoted windows start
their clocks in different places. As one published example, hubStudio's video
production page gives 24 to 72 hours from brief to first cut on simple social cuts and
one to two weeks on standard studio shoots. Always ask whether a quote includes your
own review time.

**What slows down a creative production project?**

Waiting, more than making. Each review round adds the wait for consolidated feedback
on top of the revision work, so three rounds at a weekly meeting add eighteen working
days in illustrative arithmetic. The brief is the other usual cause: in a 2021 trade
body survey, 69 percent of marketers agreed that rebriefs happen too often.

**How many revision rounds are normal?**

No method-stated norm turned up in searches run in September 2026. hubStudio publishes
three rounds of modification on finalized work, with more on retainer. Whatever the
number, define a round in the scope as one consolidated set of comments, because a
round that arrives as a thread of separate notes behaves like several.

**How fast can AI content production turn around?**

The generation step is quick: one image model's documentation puts complex prompts at
up to two minutes. Finishing is slower. One documented AI-assisted short took a
three-person team a week and a half to two weeks for about 90 seconds of film, with
cleanup, retiming and grading still required.

**How do I shorten approval time?**

Name one approver and one person who consolidates comments, book review slots before
kickoff, write the number of rounds into the scope, and put final approval on the
calendar. On the illustrative arithmetic, moving three rounds from a weekly meeting to
next-day feedback saves twelve working days, far more than faster revisions would.
