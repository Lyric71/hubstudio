# Subscription or managed production: the unit you are buying

Every comparison of these two models argues about features, turnaround
promises and monthly price. None of them starts with the question that decides
the outcome: what does the fee actually buy? Answer that and the rest follows,
including the part nobody publishes.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What is the difference between a design subscription and a production partner?

A subscription sells you access to creative capacity for a period. Production
sells you named assets, delivered. That is the whole distinction, and it
survives every feature table you will read, because it is the thing the feature
tables are built to avoid.

You can see it on the cards themselves. Subscription tiers price hours and
concurrent lanes. Not one of them commits to a number of finished assets. So
when two quotes sit side by side and one looks cheaper, you are usually
comparing a price for capacity against a price for output, which is not a
comparison at all.

<!-- SECTION: The unit -->

## Access or assets: what the fee is attached to

Read any card in this category closely and the priced unit turns out to be a
period of availability. Sometimes that period is described in hours. Sometimes
in how many jobs may be open at once. Never in deliverables.

> At the managed end of the category, the monthly fee buys a block of creative
> hours, published at 120 hours and 180 hours a month on one card read in
> September 2026, and unused hours do not roll over.
> Source: published pricing page in this category, September 2026, read
> directly, single card, no vendor named.

Hours that expire. That is the honest shape of an access product, and it is not
a criticism: gyms work the same way, and so do law firms. It just means the
risk of not using the capacity sits with you, and the risk of the work taking
longer than expected sits with you too.

Production inverts both. The unit is a defined deliverable at a defined
specification, and the risk of it taking longer than expected sits with whoever
quoted it. That is why production quotes come with scope documents and
subscriptions come with tier grids.

| What you buy | How it is priced | Who carries the overrun | What breaks at volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access to designers | Hours a month, or lanes open at once | The buyer | Concurrency, not request count |
| A delivered asset | Per asset against a written spec | The seller | Scoping time on the front end |

Neither column is the right answer. They fail in different places, and the
failure modes are what you are actually choosing between.

<!-- SECTION: The bands -->

## What do the published cards say in 2026?

There are two bands here, not one, and they are an order of magnitude apart
while sharing the same word.

> Unlimited-request design subscriptions in this category publish tiers
> between roughly $700 and $4,500 a month, billed month to month.
> Source: category rate cards collected September 2026, published pricing
> pages read directly, no vendor named.

> Managed design subscriptions in this category publish monthly floors between
> roughly $6,000 and $15,000, with dedicated tiers from roughly $30,000 a
> month on a twelve-month commitment.
> Source: category rate cards collected September 2026, published pricing
> pages read directly, no vendor named.

Same category, same search results, and more than twenty times the price
between the bottom of one band and the top of the other. These are not
competing offers. They are different products, and the cheaper band excludes
work the expensive band assumes.

| Band | Published range, September 2026 | Term | What the fee buys | Denominator published |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unlimited-request | about $700 to $4,500 a month | Month to month | One to three active requests at a time | None |
| Managed | about $6,000 to $15,000 a month | Monthly, or a twelve-month term at the top | 120 to 180 hours, two to three simultaneous projects | Hours |
| Dedicated | from about $30,000 a month | Twelve-month term | A standing team | Headcount, loosely |

Note the last column. In the unlimited band there is no denominator at all: no
hours, no asset counts, nothing you could divide the fee by. You cannot compute
a unit cost even in principle. In the managed band you can compute a unit cost
of access, and only of access.

> Divide the published managed tiers by their published hours and the category
> prices access at almost exactly $50 a creative hour: $5,995 for 120 hours and
> $8,995 for 180 hours.
> Source: arithmetic on one published rate card in this category, collected
> September 2026, no vendor named.

Two tiers on one card, four cents apart on an hourly basis. That is a rate
card wearing a subscription's clothes, and knowing it changes the conversation
you have on the call.

One more thing worth saying about the numbers above: some of the category has
stopped publishing them. At least one card read in September 2026 carries no
price at all and routes the buyer to a call instead. So treat any band here as
the visible part of the market, dated September 2026, not as its full shape.

<!-- SECTION: The queue -->

## Design subscription at volume: where the queue bites

The word "unlimited" refers to how many requests you may file. It has never
referred to how many can be worked at once, and every card says so in the tier
grid, usually one line below the price.

> "Unlimited requests" is published alongside a cap on how many requests can be
> worked at once. Across four published cards read in September 2026, that cap
> runs from one to five active items at a time, rising with the tier.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, tier
> labels read directly off the cards, no vendor named.

Four independent cards, all publishing the same mechanic in different words.
Filing a request and starting a request are separate events, and the gap
between them is where a campaign schedule quietly dies.

Run it against a real shape of work. A launch needing forty finished assets
inside two weeks does not fit into three lanes, and no amount of filing helps.
The constraint is throughput, and throughput is lanes multiplied by how fast a
lane clears, neither of which is the number on the pricing page.

Turnaround promises do not rescue this either, because of what they are
attached to.

> Turnaround promises in this category are published against small or minor
> requests, not against whole jobs. Cards read in September 2026 publish
> next-day, same-day, 24-hour and 12-hour promises, each qualified by request
> size or tier.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, read
> directly, no vendor named.

A twelve-hour promise on a minor request is a real promise about resizes. It is
not a promise about a brand film, and the cards are honest about that in a way
the comparison articles ranking above them are not.

<!-- SECTION: The gap -->

## The mid-market gap, shown rather than asserted

Take a brand spending somewhere between $3,000 and $12,000 a month on
production. That is a common enough shape: a growing consumer brand, one or two
markets, real campaign work but no in-house studio. Here is what the published
floors do to it.

The highest published unlimited tier is about $4,500 a month. The lowest
published managed tier is $5,995 a month. Between those two figures, a window
roughly $1,500 wide, no card checked in September 2026 publishes a tier at all.

Above that hole, the arithmetic gets worse rather than better. At $8,995 a
month the brand buys 180 hours. Spread over about 22 working days that is
roughly 8.2 hours a day, which is one full-time creative plus direction, in
three concurrent lanes, with the unused remainder expiring monthly. It is a
defensible purchase. It is also nowhere near a campaign capability.

And the next step up is not a step.

> The top published managed floor commits the buyer to $192,000 for the year:
> a $15,000 monthly minimum plus a $1,000 monthly software fee, on a
> twelve-month term.
> Source: arithmetic on a published rate card in this category, collected
> September 2026, no vendor named.

A brand budgeting $12,000 a month is $4,000 a month below that floor and
cannot sign a twelve-month term at a number it does not have. So the published
category offers it a product that cannot carry a campaign, or a product it
cannot afford, and nothing between the two.

That gap is the reason this page exists. Nobody is overcharging here. The
category prices capacity in blocks, and blocks do not subdivide, so a buyer
whose need falls between two blocks has nowhere to land.

<!-- SECTION: Exclusions -->

## What the fee does not cover

This is the part that most often surprises a buyer three weeks in, and it is
published in plain sight.

> None of the published subscription cards read in this category in September
> 2026 lists photography, filming, talent booking or rights clearance as an
> included service. The published scopes cover design, illustration, motion
> graphics and video editing, with stock supplied under license rather than
> originated.
> Source: four published pricing pages in this category, September 2026, each
> included-service list read directly and checked for capture, talent and
> clearance, no vendor named.

Read that carefully, because the honest version matters. It does not say
subscriptions never involve capture. It says that across four cards read on one
day, capture, talent and clearance appear on none of the included-service
lists. If your work needs a photograph that does not exist yet, the fee you are
comparing does not contain the making of it.

Exclusions also run inside the tiers, not only across the models.

> Published tier grids in this category exclude named categories of work from
> the entry tier, including short-form video and motion, animation, long-form
> video editing, front-end development and multi-brand rollout support.
> Source: published tier comparison grid in this category, September 2026,
> read directly, single card, no vendor named.

Short-form video excluded from an entry tier, in a year when short-form video
is most of what brands actually need, is worth checking before signing rather
than after.

<!-- SECTION: When a subscription is right -->

## When is a subscription genuinely the right buy?

Often. This page is not an argument against the model, and pretending
otherwise would be easy to see through.

A subscription fits when the work is a steady stream of small, well-specified
jobs against assets that already exist. Resizes across formats. Sales decks.
Social posts drawn from an existing library. Landing page variants. Work where
the brief is short, the master asset is already made, and nobody needs to book
a studio.

It fits especially well when demand is flat rather than spiky, because you are
renting a lane and a flat month uses the lane efficiently. It fits badly when
demand arrives in campaign shapes, because campaigns are spikes by definition
and a lane does not widen for them.

The clearest signal is in your own brief history. If most of your requests
begin with a file that already exists, the subscription model is aimed at you.
If most of them begin with something that has to be photographed, filmed, cast
or cleared, it is not, and the price comparison you are running is measuring
the wrong thing.

## The check to run before you sign either one

This takes about twenty minutes with a spreadsheet of last quarter's requests,
and it settles the question better than any comparison article, this one
included.

**Count the origination share.** Go through last quarter's briefs and mark each
one as either starting from an existing asset or requiring something new to be
made. The ratio is your answer. High existing-asset share points to
subscription. High origination share points to production, and the subscription
price is not comparable.

**Find your worst concurrency week.** Not your average month. Look for the week
where the most separate jobs needed to be live at once, and count them. Compare
that number to the published lane cap on the tier you are considering. If your
worst week exceeds the lanes, the model will fail in exactly that week, which
is the week that matters.

**Compute a per-asset figure both ways.** Take a real month. Divide the
subscription fee by the assets you actually received that month, and do the
same with a production quote for the same list. Neither vendor will give you
this number, because neither prices in it. You have to build it yourself, and
it is usually the first honest comparison anyone in the room has seen.

**Read the exclusions before the inclusions.** Tier grids sell on what is
included. The line that will cost you is on the excluded side, and it is
usually video, motion or rollout.

**Price the expiry.** If hours do not roll over, a quiet month is a full-price
month. Multiply your quietest month by the fee and decide whether that is
acceptable, because it will happen at least twice a year.

| Your situation | Points to | The question to settle first |
|---|---|---|
| Steady flow, small jobs, assets already exist | Subscription | Does your worst concurrency week fit the lane cap |
| Spiky campaign work, deadlines fixed | Managed production | Is scope written tightly enough to quote per asset |
| Needs capture, talent or clearance | Production | Who holds the rights record for each input |
| Under $5,000 a month with campaign needs | Neither band cleanly | Can the work be split into a made-once master plus versioning |
| Above $15,000 a month, twelve-month horizon | Either, on evidence | Ask both for a per-delivered-asset figure and see who can answer |

The pricing page sets out how hubStudio scopes work against a written spec,
the brands solutions page covers the campaign shape this comparison keeps
running into, and the production cost estimator will take your volume and formats

## How this page was built

Every band and mechanic above was read directly off a live published pricing
page on 9 September 2026, one card at a time. No aggregator, no directory, no
roundup, no vendor's summary of anybody else's prices. Bands built from two or
more independent cards are given as ranges. Anything drawn from a single card
says so in its source line.

One absence is worth stating, because it shaped the page. Across four query
phrasings, every page-one analysis of these two models is published by a
company that sells one of them. There is no independent, method-stated study of
the design subscription model to cite, which is why this page cites published
prices and published terms instead of anybody's conclusions, including its own
category's.

Prices in this category change without notice, and at least one card has
already moved to quote-only. The collection date sits on every figure for that
reason. Reviewed September 2026.

## Questions buyers ask about subscriptions and production

**What is the difference between a design subscription and a production partner?**

A subscription prices access to creative capacity for a period, published as
hours a month or as how many jobs may be open at once. Production prices named
deliverables against a written specification. The practical consequence is who
carries the overrun: with access, the buyer does, and with a delivered asset,
the seller does.

**What am I actually buying with a design subscription?**

Lanes and time. Published cards in September 2026 price 120 to 180 hours a
month at the managed end, and one to five concurrent active requests at the
unlimited end. No card checked commits to a number of finished assets, so the
fee attaches to availability rather than to output.

**Do design subscriptions work at high volume?**

They work at high volume of small jobs and fail at high concurrency. Published
tiers cap active requests at one to five at a time, so a launch needing forty
finished assets in two weeks does not fit regardless of how many requests you
are allowed to file. Check your worst concurrency week, not your monthly
average.

**When is a subscription the right choice?**

When most briefs start from an asset that already exists and demand is steady
rather than spiky. Resizes, decks, social from an existing library and landing
page variants all suit the model well. Work that needs photography, filming,
casting or rights clearance does not, since none of the cards read in September
2026 lists those as included.

**What do subscription price bands look like in 2026?**

Two bands, an order of magnitude apart. Unlimited-request tiers publish roughly
$700 to $4,500 a month, month to month. Managed tiers publish roughly $6,000 to
$15,000 a month, with dedicated teams from about $30,000 on a twelve-month
term. Between $4,500 and $5,995, no card checked publishes a tier at all.
