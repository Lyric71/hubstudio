# Digital humans in China: build cost, run cost and labeling rules

Published Chinese prices for a brand digital human run from a few hundred RMB to
300,000 RMB and up, because they price different products. Whether any of them
can go on air is decided by the deep synthesis provisions, the AI labeling
measures and the livestream marketing measures.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How much does a digital human cost in China?

Chinese rate cards price a self-serve likeness clone at a few hundred to low
thousands of RMB, a custom 2D presenter captured from a real person at 8,000 to
30,000 RMB, and a photoreal 3D character at 20,000 to 100,000 RMB on one 2026
table and 80,000 to 300,000 RMB and up on another. hubStudio prints no rate here.

Those figures are not a spread around one price. They belong to different
products, and every card below was published by someone who sells the thing it
prices. The sections on consent and rules describe production practice drawn
from the published text of each instrument. They are not legal advice.

## Why do published digital human prices disagree?

Because one word covers several products. The Chinese term is digital human,
shuziren, and the cards rarely say which product they price.

Start at the cheap end.

> Self-serve digital human tools in China price a likeness clone of a real
> person in the hundreds to low thousands of RMB: one seller's own card lists
> 398 RMB a likeness and 9.9 RMB a voice, and single-use cards relayed in
> November 2025 list 1,680 RMB for a likeness and 75 to 599 RMB for a voice.
> The same tools ask for about 20 minutes of single-person footage to build the
> clone.
> Source: published Chinese digital human rate cards, one undated and one
> relayed November 2025, collected 10 September 2026, prices as each card
> states them, no vendor named.

That is a self-serve product. The buyer uploads footage and gets an avatar that
talks to a script.

A bespoke build sits on a different table, with a schedule attached.

> A Chinese price table published in April 2026 puts a template presenter at 0
> to 5,000 RMB over one to three days, a custom 2D presenter captured from a
> real person at 8,000 to 30,000 RMB over five to ten days, a 2.5D
> semi-photoreal presenter at 30,000 to 80,000 RMB, and a photoreal 3D digital
> human at 80,000 to 300,000 RMB and up over 20 to 45 days.
> Source: a Chinese sourcing intermediary's published price table, April 2026,
> collected 10 September 2026, quoted prices with no sample or method.

> The same April 2026 table prices capabilities on top of the build: real-time
> interaction adds 10,000 to 50,000 RMB, and each additional language 3,000 to
> 10,000 RMB.
> Source: a Chinese sourcing intermediary's published price table, April 2026,
> collected 10 September 2026, quoted prices with no sample or method.

> High-end managed digital human services start around 49,999 RMB on a card
> relayed in November 2025.
> Source: published Chinese digital human rate cards relayed in a seller's
> comparison, November 2025, collected 10 September 2026, no vendor named.

The top of the range is where the cards stop agreeing.

> Published 2026 Chinese prices for a photoreal 3D character do not agree. One
> July 2026 review lists 20,000 to 100,000 RMB a character and 100,000 to
> 1,000,000 RMB for film-grade motion capture; an April 2026 table lists 80,000
> to 300,000 RMB and up for the character alone.
> Source: two Chinese price tables, April and July 2026, one from a sourcing
> intermediary and one from a site affiliated with a seller, collected
> 10 September 2026, neither stating a method.

> Four years earlier the numbers were larger. Practitioners interviewed by a
> Chinese product-management publication in August 2022 put an enterprise
> anime-style virtual human at about 100,000 RMB on average, a high-fidelity
> one at 300,000 to 500,000 RMB, and called 2 to 3 million RMB a reasonable
> ceiling for a photoreal one.
> Source: Chinese industry media feature, August 2022, figures quoted from
> founders and directors of companies selling these builds, no sample or method.

Neither 2026 page explains its gap with the other. The 2022 quotes priced whole
projects, rigging and motion capture and operation included, before four years
of tooling changes. A midpoint of any of these would be a price nobody quoted,
so this page prints each range as published.

Then look at who publishes these numbers at all.

> Across three Chinese buyer searches for digital human prices, 12 of 18 unique
> results were published by a seller of digital humans or a site affiliated with
> one, none was published by a government body, research institute or trade
> association, and none stated a method for its prices.
> Source: search-results audit run 10 September 2026, publisher type recorded
> for every result, no domain named.

That is why every price here is labeled a market claim. It is also why the
quote in your inbox matters more than any figure on this page.

## Replica, custom character or stock avatar: which do you need?

Decide the product before comparing prices. The three options differ in build
cost. They differ more in whose consent sits under the asset, and in what goes
wrong once it is live.

| Option | What it is | Published build price | Consent under it | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Licensed real-person replica | A digital human cloned from a named person's face and voice, often a founder, host or talent | Self-serve clone 398 RMB on one card, 1,680 RMB on another; custom 2D capture 8,000 to 30,000 RMB (April 2026); managed service from about 49,999 RMB (November 2025) | Portrait consent, consent for likeness and voice on a livestream, separate consent for biometric data, and the editing tool's consent prompt | The replica outlives the person's willingness: a license with no clear term can be ended on reasonable notice |
| Custom digital human | A character authored for the brand in 2D, 2.5D or 3D, not cloned from anyone identifiable | 2.5D 30,000 to 80,000 RMB (April 2026); photoreal 3D 20,000 to 100,000 RMB (July 2026) against 80,000 to 300,000 RMB and up (April 2026) | No portrait holder if nobody is identifiable; the voice actor and anyone used as a reference still need to consent | It quietly becomes a replica when a real person's photos are used as reference |
| Generic avatar | A stock presenter from a tool's library | Template 0 to 5,000 RMB (April 2026); tool tiers 28 to 300 RMB a month (July 2026) | The license chain runs through the tool provider's terms, not a person's consent | The same face can front another brand's content the same week |

That table is production practice, not a ranking, and the consent column is not
legal advice. The same national rules on synthesis, labeling and livestreaming
attach to all three rows. Nothing in any instrument read for this page exempts a
stock face.

## What does it cost to run a virtual livestreamer?

Nobody publishes that number in a form that can be checked. Recorded video is a
different matter, because it is priced like software.

A July 2026 Chinese review of digital human software lists pay-per-minute
generation at 0.5 to 7 RMB a minute, individual monthly tiers at 28 to 300 RMB,
enterprise annual plans from 2,980 RMB, one-time software licenses at 980 to
9,980 RMB, and private on-premises deployment from 120,000 RMB. The site that
published it is affiliated with a seller and discloses no testing method.

Volume packages follow the same logic.

> A May 2026 Chinese package card prices digital human video by volume: 999 RMB
> a month for up to 50 videos, 2,999 RMB for up to 200, and 7,999 RMB for an
> unlimited tier the publisher puts at about 4 RMB a video.
> Source: a Chinese seller's published package card, May 2026, collected
> 10 September 2026, per-video figures are the seller's own division.

The per-video figure is the seller dividing its own price. It says nothing about
how many of those videos a brand would approve. The article on the all-in cost
of AI video walks through the lines a generation price leaves out, and the AI
video production page describes the recorded-video side of this work.

Livestreaming is where the published evidence runs out.

> The only published figure found for a livestreaming digital human is a
> reference quotation, not a measured project: 65,000 RMB to build, 30,000 RMB
> of server hardware, and 12,000 RMB a year of maintenance.
> Source: a Chinese sourcing intermediary's published price table, April 2026,

No Chinese-language page read for this article publishes a per-hour, per-month

## What consent does a digital human built from a real person need?

Consent comes in layers, and each layer comes from a different instrument. What
follows is production practice built on the published text, not legal advice.

The base layer is China's Civil Code. Under Articles 1019, 1021 and 1022, in
force since 1 January 2021, a likeness may not be made, used or published
without the portrayed person's consent. A disputed license clause is construed
in favor of the person portrayed. Where the license term is unagreed or unclear,
either party may end it on reasonable notice. That statute text was read through
Chinese legal commentary, not on an official host.

The last of those rules is the replica's quiet risk. A digital human on a
license with no clear term rests on a permission either side can end.

Livestreaming adds a consent rule of its own, set by Article 25 of the Measures
for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing (Trial), and it
reaches voice.

> Article 25 says that where a livestream room operator or livestream marketer
> uses another person's likeness as a virtual image for livestream marketing,
> it must obtain the portrait right holder's consent and must not infringe that
> right by forging it through information technology, and that a natural
> person's voice is protected the same way.
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing
> (Trial), Article 25, April 2021; full text as reproduced on a provincial
> government portal, read 10 September 2026, matched by two secondary readings.

The capture session itself can produce personal data of the most protected
kind.

> China's Personal Information Protection Law lists biometric identification
> among the categories of sensitive personal information, and requires the
> individual's separate consent to process sensitive personal information.
> Source: Personal Information Protection Law of the People's Republic of China,
> Articles 28 and 29, in force 1 November 2021, statute text read on the
> Cyberspace Administration of China's site, 10 September 2026.

The law lists biometric identification without defining it. This page does not
say every face scan qualifies. It says the question gets asked before capture,
not after.

The editing tools are addressed too, under the Provisions on the Administration
of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services.

> Article 14 of those Provisions says that where a deep synthesis service
> provider or technical supporter offers a function that edits biometric
> information such as a face or a voice, it must prompt the user to inform the
> individual being edited in accordance with law and to obtain that individual's
> separate consent.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, Provisions on the Administration
> of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services, Article 14, instrument
> text retrieved 9 September 2026.

Note whom that article addresses: the provider or technical supporter, which
prompts the user. It does not say which of a brand, an agency or a vendor
collects the consent. That is generally treated as a matter for the contract.

| Link in the chain | What it covers | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Portrait consent | Making, using or publishing the likeness | Civil Code, Article 1019 |
| Disputed clauses | Read in favor of the person portrayed | Civil Code, Article 1021 |
| Term | A license with no clear term can be ended on reasonable notice | Civil Code, Article 1022 |
| Likeness and voice on a livestream | The portrait right holder's consent, with voice protected the same way | Livestream marketing measures, Article 25 |
| Biometric data | Separate consent to process sensitive personal information | Personal Information Protection Law, Articles 28 and 29 |
| Face and voice editing | The provider prompts the user to inform the person and obtain separate consent | Deep synthesis provisions, Article 14 |
| Uses, retention and deletion | What the replica may do, how long files and models are kept, when they are deleted | The contract, as production practice |

The last row is where most of the paperwork lives. The clause map in the AI
brand ambassadors article sets out that contract layer, including what happens
when the campaign ends and the model persists.

## Is a digital human an advertising endorser in China?

No instrument read for this page answers that. The question is open.

> China's Advertising Law defines an advertising endorser as a natural person,
> legal person or other organization, other than the advertiser, that recommends
> or certifies goods or services in its own name or image, and says an endorser
> may not recommend or certify goods it has not used or services it has not
> received.
> Source: Advertising Law of the People's Republic of China, Articles 2 and 38,
> as amended 29 April 2021. Statutory text.

Whether a digital human, or the person it was cloned from, fits that definition
is not settled by anything read here.

The Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising, in force since 1 May
2023, address livestream roles directly.

> The same Measures put advertiser duties on a seller who promotes by
> livestream, and agency, publisher or endorser duties on the room operator
> and the on-camera talent depending on their role.
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising, Article 19,
> in force 1 May 2023. Statutory text.

No article of those advertising measures mentions a virtual or AI presenter. A
brand putting product claims in a digital human's mouth is working in that gap,
and should know it.

## Which rules apply to a digital human in China?

National instruments name digital persons and AI-synthesized virtual hosts in
so many words. No national rule read for this page is written only for digital
human livestreams. The working set is below, with CAC standing for the
Cyberspace Administration of China.

| Instrument | Articles that bear on a digital human | Issuing body | Effective date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provisions on the Administration of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services | 14, 17, 18, 23 | CAC, Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, Ministry of Public Security | 10 January 2023 |
| Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content | 3, 4, 10 | CAC, Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, Ministry of Public Security, National Radio and Television Administration | 1 September 2025 |
| GB 45438-2025, labeling method standard | Whole standard | National standards register, State Administration for Market Regulation | 1 September 2025 |
| Measures for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing (Trial) | 13, 18, 25 | CAC and six other national bodies | 25 May 2021 |
| Code of Conduct for Online Hosts | 1, 14 | National Radio and Television Administration, Ministry of Culture and Tourism | Notice dated 8 June 2022; no effective date stated |
| Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising | 19 | State Administration for Market Regulation | 1 May 2023 |
| Advertising Law | 2, 38 | National statute | As amended 29 April 2021 |
| Personal Information Protection Law | 28, 29 | National statute | 1 November 2021 |
| Civil Code | 1019, 1021, 1022 | National statute | 1 January 2021 |

Start with the definition, because it is the one place a national instrument
names the product.

> The Provisions on the Administration of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information
> Services define deep synthesis technology to include three-dimensional
> reconstruction, digital simulation and similar technologies that generate or
> edit digital persons and virtual scenes.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, Provisions on the Administration
> of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services, Article 23, instrument
> text read 10 September 2026.

> The Provisions on the Administration of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information
> Services were issued jointly by the Cyberspace Administration of China, the
> Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and the Ministry of Public
> Security, released 11 December 2022 and in force since 10 January 2023.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, December 2022, regulator's release
> of the instrument.

> Article 17 requires a conspicuous label in a reasonable position or area of
> the generated or edited content where a deep synthesis service may cause
> public confusion or misrecognition, and it lists synthetic voice and voice
> imitation, and face generation, face replacement, face manipulation and
> posture manipulation, among the services that trigger it. Article 18 forbids
> any organization or individual from using technical means to delete, alter or
> conceal those labels.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, Provisions on the Administration
> of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services, Articles 17 and 18,
> instrument text retrieved 9 September 2026.

On the decision table, Article 17 bears most directly on the replica row. It
reaches a custom character wherever that character may cause confusion.

## Does a digital human need an AI label in China?

The labeling measures cover every kind of content a digital human produces.
They never name the product.

> The Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content were issued
> jointly by the Cyberspace Administration of China, the Ministry of Industry
> and Information Technology, the Ministry of Public Security and the National
> Radio and Television Administration, released 14 March 2025 and in force
> since 1 September 2025.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025.

> The Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content cover text,
> images, audio, video, virtual scenes and other information generated or
> synthesized with artificial intelligence. Video carries its visible label on
> the opening frame and around playback, audio a spoken or rhythm cue, and a
> virtual scene a label on the opening frame. The instrument never uses the
> words digital human, virtual human or livestream.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text read
> in full on 10 September 2026, Articles 3 and 4.

Two more articles follow the file after it leaves the tool.

> Article 4 of the Measures says that when a service provider offers download,
> copy or export functions for generated synthetic content, it must ensure the
> file contains a conforming explicit label.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

> Article 10 of the Measures says a user publishing generated synthetic content
> through a distribution service must proactively declare it and use the
> labeling function the provider supplies, and that no organization or
> individual may maliciously delete, alter, forge or conceal a label, or supply
> tools or services for others to do so.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

> The mandatory national standard GB 45438-2025, Cybersecurity technology,
> Labeling method for content generated by artificial intelligence, was
> published 28 February 2025 and took effect 1 September 2025, the same day as
> the Measures.
> Source: national standards register, State Administration for Market
> Regulation, February 2025.

Those articles address service providers and users. Between a brand, an agency
and a vendor, who applies the label is generally treated as a matter for the
contract, so write it into one. The China AI labeling rules article follows the
label through a production pipeline, including where it tends to fall off.

## What do China's livestream rules say about virtual hosts?

They anticipate them, and they put the first obligation on the platform.

> The Measures for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing (Trial)
> were issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China, the Ministry of Public
> Security, the Ministry of Commerce, the Ministry of Culture and Tourism, the
> State Taxation Administration, the State Administration for Market Regulation
> and the National Radio and Television Administration, and took effect on
> 25 May 2021.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, release of the Measures, April
> 2021.

> Article 13 of those Measures says a livestream marketing platform must carry
> out a security assessment under the relevant rules, and label the content
> conspicuously, where a virtual image displayed through artificial
> intelligence, digital vision, virtual reality, speech synthesis or similar
> technology is used for livestream marketing.
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing
> (Trial), Article 13, April 2021; full text as reproduced on two government
> legal portals, read 10 September 2026, the two texts identical.

Article 13 names the platform. It refers to the relevant rules without naming
them, so this page cannot say what the security assessment consists of.

What the host says on air is covered too.

> The same Measures bar livestream marketers from publishing false or misleading
> information that deceives or misleads users, and from faking transaction,
> follower, view or like data.
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Online Live Streaming Marketing
> (Trial), Article 18, April 2021; full text as reproduced on two government
> legal portals, read 10 September 2026.

> The Code of Conduct for Online Hosts, issued by the National Radio and
> Television Administration and the Ministry of Culture and Tourism in a notice
> dated 8 June 2022, says in its first article that virtual hosts synthesized
> with artificial intelligence technology, and their content, follow the code by
> reference.
> Source: National Radio and Television Administration, notice publishing the
> Code of Conduct for Online Hosts, document No. 36 of 2022, published 22 June
> 2022, instrument text read 10 September 2026.

> Among the conduct the code prohibits are exaggerated promotion that misleads
> consumers, false promises, absolute terms, and selling counterfeit or
> infringing goods while faking traffic data.
> Source: National Radio and Television Administration and Ministry of Culture
> and Tourism, Code of Conduct for Online Hosts, Article 14, June 2022,
> instrument text read 10 September 2026.

Read together, a virtual host follows the same code a human host does. That
includes the lines on false promises and absolute terms.

Platform rules are a separate layer, and this page cannot read them. Douyin's
safety and trust center and its ecommerce learning center served no rule text
to an unauthenticated fetch on 10 September 2026. Nothing here says what
Douyin's current terms for a digital human host are. Check them inside the
account before a stream is booked. The Douyin platform page covers producing
for the platform more broadly.

## What are Chinese regulators acting on?

Impersonation, above all. Two 2026 notices from the Cyberspace Administration of
China name it.

> The Cyberspace Administration of China's April 2026 campaign against AI
> application abuses lists among its first-phase targets AI face-swap and
> voice-imitation services offered without the person's authorization, and
> stealing another person's likeness to livestream or create content through
> digital virtual human technology.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, campaign notice, 30 April 2026.

> The same regulator's February 2026 action on unlabeled AI content describes AI
> face swapping and voice cloning used to impersonate athletes, actors, hosts,
> entrepreneurs and other public figures.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 12 February 2026.

> Cyberspace authorities reported in February 2026 that they had handled 13,421
> accounts and cleared more than 543,000 items of unlawful or non-compliant
> information over AI-generated content published without an AI label. The
> release is a campaign report and does not state the period covered or a
> sample.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 12 February 2026.

Those totals cover unlabeled AI content in general, not digital humans alone.
The April notice is the sharper signal. A replica built from someone's likeness
without their authorization is a named target, which puts the consent chain
ahead of the build quote in any plan.

## Questions buyers ask about digital humans in China

**How much does a digital human cost in China?**

Published Chinese rate cards list a self-serve likeness clone at 398 RMB on one
undated card, a custom 2D capture at 8,000 to 30,000 RMB, and a photoreal 3D
character at 20,000 to 100,000 RMB or 80,000 to 300,000 RMB and up, depending on
the 2026 table. Those are different products. No card states a method, so treat
each figure as a dated market claim.

**What does it cost to run a virtual livestreamer?**

No Chinese-language page read for this article published a per-hour, per-month
or per-account license price for a livestreaming digital human, or a staffing
cost with a method. The only published figure found is an April 2026 reference
quotation: 65,000 RMB to build, 30,000 RMB of server hardware and 12,000 RMB a
year of maintenance. Get the license, staffing and hours quoted separately.

**Do digital humans in China need an AI label?**

The Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content cover video, audio
and virtual scenes, though the text never says digital human or livestream. The
livestream marketing measures separately tell platforms to label virtual images
used for livestream marketing conspicuously. Between brand, agency and vendor,
who applies the label is generally treated as a matter for the contract. This is
production practice, not legal advice.

**Can a digital human host a Douyin livestream?**

National rules anticipate virtual hosts. The livestream marketing measures
address virtual images used for livestream marketing, with a platform security
assessment and conspicuous labeling, and the Code of Conduct for Online Hosts
covers AI-synthesized virtual hosts by reference. Douyin's own rule pages served
no readable rule text to an unauthenticated fetch on 10 September 2026, so
confirm the platform's current terms inside your account first.

**Do I need consent to build a digital human from a real person?**

Yes, at several layers. The Civil Code bars using a likeness without consent.
The livestream marketing measures require the portrait right holder's consent
when another person's likeness becomes a virtual image, and protect voice the
same way. Biometric identification is sensitive personal information needing
separate consent, and providers of face and voice editing must prompt users to
obtain it. This is production practice, not legal advice.

**Is a custom digital human cheaper than a celebrity?**

No published celebrity replica license fee with a method was found, so no
number answers this. The structure does. A celebrity replica is still a licensed
real-person replica. The build is one line; the likeness license, the consent
paperwork and the risk that a license with no clear term ends on reasonable
notice are the others. A character authored from no identifiable person has no
portrait holder to pay.
