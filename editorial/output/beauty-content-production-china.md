# Beauty content production in China: the category playbook

Beauty is the most heavily regulated thing a studio can shoot in China, and
almost none of that regulation is written about the shoot. It is written about
the claim. Get the claim wrong and the frame it sits in becomes the evidence.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What does beauty content in China have to do differently?

Three things. Every efficacy claim has to match what the product is registered
or filed to say, with published evidence behind it. Medical wording is banned
outright, on the label and in the ad. Any depiction of a result has to be
true, which is where retouching, before-and-after frames and generated skin
stop being craft decisions and start being compliance ones. hubStudio produces
beauty campaigns in China against these rules.

This page describes production practice, not legal advice. Every instrument is
cited so you can take it to a lawyer, which is what you should do before you
sign anything that turns on it.

Reviewed 18 September 2026.

One thing to hold onto before the detail: the rules below sit at four
different levels. There is national statute, there is national regulator
instrument, there are local guidelines that bind in one city and persuade
everywhere else, and there are platform rules that are stricter than any of
them. Flattening that into "China bans it" is the mistake that costs a reshoot,
because roughly half the strictest lines on this page are local or
platform-level, and the other half are not.

## What claims can a cosmetic make in China?

Start with the split that governs everything downstream. China sorts cosmetics
by risk, and the riskier half has to be registered before it can make its
claim at all.

> Products for hair dyeing, perming, spot removal and whitening, sun
> protection and hair-loss prevention, and any product claiming a new
> efficacy, are special cosmetics, teshu huazhuangpin, which are registered
> with the drug regulator. All other cosmetics are filed.
> Source: Cosmetics Supervision and Administration Regulation, State Council
> Order No. 727, Articles 4 and 16, in force 1 January 2021. Statutory text
> read on two government surfaces, September 2026.

A general cosmetic that talks like a special one is not a copy problem. It is
a registration problem that the copy exposed.

> China's Cosmetics Supervision and Administration Regulation bars cosmetics
> advertising from stating or implying a medical effect, and bars false or
> misleading content that deceives or misleads consumers.
> Source: Cosmetics Supervision and Administration Regulation, Article 43,
> published 29 June 2020, in force 1 January 2021. Statutory text read on the
> State Council release and a Beijing government portal, September 2026.

The same ban runs through the labeling rules, and the label list is the more
useful one for a production team because it names the mechanisms rather than
the intent.

> China's cosmetics labeling rules, in force since 1 May 2022, bar a label
> from implying a medical effect through medical terms, the names of medical
> figures or drug names, or through trademarks, patterns, font color and size,
> homophones or symbols. They also bar false, exaggerated or absolute wording,
> fabricated or unverifiable research, statistics or surveys, using an
> ingredient's function to imply an efficacy the product lacks or may not
> claim, and any assertion or guarantee of efficacy or safety.
> Source: Cosmetics Labeling Measures, National Medical Products
> Administration Announcement 2021 No. 77, Article 19, read in the State
> Council Gazette, September 2026. The article lists twelve prohibited items;
> this quote carries the ones that bear on content.

Read that list as an art direction brief and it changes what you put on set.
The rule reaches wardrobe and typography, not only copy. A white coat reads as
a medical figure. So does a stethoscope left on the counter. Setting an
ingredient name in a heavier weight than the claim around it is font size
implying efficacy, which is one of the twelve items by name.

| Claim | Status | Instrument and level | Evidence you hold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medical effect, disease treatment, drug or device confusion | Banned | Regulation Art. 43 and Advertising Law Art. 17, national statute | Nothing cures it |
| Medical figures, hospitals or doctors as proof | Banned on labels | Labeling Measures Art. 19, national regulator | Nothing cures it |
| Absolute terms such as "best" or "national level" | Banned | Advertising Law Arts. 9(3) and 57, national statute | Nothing cures it |
| Guarantee of efficacy or safety | Banned on labels | Labeling Measures Art. 19, national regulator | Nothing cures it |
| Efficacy implied through an ingredient's function | Banned on labels | Labeling Measures Art. 19, national regulator | Nothing cures it |
| Spot removal and whitening, sun protection, hair-loss prevention | Needs special registration plus a human efficacy test | Regulation Art. 16 and the efficacy standard | Registration, human test, published summary |
| Acne, nourishing, repair | Needs a human efficacy test | The efficacy standard, national regulator | Human test, published summary |
| Anti-wrinkle, firming, soothing, oil control and other catalog claims | Needs evidence | Regulation Art. 22 and the efficacy standard | Evaluation, published summary |
| Cleansing, makeup removal, makeup, fragrance, physical-only effects | Allowed without a published summary | The efficacy standard, national regulator | Label or formula basis |
| Data, percentages and survey results | Allowed if true and sourced on the asset | Advertising Law Art. 11, national statute | The source, shown |
| Before-and-after or retouched use effects | Fabricated effects are false advertising | Advertising Law Art. 28 plus local guidance | Unedited capture of real use |

Penalties are worth knowing because they scale with repetition rather than
with the size of the claim.

> Outside medical, drug and medical device advertising, no ad in China may
> refer to a disease treatment function or use medical terms, or terms that
> confuse the product with a drug or a medical device. A breach draws a fine
> of one to three times the advertising fee, or 100,000 to 200,000 RMB where
> the fee cannot be calculated or is obviously low, rising to three to five
> times the fee, or 200,000 to 1,000,000 RMB, in serious cases.
> Source: Advertising Law of the People's Republic of China, Articles 17 and
> 58, as amended 29 April 2021. Statutory text read in full on the Ministry of
> Commerce law database, September 2026.

## Which efficacy claims need an evaluation report?

The published-summary duty is the part foreign teams miss, because it has no
equivalent in most markets. Holding the evidence is not enough. It has to be
published where anyone who wants to check it can find it.

> Every efficacy claim needs a sufficient scientific basis, and the company
> that registers or files the product must publish a summary of the
> literature, research data or evaluation results behind the claim on a
> website the national drug regulator designates, for public scrutiny.
> Source: Cosmetics Supervision and Administration Regulation, Article 22, in
> force 1 January 2021. Statutory text read on two government surfaces,
> September 2026.

> Failing to publish that summary draws a fine of 10,000 to 30,000 RMB, rising
> to 30,000 to 50,000 RMB with suspension of production or operations in
> serious cases. A cosmetics ad that breaks the Regulation is penalized under
> the Advertising Law.
> Source: Cosmetics Supervision and Administration Regulation, Articles 62 and
> 69, in force 1 January 2021. Statutory text read on a Beijing government
> portal on two separate occasions, September 2026.

Which claims need which evidence comes from a separate instrument, and the
grade of evidence steps up sharply for six of them.

> China's rules for evaluating cosmetics efficacy claims, in force since 1 May
> 2021, require a human efficacy test for six claims: spot removal and
> whitening, sun protection, hair-loss prevention, acne, nourishing and
> repair. Other claims may rest on evaluation tests, literature or research
> data analysis, as the claim requires.
> Source: Standard for Evaluating Cosmetics Efficacy Claims, National Medical
> Products Administration Announcement 2021 No. 50, published April 2021. The
> regulator's own page refused automated requests in September 2026, so the
> text was read on two independent regulatory reference surfaces that agree.

> Claims a consumer can recognize directly by sight or smell, such as
> cleansing, makeup removal, makeup and fragrance, and effects that work
> purely physically, such as covering, are exempt from publishing an efficacy
> summary.
> Source: Standard for Evaluating Cosmetics Efficacy Claims, National Medical
> Products Administration Announcement 2021 No. 50, read on two independent
> regulatory reference surfaces, September 2026.

A note on how that last pair is sourced, because it bears on how much weight
to put on it. The regulator's own site returned an HTTP 412 to every automated
request on two separate attempts eight days apart, so the standard is cited
here by name and announcement number and never by article number. Anything
turning on a specific article of that standard should be read from the
instrument itself before you build to it.

> China's cosmetics classification catalog sorts efficacy claims into 26
> categories, plus a separate category for any new efficacy that fits none of
> them.
> Source: Cosmetics Classification Rules and Catalog, National Medical
> Products Administration Announcement 2021 No. 49. Read through a legal
> portal's reproduction of the catalog, dated October 2022, and matched
> against two regulatory reference pages, September 2026.

The practical consequence for a shoot: the script and the supers get locked
against the filed claim set before the call sheet goes out, not after the
edit. Post cannot fix a claim that was never filed.

## Can beauty ads in China show before and after?

This is the question the English-language pages either skip or answer wrongly,
usually by asserting a flat national ban that no instrument contains. The
honest answer has layers, and the layers are the useful part.

At national level there is no cosmetics-specific rule naming before-and-after
imagery. There is something broader.

> Fabricating the effect of using a product is one of the forms of false
> advertising China's Advertising Law names.
> Source: Advertising Law of the People's Republic of China, Article 28, as
> amended 29 April 2021. Statutory text read on the Ministry of Commerce law
> database, September 2026.

The most precise language anywhere sits in a local guideline, and it opens by
granting something rather than forbidding it. That opening sentence is the
single most useful line in this whole area for a production team.

> Shanghai's cosmetics advertising guideline opens its rule on showing product
> effects by allowing moderate artistic rendering of a cosmetic's efficacy.
> The same article then requires any depiction of use effects to be true, bars
> misleading consumers through excessive retouching, editing, software
> processing or special shooting techniques, and bars fabricated or edited
> before-and-after images or video.
> Source: Shanghai Cosmetics Industry Advertising Compliance Guideline,
> Article 28, issued 28 February 2024 by the Shanghai Administration for
> Market Regulation and the Shanghai Medical Products Administration, read on
> the Shanghai government host of the text, September 2026. A local guideline,
> not a national rule.

Moderate artistic rendering is allowed. Excessive processing that misleads is
not. Fabricated or edited before-and-after footage is not. No instrument
defines where moderate ends, so the working line a studio can actually hold is
procedural rather than aesthetic: the result on screen has to be a result the
product produced, and you should be able to show the unedited capture it came
from.

Beijing's guideline, issued more recently, does not address the question at
all. Three separate reads of the city's text and its regulator's own
interpretation confirmed the absence. That leaves the Shanghai wording short of
a national consensus, and treating it as one overstates what has actually been
written down.

Enforcement, meanwhile, is real and small and instructive.

> In February 2025 a regional market regulator published two cases against
> cosmetics sellers over before-and-after images. One advertised a hair
> product on a messaging platform with before-and-after pictures and the line
> "many white hairs before use, none after," and was fined 6,000 RMB for false
> advertising. The other posted on a short-video platform a face with obvious
> spots before a whitening mask and none after, alongside skin-improvement
> percentages with no stated source, and was fined 5,000 RMB.
> Source: Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Administration for Market
> Regulation, ten typical illegal advertising cases, 21 February 2025. The
> regulator's own case bulletin, citing Advertising Law Articles 11 and 28 and
> Cosmetics Regulation Article 43. Two small regional cases, not a measure of
> national enforcement.

Note what the second case was actually punished for. Not the image alone: the
image plus three percentages with nothing behind them. Those two failures
travel together in almost every beauty asset that gets pulled, and the
percentages are the easier one to fix.

One neighboring regime is stricter and frequently gets confused with this one.

> China's market regulator targets medical aesthetics ads that create
> appearance anxiety, use patients' names or images for before-and-after
> treatment comparisons, or use endorsers, and it counts "recommendation
> officers" and "experience officers" as endorsers. The same guidelines target
> cosmetics that promote disease treatment functions tied to medical
> aesthetics.
> Source: State Administration for Market Regulation, Enforcement Guidelines
> on Medical Aesthetics Advertising, Announcement 2021 No. 37, 1 November
> 2021, read on two government-hosted copies, September 2026.

If the brand sits next to a clinic, as post-procedure skincare does, the
medical aesthetics guideline is the one that bites, and patient
before-and-after is out.

## What do the rules say about models and endorsers?

No instrument read addresses models as such. Three separate rules reach them
anyway, and together they decide what a beauty casting and a beauty release
have to cover.

> An endorser in China may not recommend or vouch for a product they have not
> used, and no child under ten may be an endorser. An endorser in breach
> forfeits the illegal income and is fined one to two times that amount.
> Source: Advertising Law of the People's Republic of China, Articles 38 and
> 61, as amended 29 April 2021. Statutory text read on the Ministry of
> Commerce law database, September 2026.

> Beijing's market and medical products regulators require cosmetics ad claims
> to match the product's registration or filing and its published efficacy
> summary. Their guideline bars ads from creating appearance anxiety or using
> medical terms, bars general cosmetics from claiming the efficacy of special
> cosmetics, and says endorsers must be at least ten and may only recommend
> cosmetics they have used.
> Source: Beijing Cosmetics Advertising Guideline, issued 27 June 2025 by the
> Beijing Administration for Market Regulation and the Beijing Medical
> Products Administration, read on the city's government portal and the
> regulator's own interpretation, September 2026. A local guideline, not a
> national rule.

Then the likeness itself. Generated and edited faces change the paperwork
here.

> Under China's Civil Code, a likeness may not be made, used or published
> without the portrayed person's consent; where a license clause is disputed
> it is construed in favor of the person portrayed; and where the license term
> is unagreed or unclear either party may end it on reasonable notice.
> Source: Civil Code of the People's Republic of China, Articles 1019, 1021
> and 1022, in force 1 January 2021. Statutory text.

> Under China's deep synthesis provisions, in force since 10 January 2023, a
> provider offering face or voice editing must prompt the user to inform the
> person being edited and obtain that person's separate consent.
> Source: the Provisions on the Administration of Deep Synthesis Internet
> Information Services, Article 14, issued by the Cyberspace Administration of
> China, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and the Ministry
> of Public Security, read on the regulator's site, September 2026.

Separate consent is the operative phrase. A model release that covers the
shoot does not automatically cover editing that model's face afterward, and on
a beauty job face editing is not an edge case, it is the work.

## Who has to mark a beauty post as an advertisement?

Seeding content, zhongcao, is the backbone of beauty in China, and it is
advertising when it carries a way to buy.

> Under China's Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising, in
> force 1 May 2023, an advertisement must be identifiable as one, and any post
> that promotes goods through knowledge introductions, experience sharing or
> consumer reviews while carrying a purchase link must be conspicuously marked
> "advertisement."
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising, Article 9,
> State Administration for Market Regulation Order No. 72, issued 25 February
> 2023, third paragraph read verbatim, September 2026.

> The same Measures put advertiser duties on a seller who promotes by
> livestream, and advertising operator or publisher duties on the room
> operator providing design, production, agency or publishing services.
> Source: Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising, Article 19,
> in force 1 May 2023. Statutory text read verbatim, September 2026.

> Shanghai's cosmetics advertising guideline applies those internet
> advertising rules to seeding posts, reviews and store-visit content used to
> advertise cosmetics on internet platforms.
> Source: Shanghai Cosmetics Industry Advertising Compliance Guideline,
> Article 29, issued 28 February 2024, read on two hosts of the text,
> September 2026. A local guideline, not a national rule.

The production consequence is a deliverable, not a policy. If a creator asset
can carry a purchase link, the disclosure mark belongs in the layout from the
first layout, sized to survive the platform's own crop.

## What gets a beauty asset rejected in platform review?

Platform rules are stricter than the instruments, and they are also the
hardest to read. Here is exactly what was and was not reachable in September
2026, because a page claiming more than this is guessing.

> A beauty industry content review manual on a Chinese creator-marketing
> platform's help center, dated September 2026, bars creator content from
> medical-effect wording, from comparing a product with medical aesthetics
> treatments, from claims such as "suits all skin types," and from wrinkle and
> aging wording. It requires authorization for a celebrity's likeness and
> proof for any data, patent or award a post cites.
> Source: Xingtu marketing service platform help center, beauty industry
> content review manual, page dated 2 September 2026, read unauthenticated on
> two occasions in September 2026. A primary reading for that page's scope
> only.

That manual carries the sharpest lesson on the page. Anti-wrinkle is a filed
category in the national catalog, something a product can lawfully be
registered to claim, and wrinkle wording is still constrained in creator
content on that surface. Clearing the regulator does not clear platform review,
and the two get checked by different people at different times.

> Douyin's ecommerce beauty promotion standards, as revised in May 2022,
> barred medical-effect wording from cosmetics promotion, along with the names
> of medical aesthetics procedures such as whitening injections, skin-booster
> injections and botulinum toxin.
> Source: Chinese ecommerce trade press and a web-services repost reporting
> the platform's revised standards, in force 17 May 2022, read September 2026.
> The platform's rule center was not reached, and the current version may
> differ.

> RedNote's community standards page returned no rule text to an
> unauthenticated request on two dates in September 2026, matching four
> earlier requests to its creator, advertiser, help and school surfaces.
> Source: direct unauthenticated fetches of RedNote's own surfaces, September
> 2026, response content recorded per request. A primary observation, not a
> rule.

So: one platform surface readable and current, one readable only through
four-year-old press, one not readable at all. RedNote's beauty rules were not
reached, which is not the same as saying it has none. Assume they exist and get
them from inside the account before a beauty batch goes up.

On rejection reasons, a caveat worth stating plainly. No platform rejection
notice was obtained for this page, and no source ranks rejection grounds by
frequency. What the table below carries is the grounds the regulator and the
platform manual state, not a count of what actually gets pulled most often.

| Rejection ground | Where it is stated | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Medical-effect wording in copy or supers | Xingtu manual; Douyin 2022 standards | Platform |
| Comparing a cosmetic to a medical aesthetics treatment | Xingtu manual; SAMR guidelines | Platform and national |
| "Suits all skin types" and similar universal claims | Xingtu manual | Platform |
| Wrinkle and aging wording in creator content | Xingtu manual | Platform |
| A percentage or data point with no source on the asset | Advertising Law Art. 11; 2025 case bulletin | National statute |
| A before-and-after frame showing a result the product did not produce | Advertising Law Art. 28; 2025 case bulletin | National statute |
| Celebrity likeness or third-party IP without authorization on file | Xingtu manual; Civil Code | Platform and national |

## Does retouched or generated skin break a platform rule?

One marketplace has written a rule that reaches this directly, and beauty is
the category where it bites hardest, because skin is exactly what a generative
model is best at improving and exactly what the customer will compare against
the mirror.

> A Chinese marketplace published what was reported as the sector's first rule
> aimed specifically at AI fake product images, in force 27 March 2025, naming
> four failure categories: product material or style that does not match the
> real item, distorted or exaggerated product effects, an obvious cutout and
> paste look, and bodies or scenes that break physical laws. It is enforced by
> a detection model that intercepts images at upload, and the platform said a
> notice marking AI synthesis would appear on product detail pages.
> Source: Science and Technology Daily, 28 March 2025, reporting Taobao's own
> rule announcement and its detection-model enforcement.

> Applied to beauty, a retouched or generated skin result that shows more than
> the product delivers falls into that rule's "distorted or exaggerated
> product effects" category. The rule carries no clause about skin; this is a
> reading of its categories.
> Source: a reading of the Taobao AI fake-image rule's published categories,
> September 2026. This describes production practice, not legal advice.

Stack that against Shanghai's Article 28 and the two rules point the same way
from opposite directions. One says an exaggerated effect is a fake image. The
other says excessive processing of a use effect misleads. Neither cares
whether a camera or a model made the pixels.

Then there is the labeling duty, which is separate again and applies whatever
the content shows.

> China's rules on labeling AI-generated content, in force since 1 September
> 2025, call for a visible label on generated images and video, an implicit
> label in the file's metadata, and a visible label that stays on exported or
> downloaded copies.
> Source: the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, issued 14
> March 2025 by the Cyberspace Administration of China and three other
> national bodies, read on the regulator's site, September 2026.

How those duties fall between a brand, its studio and its tool vendor is
generally treated as a matter for the contract rather than settled by the
Measures themselves. The studio-side habit that survives every reading of it:
label at export, keep the metadata, and keep the generation record per asset.
hubStudio's article on China's AI labeling rules walks that pipeline stage by
stage.

## The pre-shoot compliance checklist

Run this before the call sheet goes out, not in the edit. Each line names the
instrument behind it.

- **Claims locked to the filing.** Every on-image and scripted claim mapped to
  the product's registration or filing and its published efficacy summary.
  Regulation Article 22, plus the Beijing and Shanghai guidelines.
- **Special claims on special products only.** Whitening, spot removal, sun
  protection and hair-loss claims appear only on registered special cosmetics.
  Regulation Article 16.
- **No medical vocabulary anywhere, including the set.** Copy, supers, props
  and wardrobe. Advertising Law Article 17, Labeling Measures Article 19, and
  the platform manual.
- **Before-and-after from unedited capture only.** Keep the retouch log. The
  log is practice, not law. Advertising Law Article 28 and Shanghai Article 28.
- **Every percentage carries its source on the asset.** Advertising Law
  Article 11, and the reason one of the two 2025 cases was fined.
- **Endorsers have used the product and are ten or older.** Record the use.
  Advertising Law Article 38.
- **Likeness consent covers China, the platforms and the term.** Civil Code
  Articles 1019, 1021 and 1022, with separate consent for face or voice
  editing under deep synthesis Article 14.
- **Generated or edited skin labeled, and honest.** The explicit and implicit
  labels under the CAC Measures, and never showing more than the product does.
- **Purchase-link posts marked "advertisement."** Livestream roles assigned
  before the room opens. Internet Advertising Measures Articles 9 and 19.
- **Authorizations and proof ready for review.** Celebrity portrait and IP
  authorizations, plus documentation for any data, patent or award. The
  platform manual.

## Questions buyers ask

### What claims can cosmetics make in Chinese advertising?

Claims must match what the product is registered or filed to say, and the
evidence summary behind them has to be published. Medical wording, absolute
terms, guarantees of efficacy and efficacy implied through an ingredient's
function are all banned. Whitening, spot removal, sun protection and
hair-loss claims require registration as special cosmetics first.

### Do skincare efficacy claims need testing in China?

Some do. China's efficacy evaluation standard requires a human efficacy test
for six claims: spot removal and whitening, sun protection, hair-loss
prevention, acne, nourishing and repair. Other claims can rest on evaluation
tests, literature or research data analysis. Sensory claims like cleansing and
fragrance, and purely physical effects, are exempt from publishing a summary.

### Can beauty ads in China show before and after images?

No national cosmetics instrument bans them outright. Fabricating a use effect
is false advertising under the Advertising Law, Shanghai's local guideline
bars fabricated or edited before-and-after footage while allowing moderate
artistic rendering, and a regional regulator fined two sellers over
before-and-after images in February 2025. Treat the Shanghai language as the
working standard.

### What does a beauty brand need for Tmall, Douyin and RedNote?

A main image set with a white-background image, a long detail page, a separate
mobile detail page, a spec chart, store banners and short video, plus vertical
video at 9:16 for Douyin and a 3:4 cover for RedNote. The parts are stable
across every source checked. The pixel values are counted from published
sources, not read from the platforms, so confirm them in your own backend.

### Can retouching or AI skin break Chinese platform rules?

Yes. One marketplace's AI fake-image rule names distorted or exaggerated
product effects as a failure category and enforces it with a detection model
at upload. Shanghai's guideline separately bars misleading consumers through
excessive retouching. Generated content also carries visible and metadata
labeling duties under rules in force since September 2025.
