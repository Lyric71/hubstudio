# Your production roster is consolidating. The 14 questions to ask now

Advertising production is being pulled out of individual agencies and into
centralized units. Your contracts were written against the old shape. Here is
the instrument for finding out what actually changed.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What is a production roster review, and what should it ask?

A production roster review is a structured reassessment of every partner who
makes your advertising assets, run when your supply base changes shape rather
than when a contract happens to expire. Production here means advertising
production: images, films, platform variants. It asks four things. Whether your
contract still binds who you thought it bound, how the commercials work now,
where the bidding conflicts sit, and what leaves with you. hubStudio published
the fourteen questions below so buyers can score partners consistently.

Most of what is written about rosters answers a different question. It tells
procurement how many agencies to keep, or it lists things to ask a video
company you are about to hire for the first time. Both are useful. Neither
helps the marketing director who already has six production partners, has just
been told that three of them now report into the same centralized unit, and
has to decide by the end of the quarter whether that is a problem.

If that is you, start at the four groups and come back for the evidence. The
questions work without it. They are simply harder to insist on.

<!-- SECTION: What actually changed -->

## What changed in production supply, and when

Something structural moved between 2025 and early 2026, and it moved fast
enough that most rosters have not been re-examined since.

> Between 2025 and early 2026 the largest agency groups each folded production
> teams that used to sit inside separate agencies into one centralized
> production unit. Source: trade coverage of the category, February 2026, a
> count of four publicly announced reorganizations, no group named here.

Read that as a supply-side fact and it is unremarkable. Groups reorganize
constantly. Read it as a buyer and it is more interesting, because the
distributed model it replaced was the one your contracts were written against.
Under that model each agency ran its own production capability, and when you
appointed the agency you appointed the studio behind it. The two came
together. Now they can come apart.

The buy side was already moving in the same direction, and it was measured.

> Centralization was named as a returning production model, alongside the rise
> of outsourced teams sitting on the client's own site. Source: global
> advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025, a qualitative
> trend finding across the same 50 brands, with no figure attached.

> Two thirds of the brands surveyed had changed their agency model within the
> previous four years. Source: global advertiser trade body content production
> research, March 2025, same survey of 50 global brands, benchmarked against
> the same study run in 2021.

Two thirds, in four years. Whatever your roster looked like when you signed
it, the odds are it does not look like that now.

And the pot being fought over got smaller while the work got harder.

> Production accounted for an average of 19 percent of total marketing budgets,
> against 24 percent before the pandemic. Source: global advertiser trade body
> content production research, March 2025, a survey of 50 global brands across
> more than ten sectors answered mainly by senior marketing procurement leads.

> Nearly two thirds of the brands surveyed expect to increase social and
> short-form video production. Source: global advertiser trade body content
> production research, March 2025, same survey of 50 global brands.

Five points of budget share gone, volume expectations up. That is the pressure
that makes consolidation attractive to a supplier, and it is why the questions
below lean hard on commercials.

One more, because it changes who is even on the roster:

> One third of the brands surveyed now build production relationships directly
> with the digital platforms. Source: global advertiser trade body content
> production research, March 2025, same survey of 50 global brands.

<!-- SECTION: Why review now -->

## Why a roster review is the moment production budgets actually move

Because it is the only moment they can. Production spend is sticky in a way
media spend is not. It is attached to relationships, to file archives, to
people who know the product, and to a hundred small conventions nobody wrote
down. Left alone, it renews.

A structural change to your supply base is the event that legitimately reopens
it. Not a bad quarter, not a new CMO. A change in who owns the studio.

The other reason to do it now is that the last time anyone published a
buy-side measurement of how production was actually being bought, the answer
was uncomfortable.

> Fewer than half of the advertisers surveyed required their agency to disclose
> whether a production job was being bid to an in-house or affiliated
> production company. Source: US advertiser trade body production transparency
> study, August 2017, built on a task force of 30 member executives and 12
> subject-matter organizations plus a supporting member survey whose respondent
> count was not published.

> More than 60 percent of the advertisers surveyed either did not require, or
> did not know whether their agency contract required, production rebates and
> other incentives to be passed back to them. Source: US advertiser trade body
> production transparency study, August 2017, same survey, respondent count not
> published.

Those figures are from 2017. They describe 2017 practice, not 2026 practice,
and both are percentages with no absolute figure published behind them. Treat
them as the last published buy-side measurement of production bidding and
rebate terms rather than as a description of your roster today.

The reason they are still worth putting in front of a partner is that the
question they measured has not gone away.

> The UK advertiser trade body brought its marketing procurement members
> together to examine competitive bidding in production and the growth of
> agency-owned production companies. Source: UK advertiser trade body marketing
> procurement session, January 2024, a members' panel, no figures published.

Seven years after the study, the trade bodies were still convening members on
the same problem. That is not a solved issue. That is an open one that stopped
being reported.

## What are the four things a roster review has to establish?

Fourteen questions, four groups. The grouping matters more than the count,
because each group fails differently and each one is owned by a different
person on your side.

| Group | What it establishes | Who owns it on your side |
|---|---|---|
| Contract continuity | Whether the paper still binds who you think it binds | Legal, with procurement |
| Commercials | How the price is actually built, and who else is paid | Procurement |
| Bidding conflict | Whether the party advising the spend also receives it | Marketing, with procurement |
| Platform and data exit | What you can take with you, and in what state | Marketing operations |

Ask them of every partner on the roster. That includes the incumbent you like,
the one you inherited, and the one writing this. A question set that exempts
the author is a sales document, not an instrument.

<!-- SECTION: Contract continuity -->

## Group one: does the contract still bind who you think it binds?

This group exists because a reorganization can change the entity delivering
your work without changing the entity on your contract. Sometimes that is
fine. You need to know which it is.

| Question | What a good answer sounds like | What a weak answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Which legal entity delivers our work today, and is it the entity we contracted with? | Names both, confirms they match, or explains precisely where they diverged and when | "It is all the same group" |
| Has our work moved between entities or units since we signed, and were we told? | A dated list of what moved and when, volunteered before you asked | "Nothing material has changed" |
| Does our agreement have a change-of-control or assignment clause, and what does it let you do without our consent? | Quotes the clause and says what it permits | Offers to check and come back |
| Who is our named production lead, and where does that person sit in the new structure? | A name, a reporting line, and what happens if they leave | A team inbox |

The last one deserves a note. It is tempting to assume that a centralized unit
changes who your producer answers to, and that this changes how your work gets
prioritized. That is a reasonable worry. It is not a documented fact, and no
buy-side or supply-side source establishes it as a pattern. So ask it. Do not
assume the answer.

<!-- SECTION: Commercials -->

## Group two: how is the price actually built?

The commercial group is where the 2017 findings still bite. Not because the
numbers are current, but because the mechanisms they exposed are still
available to anyone who wants to use them.

| Question | What a good answer sounds like | What a weak answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Show us the cost build for our last three jobs, line by line. | Sends them, unprompted, with labor separated from pass-through | "We work to an agreed rate card" |
| Which lines are marked up, and by how much? | A number, applied consistently, disclosed in the contract | "Our fees are all-in" |
| Do you receive rebates, incentives, or volume credits from any supplier, platform, or production service used on our work? If so, what happens to them? | Discloses them and states the pass-back mechanism | "We do not take rebates" with nothing in the contract to that effect |
| How does your pricing respond when our volume drops by half? | Sets out the fixed and variable split, including what stays fixed | "We would work with you on that" |

That last question is the one most likely to produce a useful silence. A
partner with real fixed costs behind your account will say so. A partner
whose price is mostly variable can tell you that and prove it from the
build.

The rebate question is worth asking even when you are confident of the answer.
Its value is that it converts an assumption into a contractual position.
The 2017 finding was not that most advertisers were being cheated. It was
that most advertisers did not know, and had not written down, what should
happen.

<!-- SECTION: Bidding conflict -->

## Group three: is the party recommending the spend also receiving it?

This is the oldest question in production procurement and the one that
centralization makes sharper, because a group with one production unit has an
obvious internal home for any job it recommends.

| Question | What you are listening for |
|---|---|
| When you run a competitive bid, do any of the bidders belong to your group, and do you tell us before the bid or after? | Before, always, in writing, as standard practice. "We disclose on request" means nobody has ever requested it |
| How many genuinely independent bids did our last three projects receive, and who were the losing bidders? | Numbers and roles, even anonymized. A general assurance that everything is competitively bid is not an answer to a question about three specific projects |
| Who writes the specification the bid is run against? | A named role, sitting apart from any party that can win the work |
| If we brought in a production partner from outside your group, would you brief them, and on what terms? | Willingness, on the same terms, with a coordination fee scoped in advance. Watch for a fee set high enough to make the option theoretical |

The specification question is the load-bearing one, and it is the one buyers
skip. Competitive bidding is easy to defeat without breaking a single rule.
You write a specification that only one bidder can meet comfortably, then you
run a scrupulously fair process against it. Every bid is genuine. The outcome
was decided before any of them arrived. So ask who holds the pen.

<!-- SECTION: Platform and data exit -->

## Group four: what leaves with you, and in what state?

Every production partner now runs your assets through some system. A digital
asset manager, an automation platform, a generation pipeline, often all three
stacked. The exit questions used to be about tapes and archives. Now they are
about formats and metadata, and about what rights travel with the file.

| Question | What a good answer sounds like | What a weak answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| If we leave in ninety days, what exactly do we receive? | A named list: layered masters, source files, project files, fonts, metadata, provenance records, with formats | "All your assets" |
| Do we get editable source files or only flattened outputs? | Editable, in a stated format, as a contractual deliverable | Flattened exports, or "the working files stay in our system" |
| Where is our material stored, under which jurisdiction, and for how long after we leave? | Named locations, a stated retention period, a deletion commitment | Vagueness about the cloud |
| For any AI-assisted work, what provenance record comes with each asset, and does it survive export? | A per-asset record with model, references, rights status, human edits, and approver, and a statement of what survives download | "Everything is compliant" |

The final question separates partners quickly, and it will keep separating
them as disclosure regimes tighten in more markets. A partner who has never
been asked will tell you the work is compliant. A partner who has built for it
will show you the record schema.

## How do you score fourteen questions across a whole roster?

Copy the table below, one column per partner. Score each row 0, 1 or 2. Zero is
no answer or a deflection. One is an answer with no evidence behind it. Two is
an answer with a document, a number, or a clause attached.

Sixteen rows, thirty-two points available. The total matters less than the
shape. A partner who scores well on commercials and badly on exit is a
different problem from one who scores the other way around, and the second is
usually worse, because commercial terms can be renegotiated and an archive you
cannot get out of cannot.

| Question | Group | Partner A | Partner B | Partner C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Delivering entity matches contracted entity | Continuity | | | |
| Work movement disclosed | Continuity | | | |
| Change-of-control clause explained | Continuity | | | |
| Named production lead and reporting line | Continuity | | | |
| Cost build supplied, line by line | Commercials | | | |
| Markup disclosed and consistent | Commercials | | | |
| Rebates disclosed with pass-back mechanism | Commercials | | | |
| Price response to a volume drop | Commercials | | | |
| Group bidders disclosed before the bid | Bidding | | | |
| Independent bid count evidenced | Bidding | | | |
| Specification written by a separate party | Bidding | | | |
| External partner briefing terms | Bidding | | | |
| Ninety-day exit list, named and formatted | Exit | | | |
| Editable source files as a deliverable | Exit | | | |
| Storage location, jurisdiction, retention | Exit | | | |
| Per-asset provenance record that survives export | Exit | | | |

Sixteen rows for fourteen questions. The two exit questions about files and
storage each split into two rows, because a partner can pass one half and fail
the other, and averaging that hides the failure. Keep them split.

Run it as a written exercise, not a conversation. Written answers can be
compared side by side and attached to a contract. Conversations cannot, and
the half-answer that sounded reasonable across a table tends not to survive
being typed out.

The question set was last revised in September 2026. The evidence under it
carries its own dates, which is the part worth checking before you rely on any
of it, here or anywhere else.

<!-- SECTION: What to do with the result -->

## What do you do with a roster that scores badly?

Usually not what people expect, which is to fire someone.

A low score is most often a documentation problem rather than a conduct
problem. Nobody ever asked, so nothing got written down, so nothing can be
evidenced now. The fix is a contract amendment. Amendments are cheap. A pitch
is the most expensive thing in marketing procurement, it eats months of your
team's attention, and it frequently ends with a partner who cannot answer
these questions either.

Reserve it for the two cases that warrant it: a partner who will not disclose a
bidding relationship, and a partner who cannot commit to an exit position.
Those are structural. Everything else is paperwork you have not done yet.

Worth saying plainly, since this is published by a production company. We
answer the same sixteen rows. The brands solutions page and the work index set
out how the engagements are structured, and the pricing page states the
commercial model in the same terms group two asks for. If any of it does not
survive the questions, that is useful to you either way.

## Questions buyers ask about roster reviews

**What is a production roster review?**

A structured reassessment of every partner who makes your advertising assets.
It is different from an agency pitch, which replaces a partner, and different
from a vendor selection, which adds one. A roster review examines the partners
you already have, usually because something changed in how production supply
is organized rather than because a contract expired.

**How often should we review our production roster?**

Whenever the structure of your supply changes, and otherwise every two to
three years. Structural triggers matter more than the calendar: a partner
reorganizing, your work moving between entities, a shift in where your volume
sits, or a new disclosure obligation in a market you sell into.

**What does production consolidation mean for a brand?**

It means production capability that used to sit inside individual agencies now
sits in a shared unit. Trade coverage in February 2026 counted four such
reorganizations among the largest groups between 2025 and early 2026. The
practical consequence is that the entity making your work may no longer be the
entity you contracted with, which is what group one of these questions tests.

**Should procurement or marketing run the review?**

Both, on different groups. Legal and procurement own contract continuity and
commercials. Marketing owns bidding conflict, because only marketing can judge
whether a specification was written to favor one bidder. Marketing operations
owns exit, because only they know what a usable file actually is.

**Do these questions apply to an in-house studio?**

Most of them. Contract continuity does not apply, and the bidding group changes
shape, but the commercial questions apply with more force, because an in-house
unit rarely has its cost build examined at all. The exit questions apply
unchanged, since an in-house team still runs your assets through systems you
may not control.

**What is a production rebate?**

A payment, credit, or incentive that flows from a supplier back to the party
that placed the work, rather than to the advertiser who paid for it. Volume
credits from a rental house, a post facility, or a platform all count. The
question for your contract is not whether they exist but where they land, and
group two above is where you settle it in writing.
