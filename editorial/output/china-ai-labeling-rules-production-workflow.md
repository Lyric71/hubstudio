# China's AI labeling rules: what your production workflow has to do

The rules have been in force since September 2025 and they are well explained.
What nobody has published is the part that actually costs you: which stage of
your pipeline drops the label, and what has to be in the file you hand over.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What do China's AI labeling rules require a production workflow to do?

Two things, in two different places. Every piece of generated content carries a
visible mark a person can see, and a second label written into the file
metadata that machines read. The visible one has to survive export. The
metadata one usually does not survive a normal retouch and delivery pipeline,
which is where a production workflow quietly fails. hubStudio published the
stage-by-stage map below so a producer can find that break in their own
pipeline.

This describes production practice, not legal advice. The instruments are cited
so you can take them to a lawyer, which is what you should do before signing
anything that turns on them.

Reviewed September 2026. The rules are young and the enforcement pattern is
still forming, so check the dates on everything below, including here.

<!-- SECTION: The instruments -->

## Which rules are we actually talking about?

Two documents, both live since the same day.

> The Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content were issued
> jointly by the Cyberspace Administration of China, the Ministry of Industry
> and Information Technology, the Ministry of Public Security and the National
> Radio and Television Administration, released 14 March 2025 and in force
> since 1 September 2025.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025.

> The mandatory national standard GB 45438-2025, Cybersecurity technology,
> Labeling method for content generated by artificial intelligence, was
> published 28 February 2025 and took effect 1 September 2025, the same day as
> the Measures.
> Source: national standards register, State Administration for Market
> Regulation, February 2025.

The Measures say what must happen. The standard says what it has to look like
in the file. Most English coverage stops at the first and never opens the
second, which is why so much of it reads as a summary of intentions rather
than something a producer can build against.

<!-- SECTION: Two labels -->

## What is the difference between an explicit and an implicit label?

One is for people. One is for machines. They behave completely differently in
a pipeline, and treating them as a single requirement is the most common
mistake we see.

The explicit label, xianshi biaoshi, is the visible one: a mark on the image, an
opening-frame notice on video, a spoken or tonal cue on audio.

The implicit label, yinshi biaoshi, lives inside the file.

> Article 5 of the Measures puts the implicit label in the file metadata and
> says it carries content attribute information, the service provider name or
> code, and a content ID, among other production element information. The same
> article defines file metadata as descriptive information embedded in the file
> header in a specific encoding format.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

On what that metadata looks like in practice, the standard is the authority,
and here the evidence is one step weaker.

> Published readings of GB 45438-2025 agree that the implicit label is carried
> as a JSON object in the file metadata with fields for the label flag, the
> content producer, a production ID, the content propagator and a propagation
> ID, and that the visible text label should be at least 5 percent of the
> shortest side of the frame, running at least two seconds on video.
> Source: two independent published readings of the standard text, 2025 and
> 2026. The standard's own text sits behind the national register's viewer and
> was not read directly.

Treat the sizing numbers as a working target rather than a settled cap, and
confirm them against the standard before a campaign turns on them.

| | Explicit label | Implicit label |
|---|---|---|
| Who reads it | A person | A platform, automatically |
| Where it lives | In the frame, the opening seconds, the audio | In the file header metadata |
| Survives a re-export | Yes, it is pixels | Frequently not |
| Survives a platform re-encode | Yes | Often stripped |
| What breaks it | Cropping, overlaying, replacing the first frames | Almost any conversion that rewrites the header |

That table is the whole problem in five rows. The label you can see is the one
that survives. The label carrying the accountability is the one that breaks.

<!-- SECTION: The export rule -->

## What does the export requirement mean for file formats?

This is the clause that binds a production pipeline, and the detail matters.

> Article 4 of the Measures says that when a service provider offers download,
> copy or export functions for generated synthetic content, it must ensure the
> file contains a conforming explicit label.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

Read that carefully. The export duty attaches to the explicit label. It is the
visible mark that has to be in the downloaded file, not the metadata. A lot of
secondary commentary blurs the two and leaves people believing that any export
carrying clean metadata is compliant. It does not say that.

The practical consequence for a studio is unglamorous. If your delivery format
flattens, transcodes, or strips headers, your visible label needs to be baked
into the pixels or the audio before that step, and your metadata label needs to
be re-attached after it. Doing it the other way around produces a file that
looks compliant in the edit and is not compliant on delivery.

<!-- SECTION: The pipeline -->

## Where does the label attach, and where does it get lost?

Here is the pipeline, stage by stage. Print it, walk your own workflow against
it, and mark the stages you cannot currently evidence.

| Stage | Explicit label | Implicit label | The usual failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Generation | Applied by the service | Written by the service | Nothing, if the tool conforms |
| Ingest into the edit | Intact | Often dropped on import | The editor reads pixels, not headers |
| Retouch and compositing | Survives unless cropped | Rewritten or discarded on save | A new file is written, and nothing carries the old header |
| Versioning and resizing | At risk when the frame is recropped | Usually gone | Batch resize tools rarely preserve custom metadata |
| Export and delivery | Must be present, per Article 4 | Should be re-attached here | The step nobody owns |
| Platform upload | Read by the platform | Read by the platform if present | Re-encode strips what survived |
| Archive | Whatever shipped | Whatever shipped | The archive inherits the gap silently |

You do not have to take the failure column on trust. The regulator published
what it found.

> A November 2025 enforcement round found generation services adding no visible
> label, omitting the label when files were exported, and failing to embed
> metadata identifying the producer and the content, and found distribution
> services failing to verify implicit labels, failing to show a prominent
> notice, and failing to add distribution metadata.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 25 November 2025.

Omitting the label when files were exported. That is the export stage in the
table above, named by the authority enforcing the rule.

What happens after upload is not entirely in your hands either.

> Article 6 of the Measures gives a distribution platform three cases in which
> it adds a prominent notice: the implicit label in the metadata confirms the
> content is generated, the user declares it is generated, or neither is
> present but an explicit label or other generation traces are detected, in
> which case the platform marks it as suspected.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

Note the third case. Content that arrives with no metadata and no declaration
can still be marked as suspected on the strength of traces. From a brand's
point of view, suspected is the worst of the three outcomes: the same
disclosure, without the control over how it is worded.

And platforms do not behave uniformly.

> In a September 2025 test, AI videos from two Chinese generation tools were
> uploaded to six Chinese platforms, both with and without the tool's visible
> watermark and with no manual declaration. One platform flagged every clip,
> one flagged only the watermarked versions, and four flagged none.
> Source: a Beijing daily's own controlled upload test, September 2025, six
> platforms, two content categories, watermarked and stripped variants.

One test, one publication, before most of the enforcement activity. It is not a
compliance benchmark. It is evidence for a narrower point that still holds:
platform behavior varies, so you cannot delegate your labeling to whatever the
platform happens to do on upload day.

<!-- SECTION: The handover pack -->

## What has to be in the file you hand over?

This is the section that changes how a production company works, and it rests
on a provision almost nobody cites.

> Article 9 of the Measures allows a provider to hand over content without an
> explicit label where a user applies for it, once the user agreement has set
> out the user's labeling obligation and liability, and requires the provider
> to keep the related logs for at least six months.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

Read what that does. An unlabeled master is contemplated, not forbidden. It is
conditional on paperwork and on logs. That is the legal shape of a normal
production handover, where the studio delivers a clean master and the brand
publishes the labeled version, and it means the handover pack is not an
administrative nicety. It is the condition.

So a pack that satisfies it contains, at minimum:

- The delivered master, and a statement of whether it carries the explicit
  label or was handed over unlabeled.
- The signed agreement clause that sets out who carries the labeling
  obligation, if the master is unlabeled.
- The implicit label content that belongs in the file: content attribute, the
  producing service name or code, the content ID.
- A record of which stages rewrote the file, so a missing header can be
  explained rather than guessed at.
- The retention commitment covering the logs, at least six months, and who
  holds them.
- The labeled variants actually published, per market and per platform.

Six months is a floor, not a target. It is also shorter than most campaign
cycles, which is worth noticing: a file delivered in March and questioned in
November sits outside the minimum. Set your own retention against your campaign
calendar, not against the floor.

<!-- SECTION: Who is responsible -->

## Who is responsible, the brand or the agency?

Less settled than most explainers imply, and worth being careful about.

The Measures assign duties to named roles: service providers, distribution
platforms, app stores, and users. They do not say which of those a brand is, or
an agency, or a production studio. That mapping is interpretation, and it is
the interpretation that most published guidance quietly performs on your
behalf.

So the honest formulation is that a brand publishing generated content through
a Chinese platform is generally treated as a user, with the declaration duty
that follows. A studio that operates a generation service is generally treated
as a provider. An agency sitting in between is generally treated according to
what it actually does with the file rather than what the contract calls it.
Those are working assumptions to take to counsel, not statements of law.

One thing the instrument does say without ambiguity:

> Article 10 of the Measures says a user publishing generated synthetic content
> through a distribution service must proactively declare it and use the
> labeling function the provider supplies, and that no organization or
> individual may maliciously delete, alter, forge or conceal a label, or supply
> tools or services for others to do so.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, March 2025, instrument text.

Proactively. The duty does not wait for the platform to ask.

The second half of that article has a practical edge that catches production
teams by surprise. Supplying tools or services for others to remove a label is
itself covered. A cleanup step in your pipeline that strips labels for
convenience is not a neutral technical operation.

<!-- SECTION: Enforcement -->

## Has any of this actually been enforced?

Yes, repeatedly, and the pattern is more useful than the totals.

> Cyberspace authorities reported in February 2026 that they had handled 13,421
> accounts and cleared more than 543,000 items of unlawful or non-compliant
> information over AI-generated content published without an AI label. The
> release is a campaign report and does not state the period covered or a
> sample.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 12 February 2026.

Those are campaign totals with no stated period behind them, so read them as
evidence of activity rather than as a measured rate.

> The same February 2026 release lists the sale of tutorials and software for
> removing AI labels among the five categories of violation it acted on.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 12 February 2026.

> An April 2026 enforcement campaign names two labeling failures directly:
> content published with no label or with a label whose size, position or
> transparency does not conform, and the teaching or supply of label-removal
> tutorials and tools.
> Source: Cyberspace Administration of China, 30 April 2026.

Size, position or transparency. That is a conforming-label problem, not a
missing-label problem, and it is the one a brand is most likely to trip over
while believing it complied. A label placed for design reasons rather than for
the standard is still a failure.

On penalties, resist the temptation to plan around a number. The Measures do
not create a new schedule of fines, and enforcement runs back through existing
instruments and authorities. Anyone quoting you a figure is inferring it.

## Questions production teams ask about the labeling rules

**Do China's AI labeling rules apply to a foreign brand?**

They attach to content published through services operating in mainland China,
not to the nationality of the advertiser. If you publish generated content on a
Chinese platform, the declaration duty in Article 10 is engaged regardless of
where your team sits. Whether your agency or your studio shares that duty
depends on the role each actually plays, which is a question for counsel.

**What is the difference between an explicit and an implicit label?**

The explicit label is visible: a mark on the image, an opening-frame notice on
video, an audio cue. The implicit label is metadata written into the file
header, carrying content attribute information, the producing service name or
code, and a content ID. Article 4 requires the explicit label to be present in
a downloaded or exported file.

**Does the label have to survive download?**

The explicit one, yes. Article 4 requires a service offering download, copy or
export to ensure the file contains a conforming explicit label. The implicit
metadata label is not what that clause is about, which is a distinction a lot
of secondary commentary loses.

**Can a studio deliver an unlabeled master?**

Article 9 contemplates it. A provider may hand over content without the
explicit label where the user applies for it and the user agreement sets out
the user's labeling obligation and liability, and the provider keeps the
related logs for at least six months. So the unlabeled master is conditional on
paperwork and record keeping, not simply available on request.

**Where in a production pipeline does the label usually get lost?**

At export, on the evidence of the regulator's own November 2025 enforcement
round, which found labels omitted when files were exported and metadata
identifying the producer missing. Retouch and batch resizing are the other
common points, because both write a new file and few tools carry custom header
metadata across.

**What are the penalties?**

There is no schedule of penalties in the Measures themselves. Enforcement is
routed back through authorities and instruments that already existed, and what
has been published so far reads as account handling and content clearance
rather than financial penalties. If someone quotes you a number, ask which
instrument it comes from, because it is not coming from this one.
