# What a finished brand asset actually costs in 2026

Every page that answers this question gives you a range without telling you
what one asset is, where the number came from, or who paid for the research.
This one defines the unit first, publishes absolute figures, and shows the
method. No email gate.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What does one finished brand asset cost?

Between roughly $0.25 for a bulk ecommerce image edit and several thousand for
a directed brand film, and the spread is that wide because "one asset" means
six different things depending on who is quoting. Published studio rate cards
collected in September 2026 put a standard product image at $39 to $50 and a
short video clip at about $93. hubStudio published this page because the
category will not.

Read the definition below before the numbers. Skipping it is how buyers end up
comparing a quote for a photograph against a quote for a campaign.

<!-- SECTION: The unit -->

## What counts as one finished asset?

Nobody has settled this. There is no ISO standard, no trade-body definition,
and the three published conventions that come closest draw the line in three
different places for three different purposes.

| Convention | What it treats as one unit | What it does not count as new |
|---|---|---|
| The asset identity registry | One registered advertising asset, coded and carried across platforms | No published rule on lengths, cuts, language versions or resizes |
| The production bid form | One production, estimated across sections A to P, with stills and social bid as separate breakouts | Alternate setups shot inside the same production |
| The performers' union agreement | One commercial, for session-fee purposes | Alternate scenes shot in the same session, and edits inside defined parameters |

Three conventions, three boundaries. A resize is a new asset under none of
them explicitly, and a new asset under all of them in practice, because
somebody has to make it.

**So here is the definition this page uses, stated openly because the industry
has not supplied one.** One finished asset is one deliverable file, in one
specified format and one specified duration or dimension, approved for use and
cleared for the channel it will run on.

Under that definition a resize is a new asset. A language version is a new
asset. An alternate cut is a new asset. An alternate take shot in the same
session is not.

You may disagree with that line. That is fine, and it is the point: you now
know where it sits, which is more than any quote you receive will tell you.
Every band below is quoted against the unit its own source card states, and
where that differs from the definition above, the page says so.

## What do published rate cards actually say?

These are read directly from published pricing pages, one card at a time, in
September 2026. Not aggregated, not estimated, not taken from a roundup.

> The floor for ecommerce image editing sits between roughly $0.25 and $1.20 an
> image on published cards in this category, with one card adding a fixed
> platform fee of about $95 a month on top.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly and compared, no vendor named.

> Published studio rate cards put a standard product image at $39 to $50, a
> model or lifestyle image at about $95, a short video clip at about $93 and a
> single UGC video at about $89, with editing add-ons around $49 each and a
> finished pro edit at about $199.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly, prices quoted as each card states them.

Put those two blockquotes side by side and the first useful thing appears. The
gap between an image edit at $0.25 and a product image at $39 is not a
negotiating range. It is two different products wearing the same noun.

| Asset type | Published band | What that card's unit actually includes |
|---|---|---|
| Ecommerce image edit | $0.25 to $1.20 per image | Retouching an image you already own. No capture |
| Standard product image | $39 to $50 | Capture of a supplied product on a plain background |
| Model or lifestyle image | about $95 | Capture with a person or a set |
| Short video clip | about $93 | One clip, one format |
| Single UGC video | about $89 | One creator-style video |
| Editing add-on | about $49 each | One change to an existing deliverable |
| Finished pro edit | about $199 | A produced edit rather than a tweak |

Retouch is not production. It is the cheapest line on this page by two orders
of magnitude, and it is the line most often quoted when a buyer asks what
content costs.

<!-- SECTION: What is inside -->

## What is inside a per-asset price?

The strongest evidence on this comes from the production industry's own
paperwork, and it contains an absence worth more than most of the figures.

> The US commercial production trade body's standard bid form itemizes a
> production across sections A through P, from prep crew to breakout talent, and
> carries no line at all for media usage payments.
> Source: the trade body's own free bid form, updated January 2023, downloaded
> and read in full September 2026.

Sixteen sections covering everything from prep to talent, and usage is not one
of them. Usage is what you pay to keep running the asset, and the standard
document for pricing production does not price it. So a production quote and
the cost of having that asset in market are different numbers, and the industry's
own form is built on that assumption.

The same body is explicit about the question buyers should be asking.

> The same trade body's 2026 bidding guide tells bidders to establish whether a
> stated budget is all in, and whether stills, social elements and behind the
> scenes carry separate decks and separate budgets.
> Source: the trade body's published bidding reference guide, 2026 edition.

Stills, social and behind the scenes as separate budgets. If you have ever
received a film quote and then a second invoice for the social cutdowns, that
is not a vendor being sharp. That is the category's standard structure.

Talent adds a third layer with its own boundary rules.

> Under the US performers' union commercials agreement effective 1 April 2025,
> alternate scenes shot for one commercial in the same session are not treated
> as new commercials, while an edit or lift outside the defined parameters
> creates a new commercial and new fees.
> Source: the union commercials agreement effective April 2025, as read by two
> independent law firms. The union's own rate documents are not machine
> readable.

An edit outside the parameters creates a new commercial. Not a new file. A new
commercial, with new fees attached. That is a versioning cost that lives
entirely outside the production quote.

So the honest anatomy of a per-asset cost has four layers, and most published
figures cover only the first:

| Layer | What it covers | Usually in the quoted price |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Direction, capture, crew, retouch, finish | Yes |
| Versioning | Resizes, cuts, language versions | Sometimes, often a separate deck |
| Rights and talent | Usage, session fees, renewals | No |
| Management | Project management, trafficking, QA | Varies, often invisible |

## How do the four buying models compare?

As models, not as companies. What differs is the unit you are charged for,
which changes what a low number means.

> Published managed design subscriptions state monthly floors between roughly
> $6,000 and $15,000, and the one card offering a named dedicated team starts at
> roughly $30,000 a month on a twelve-month term, plus a $1,000 monthly software
> fee.
> Source: published subscription rate cards in this category, September 2026,
> published pricing pages only, no vendor named. The $30,000 dedicated floor
> appears on a single card.

> Unlimited-request design subscription tiers run from roughly $700 to roughly
> $4,500 a month, month to month, with no capture, no talent and no usage
> included.
> Source: published subscription rate cards in this category, September 2026,
> two independent pricing pages read directly.

Two subscription bands, an order of magnitude apart, both called
subscriptions. The cheaper one excludes capture, talent and usage, which is to
say it excludes the three layers that make an asset a campaign asset.

| Model | The unit you buy | Published band, September 2026 | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelance | An hour or a day | Not published in any comparable form | Coordination cost sits with you, and does not appear anywhere |
| Agency retainer | Access to a team | Rate benchmarks exist but are member-only | Hourly rates say nothing about assets delivered |
| Design subscription | Active requests per month | $700 to $4,500 unlimited-request tiers; $6,000 to $15,000 managed floors | Excludes capture, talent and usage. Queue depth decides real throughput |
| Studio production | A delivered asset | $39 to $199 per asset on published studio cards | Minimums and scope. A per-asset card assumes a defined brief |

On the agency row, note what happened when we went looking:

> The UK advertiser trade body's 2025 creative and media agency hourly rate
> benchmarks are released only to members who contribute their own rates, in
> aggregated form.
> Source: the trade body's own published access condition, read September 2026.

Contribute your own rates to see anyone else's. That is a reasonable way to
run a benchmark and a complete barrier to a buyer trying to sanity-check a
quote.

<!-- SECTION: Why benchmarks fail -->

## Why does no usable benchmark exist?

Because of who would have to publish it. Four structural reasons, and none is
a conspiracy.

**Agencies will not disclose unit cost**, because a per-asset figure exposes
margin. The trade body evidence on this is old but it is the only measurement
anyone published.

> Fewer than half of respondents required in-house bidding disclosure, and more
> than 60 percent did not require production rebates be returned.
> Source: US advertiser trade body production transparency study, August 2017.
> The respondent count was never published, which is the point.

Read the source line again. The one study anyone published on production
transparency withheld its own sample size.

**Software vendors publish only figures that flatter their pricing.** A cost
comparison from a company selling seats will find that seats are good value.
Nobody had to lie for that to happen. The question got chosen before the
research started.

**Consultancies gate.** The numbers exist behind a form, which means they
cannot be cited, checked or argued with.

**And the largest dataset in the category measures the wrong thing.**

> The largest creative dataset published in this category, 578,750 creatives
> across 6,015 brands and $1.29 billion of ad spend, reports weekly creative
> volume and expected hit rate. It reports no cost per asset in any currency.
> Source: a creative analytics benchmark in this category, 2026, platform data,
> period covered not stated by the publisher.

Half a million creatives, over a billion dollars of spend, and no cost figure.
It answers how many you need and how many will work. It does not answer what
one costs, because the platform holding that data never sees an invoice.

Meanwhile the money is under pressure from both directions.

> Production accounted for an average of 19 percent of total marketing budgets,
> against 24 percent before the pandemic.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> a survey of 50 global brands across more than ten sectors. It publishes no
> absolute cost per asset.

> Two thirds of brands changed their agency model in the previous four years.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> the same 50-brand survey, benchmarked against the same study run in 2021.

Five points of budget share gone, two thirds of models changed, and still no
published unit cost to plan against.

## How this page was built

Every band on this page was read directly from a published pricing page on
9 September 2026, one card at a time, and is quoted against the unit that card
states. Bands built from two or more independent cards are given as ranges. A
band drawn from a single card is labeled as such. No figure on this page comes
from an aggregator, a roundup or a vendor's estimate of somebody else's prices.
Where a percentage appears, it is a share of something else, a budget or a set
of survey respondents, never a cost standing in for a number the source would
not print.

What that method cannot do, said plainly. Published cards skew toward the
productized end of the market, because that is the end that publishes. Custom
and enterprise work is under-represented here by construction, and every card
read carried a tier with no published price. Treat these bands as the floor of
the visible market rather than as an average of all work done.

The bands date fast. Prices change without notice, which is why the collection
date sits on every figure rather than at the bottom of the page. Reviewed
September 2026.

the inputs you already know and returns a range rather than a quote. The pricing
page sets out how hubStudio scopes work, and the AI excellence page covers what
changes when generation carries part of the load.

## Questions buyers ask about cost per asset

**How much does one creative asset cost?**

Published studio rate cards collected in September 2026 put a standard product
image at $39 to $50, a model or lifestyle image at about $95, a short video
clip at about $93 and a finished pro edit at about $199. Bulk ecommerce image
editing runs far lower, roughly $0.25 to $1.20 an image, because it retouches
an image that already exists rather than capturing one.

**What counts as one finished asset?**

There is no industry definition. This page uses one deliverable file, in one
specified format and one specified duration or dimension, approved and cleared
for its channel. Under that definition a resize, a language version and an
alternate cut are each new assets, while an alternate take shot in the same
session is not.

**Why do agencies not publish cost per asset?**

A per-asset figure exposes margin. The only published measurement of
production transparency, from a US advertiser trade body in August 2017, found
fewer than half of respondents required in-house bidding disclosure and more
than 60 percent did not require rebates be returned. That study did not
publish its own respondent count.

**Is a subscription cheaper than a production studio?**

It depends what you count. Unlimited-request design subscriptions publish tiers
of roughly $700 to $4,500 a month with no capture, no talent and no usage
included, while managed subscriptions publish floors of roughly $6,000 to
$15,000. A subscription prices access to designers; production prices delivered
assets. Compare them per delivered asset, not per month.

**What does a cost per asset figure usually leave out?**

Usage rights, most often. The US commercial production trade body's standard
bid form itemizes a production across sections A to P and carries no line for
media usage payments at all. Versioning and trafficking are the other two:
under the performers' union agreement effective April 2025, an edit outside
defined parameters creates a new commercial with new fees.

**How much does a 30-second brand film cost?**

No trustworthy published band exists for this at the finished-film level, which
is why this page does not print one. The figures circulating are seller
estimates without a sample or a method. What can be said from primary sources
is that stills, social elements and behind the scenes are bid as separate
budgets, so a film number and a campaign number are never the same number.
