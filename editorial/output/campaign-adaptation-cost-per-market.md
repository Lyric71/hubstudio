# Campaign adaptation cost: pricing one master across N markets

Search this and you get media budgets, because the production question has no
agreed vocabulary. "Adaptation" covers a resize and a reshoot with local talent,
which are not the same purchase and should never share a line on a budget.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## How much does campaign adaptation cost per market?

There is no per-market average, and no source publishes one. What exists is a
single published rate card, one worked example of a ladder, and a great deal of
confident writing with no denominator behind it.

> Across six phrasings a buyer would actually type, forty-nine results returned
> one page that puts a price on an adaptation and says what the price includes.
> No page in the set defines adaptation as a ladder of levels with a cost
> attached to each level.
> Source: search result review conducted 10 September 2026, six queries, every
> result classified by page type and by whether it publishes a cost.

One page in forty-nine. And the shape of who is writing explains the rest.

> Roughly four in five results for these queries are published by a party
> selling the service, the software or the media being described. Not one is a
> buyer-side benchmark, a trade body instrument or a survey with a stated
> sample.
> Source: search result review conducted 10 September 2026, forty of forty-nine
> results counted by hand from the six result sets.

So this page does the only useful thing available: it defines the unit, prices
what can be priced, and is explicit about where the arithmetic stops.

<!-- SECTION: vocabulary -->

## What is a toolkit adaptation?

The words buyers use are versioning, toolkit adaptation, master and adapt, and
localization, and they are used interchangeably for jobs that differ by more
than an order of magnitude in effort.

A useful unit needs three numbers, not one. A rollout is one master, across N
markets, in M formats. Multiply those and you get slots, which is the thing
being bought. One master across twelve markets in six formats is seventy-two
slots, and a quote that does not state its slot count is not comparable to
anything.

But slots alone still hide the important variable, which is how deep the change
goes in each slot. That is the ladder nobody publishes, so here is one.

<!-- SECTION: the ladder -->

## The five levels, defined here because nobody else defines them

**This ladder is this page's own definition.** No trade body publishes it, no
rate card uses these names, and it is offered because the category has no
shared vocabulary and a buyer needs one to compare quotes. Use it as a scoping
instrument, not as an industry standard.

| Level | What changes | What stays | What it scales with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resize | Frame and safe areas only | Every creative element | Formats, not markets |
| Retitle | On-screen text and end card | Footage, voice, music | Languages, not markets |
| Recut | Structure, duration, pacing | Captured material | Formats and platforms |
| Recast | Voice, talent, or on-screen product | Concept and structure | Markets, and rights |
| Reshoot | New capture in market | The idea only | Markets, fully |

Read the last column. Only two of the five levels scale with the number of
markets at all, which is the single most useful thing on this page. A rollout
priced as though every slot were a reshoot is priced wrong, and so is one
budgeted as though every slot were a resize.

<!-- SECTION: what is published -->

## What one published card actually prices

There is exactly one, and it is worth reading closely because of what it names.

> One published adaptation rate card in this category starts at 1,350 euro
> excluding VAT for a 15-second commercial adaptation, and states exactly what
> that buys: a new voice-over, new titles, a new packshot, an audio mix and
> mastering.
> Source: a published commercial adaptation rate card, read 10 September 2026.
> One card, one duration, one inclusion list, and no publication date printed on
> the page.

That inclusion list is the valuable part, more than the number. Voice, titles,
packshot, mix, master. In the ladder above, that is a recast without new
capture, and knowing which level a price covers is what makes two quotes
comparable at all.

Note also what sits outside it.

> The same card bills broadcast master files separately, at 65 euro per file,
> and quotes anything outside the inclusion list, logo changes, dubbing an
> actor, recording a new product line, only after scoping it.
> Source: the same published adaptation rate card, read 10 September 2026.
> Published prices read directly from the seller's own page.

Deliverable files billed per file, and everything genuinely creative quoted
separately. That is the standard shape, and it means the headline number covers
the middle of the job and neither end.

For the lighter levels of the ladder, the relevant published rates come from
different categories entirely, which is itself a finding: no single card prices
the whole ladder.

> Published rate cards in this category put the floor for ecommerce image
> editing between roughly $0.25 and $1.20 an image, with one card adding a fixed
> platform fee of about $95 a month on top.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly and compared, no vendor named.

> Published studio rate cards put a standard product image at $39 to $50, a
> model or lifestyle image at about $95, a short video clip at about $93 and a
> single UGC video at about $89, with editing add-ons around $49 each.
> Source: published pricing pages in this category, collected September 2026,
> two independent cards read directly, prices quoted as each card states them.

Language work is priced on a different basis again, and the reason matters.

> Published rate cards in the translation category put professional English to
> Simplified Chinese at roughly $0.08 to $0.18 per source word, and price
> transcreation per hour or per project instead, because source word count does
> not track the creative work.
> Source: published pricing pages collected across the category, September 2026,
> no company named. Rate cards only, no survey and no method published behind
> the band.

Transcreation is priced by time because rewriting a line so it lands in another
culture has no relationship to how many words the original had. Any adaptation
budget that prices copy by word count has assumed the cheap version of the job.

These bands come from different cards, different categories and different
currencies. They are not additive and this page does not add them, because a
sum built from euro, dollar and per-word units would read as a quote while
meaning nothing.

<!-- SECTION: why no benchmark -->

## Why no benchmark exists to check a quote against

Two structural reasons, and neither is anyone behaving badly.

The organizations best placed to publish rate benchmarks put them behind
membership, and the membership condition is that you contribute your own rates
first.

> The UK advertiser trade body's 2025 creative and media agency hourly rate
> benchmarks are released only to members who contribute their own rates, in
> aggregated form.
> Source: the trade body's own published access condition, read September 2026.

That is a reasonable way to run a benchmark and a complete barrier to a buyer
trying to sanity-check one quote. It also means the numbers that do exist cannot
be cited, argued with or checked.

Meanwhile the largest dataset anywhere near this question measures something
else entirely.

> The largest creative dataset published in this category, 578,750 creatives
> across 6,015 brands and $1.29 billion of ad spend, reports weekly creative
> volume and expected hit rate. It reports no cost per asset in any currency.
> Source: a creative analytics benchmark in this category, 2026, platform data,
> period covered not stated by the publisher.

More than half a million creatives and no cost figure. It can tell you how many
versions a brand runs and how many work. It cannot tell you what one costs,
because the platform holding that data never sees an invoice. Volume data and
cost data live in different places, and nobody has joined them.

<!-- SECTION: linearity -->

## Does adaptation cost scale linearly with markets?

No, and the places it breaks are predictable enough to plan around.

**Markets and languages are not the same count.** A twelve-market rollout is
rarely twelve languages. If eight languages cover twelve markets, the language
work is two thirds of what a naive model assumes, while the legal and platform
work stays at twelve. Those two lines move independently and should be budgeted
separately.

Run it on the one published price, keeping it in its own currency. Twelve
adaptations at 1,350 euro is 16,200 euro. Eight is 10,800 euro. That single
distinction, markets against languages, is a 5,400 euro difference on the same
brief, before anyone negotiates anything.

**Deliverable files scale with markets times formats, not with markets.** At 65
euro a broadcast master, twenty-four files is 1,560 euro. That line is small per
unit and grows fastest, because it multiplies twice.

**Rights do not scale smoothly at all.** They step.

> Under the US performers' union commercials agreement effective 1 April 2025,
> alternate scenes shot for one commercial in the same session are not treated
> as new commercials, while an edit or lift outside the defined parameters
> creates a new commercial and new fees.
> Source: the union commercials agreement effective April 2025, as read by two
> independent law firms. The union's own rate documents are not machine
> readable.

That is the non-linear breakpoint in the whole model. Recuts inside the defined
parameters are cheap versioning. One recut outside them is a new commercial with
new fees, and the cost jumps rather than climbs. It is also invisible in every
production quote, because the industry's own bid form does not price it.

> The US commercial production trade body's standard bid form itemizes a
> production across sections A through P, from prep crew to breakout talent, and
> carries no line at all for media usage payments.
> Source: the trade body's own free bid form, updated January 2023, downloaded
> and read in full September 2026.

**Legal and platform load steps by market too.** Some markets add a compliance
layer that has nothing to do with creative effort and everything to do with what
must be recorded, labeled and disclosed. China is the clearest case, and the
article on what it costs to localize a campaign for China covers that stack
rather than repeating it here.

## A toolkit at twelve markets, line by line

One master, twelve markets, six formats, eight languages. Each line is quoted in
the currency its own source publishes, and no total appears, because these come
from different cards with different units and adding them would produce a
number that looks like a quote and means nothing.

| Line | Scales with | Published basis |
|---|---|---|
| Master production | Nothing, it is the fixed cost | Not published as an adaptation rate |
| Resize across formats | Formats only, 6 | Image editing floor, $0.25 to $1.20 an image |
| Retitle and end cards | Languages, 8 | Per source word, $0.08 to $0.18, or per hour for transcreation |
| Recut per platform | Formats times platforms | Not published anywhere as a rate |
| Recast, voice and packshot | Languages or markets, depending on the brief | 1,350 euro per 15-second adaptation on one card |
| Deliverable masters | Markets times formats, 72 | 65 euro per file |
| Rights and usage | Steps, not scales | Not on the bid form at all |
| In-market compliance | Markets, unevenly | Varies by market, see the China localization article |

Two rows carry no published basis at all. Recut and master production are
central to any rollout and neither has a rate anyone publishes, which is worth
knowing before you accept a quote that prices them confidently.

The pattern in the "scales with" column is the deliverable here. Copy that
column into your own budget, count each driver once, and you will have a model
that survives contact with a real rollout better than any per-market average
would.

<!-- SECTION: what drives it up -->

## What drives adaptation cost up

**Treating every slot as the deepest level.** The commonest and most expensive
error. Scope each slot to a ladder level before pricing anything.

**Counting markets where you should count languages,** or the reverse. They
drive different lines and the gap between them is real money.

**Discovering rights late.** A recut that steps outside the agreed parameters
converts a versioning job into a new commercial. Settle the parameters when the
master is made, not when the cutdowns are due.

**Pricing copy by the word.** Transcreation is priced by time for a reason, and
a word-count budget has silently assumed translation instead.

**Leaving deliverable files out of the count.** They multiply twice and are the
line most often forgotten, being individually trivial.

There is one more constraint worth naming, which is the budget the whole thing
comes out of.

> Production accounted for an average of 19 percent of total marketing budgets,
> against 24 percent before the pandemic.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> a survey of 50 global brands across more than ten sectors. It publishes no
> absolute cost per asset.

> Nearly two thirds of those brands expect to increase social and short-form
> video production, and one in three now builds production relationships
> directly with digital platforms.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025.
> The same 50-brand survey.

More formats out of a smaller share. That is the pressure making the ladder
worth using: the only way those two facts coexist is if most slots are being
delivered at the cheaper levels, deliberately rather than by accident.

## Questions buyers ask about adaptation cost

**How much does campaign adaptation cost per market?**

No per-market average exists and no source publishes one. Across six buyer
phrasings, one page in forty-nine puts a price on an adaptation and says what it
includes: 1,350 euro excluding VAT for a 15-second commercial covering new
voice-over, titles, packshot, mix and master, with deliverable files billed
separately at 65 euro each.

**What is a toolkit adaptation?**

One master reworked across N markets and M formats. The useful unit is slots,
markets times formats, so one master across twelve markets in six formats is
seventy-two slots. A quote that does not state its slot count cannot be compared
to another quote.

**Does adaptation cost scale linearly with markets?**

No. Resizes scale with formats, retitling scales with languages, and only
recasting and reshooting scale with markets. Rights step rather than scale: a
recut inside agreed parameters is versioning, while one outside them creates a
new commercial with new fees under the US performers agreement.

**What drives adaptation cost up?**

Scoping every slot at the deepest level, confusing market count with language
count, discovering rights parameters after the master is made, pricing
transcreation by source word when the category prices it by time, and forgetting
deliverable masters, which multiply across markets and formats and are billed
per file.

**What is the difference between versioning and adaptation?**

In practice the words are used interchangeably, which is the problem. This page
splits the work into five levels, resize, retitle, recut, recast and reshoot, so
that a quote can be matched to a level. That ladder is this page's own
definition rather than an industry standard.
