# In-house studio or outsourced production: the fully loaded model

The comparison you have been shown is arithmetic resting on a number nobody
published. Two multipliers decide the whole answer, the benefits load and the
utilization rate, and the pages arguing this question source neither of them.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## Is an in-house studio cheaper than outsourcing?

Unanswerable as posed, and the reason is worth more than a verdict would be.
One side of this comparison publishes a denominator and the other does not.

Outsourced production publishes rates, and at the managed end of the
subscription category you can divide a fee by published hours and get a cost per
hour. In-house has no published denominator anywhere: no statistical agency, no
trade body and no operator publishes what share of a salaried creative's year
reaches finished work. So the honest output of this page is a model you fill in
with your own numbers, plus a clear map of which lines nobody can fill in for
you.

<!-- SECTION: the evidence -->

## Why no usable model exists

Start with who is writing about this, because it explains the shape of
everything you will read.

> Across four buyer phrasings searched in September 2026, 25 of the 34 pages
> returned were published by a company with a commercial interest in the
> answer, and none of the 34 was published by a statistical agency or an
> advertiser trade body. Every page that published a fully loaded annual
> figure did so without citing a source for the benefits multiplier it used,
> and not one stated a utilization rate.
> Source: search-results audit run September 2026 across four buyer phrasings,
> publisher type recorded for every result, no domain named.

Roughly three in four written by someone selling one of the answers is bad
enough. The stronger half of that finding is the zero: not one page on this
subject, across four phrasings, came from a statistical agency or an advertiser
trade body, even though both publish data that bears on it directly.

That is why every fully loaded total you have seen is confident and unsourced.
The multiplier was guessed, and a guessed multiplier compounds across every
seat.

<!-- SECTION: the multiplier -->

## The multiplier the category gets wrong

There is a published figure for this, and it does not say what the category
thinks it says.

> Private-sector employers paid an average of $46.60 an hour worked in total
> compensation in March 2026: $32.60 in wages and salaries and $14.01 in
> benefits, which is 30.1 percent of the total.
> Source: national employer compensation cost series, March 2026 reference
> period, released June 2026, employer cost per hour worked across private
> industry.

Here is where the category goes wrong. "Benefits are about 30 percent" gets
repeated everywhere, and it is a misreading of that release. The 30.1 percent
is benefits as a share of *total compensation*, not as a markup on salary. As a
markup, the same numbers give something quite different.

> Turn that into the multiplier a budget holder actually needs and salary
> understates the real cost by about 43 percent: $46.60 divided by $32.60 is
> 1.43.
> Source: arithmetic on the national employer compensation cost series, March
> 2026 reference period, shown on the page.

That is US private-industry data, and the distinction matters if you are
staffing elsewhere: the ratio is specific to that market and that reference
period. Within it, the circulating multipliers of 1.25 to 1.4 all sit below the
published figure, at every point in their range. Applied to a four-person unit,
using 1.3 instead of 1.43 understates the wage-and-benefit line by roughly forty
thousand dollars a year before anything else is counted.

<!-- SECTION: the model -->

## What does a fully loaded in-house model include?

Three layers. The first is published, the second is arithmetic on the first,
and the third is where the category stops looking.

**Layer one, salaries, which are published.**

> Median annual wages in the national occupational wage series for May 2025
> run $62,960 for graphic designers, $75,420 for film and video editors,
> $74,990 for camera operators and $114,850 for art directors.
> Source: national occupational employment and wage statistics program, May
> 2025 reference period, full establishment survey.

Note the reference period. Those wages are May 2025 and the compensation ratio
above is March 2026, so they are separate instruments and neither should be
described as current in the other's terms.

Medians also hide more than they show here.

> The published spread inside a single creative title is wider than the gap
> between titles. Graphic designers run from under $39,520 at the tenth
> percentile to over $104,910 at the ninetieth in the same May 2025 series.
> Source: national occupational employment and wage statistics program, May
> 2025 reference period.

A designer costs anywhere across a range wider than the distance from designer
to art director. Which means picking a median for your model is already a
judgment call, and a model built on medians is a sketch.

**Layer two, the loaded figure.** Take a four-seat unit at published national
medians: an art director, a film and video editor and two graphic designers.
Salaries total $316,190. Applying the published private-industry multiplier of
1.43 gives about $452,000 a year in wages and benefits alone.

| Line | Source | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Art director, median | May 2025 wage series | $114,850 |
| Film and video editor, median | May 2025 wage series | $75,420 |
| Graphic designer, median | May 2025 wage series | $62,960 |
| Graphic designer, median, second seat | May 2025 wage series | $62,960 |
| Salary subtotal | Sum of the above | $316,190 |
| Wages plus benefits at the published US private-industry ratio | 1.43 multiplier, March 2026 series | about $451,977 |

That $452,000 is higher than most of the fully loaded totals circulating for
teams this size. Read that again, because it is the tell: those totals claim to
include everything and land below a figure that includes only wages and
benefits.

**Layer three, the eight lines with no benchmark.** This table publishes empty
cells on purpose.

| Line | Is there a published benchmark? |
|---|---|
| Recruitment and agency fees to fill each seat | No creative-specific benchmark found |
| Replacement cost when a seat turns over | No creative-specific benchmark found |
| Idle capacity between campaigns | None found on either side of the market |
| Share of the year on trafficking and versioning rather than origination | None found |
| Freelancer surge at peak | None found |
| Software seats per creative | Published only by the software vendors themselves |
| Studio space, equipment, storage and compute | None found as a per-seat benchmark |
| Management overhead above the unit | None found |

Eight lines, every one of them real money, none of them with a number you can
cite. Any page handing you a single fully loaded total has filled these in from
somewhere it is not telling you.

<!-- SECTION: utilization -->

## What utilization rate does an in-house team actually hit?

Nobody knows, and this is the load-bearing gap in the whole comparison.

Utilization bands do circulate, at 60 to 70 percent, 65 to 80, 70 to 80, 75 to
85. Every one found in this research was published by time-tracking software,
agency-management software or an agency financial consultancy, with no sample
and no fielding date. There is a worse problem than the missing method: they
all measure *billable* utilization at an agency, and an in-house studio has no
billable hours. Wrong instrument, wrong population.

So the table below contains no benchmark. **Every row is a hypothetical you
supply, not a measured rate.** Four seats at 2,080 paid hours is 8,320 paid
hours a year. Divide $451,977 by that and you get $54.32 an hour paid. Divide
again by whatever utilization share you believe applies to your own team.

| Utilization you assume, not a benchmark | Cost per productive hour |
|---|---|
| 80 percent | about $68 |
| 60 percent | about $91 |
| 40 percent | about $136 |

Those three rows are illustrations of arithmetic, not findings. Pick the one
matching your own honest estimate, and note how violently the answer moves: the
gap between the top and bottom rows is twice the number itself.

Now the outsourced side, and this comparison has to be handled carefully.

> At the managed end of the category, the monthly fee buys a block of creative
> hours, published at 120 and 180 hours a month on one card read in September
> 2026, with unused hours expiring. Divided out, that prices access at almost
> exactly $50 a creative hour.
> Source: arithmetic on one published rate card in this category, collected
> September 2026, no vendor named.

Set that beside the three rows above and what you have is one published number
against three hypothetical ones. That asymmetry is a fact about disclosure, not
a verdict about cost. The outsourced denominator is knowable because a card
publishes it. The in-house denominator is unknowable because nobody publishes
it, including the companies running the studios. Anyone converting that into
"outsourcing is cheaper" has swapped a claim about evidence for a claim about
money.

<!-- SECTION: the hiring market -->

## The seat you are costing may be harder to fill than to fund

A loaded model prices a seat as though the seat exists. Two published facts
complicate that, and neither appears in the comparisons.

The first is the spread inside a title, already above. A median hides a range
wide enough that two companies hiring "a designer" in the same month can be
buying quite different people at quite different prices, and the model you build
in a spreadsheet assumes you land at the median rather than where the market
actually clears for the person you want.

The second is direction of travel in the occupation itself.

> Graphic design employment is projected to decline about 2 percent between
> 2025 and 2035, a loss of roughly 4,200 positions.
> Source: national employment projections program, 2025 to 2035 projection
> decade.

Be careful what that does and does not say. It is a projection about the
occupation as counted, not a forecast about demand for design, and a 2 percent
decline over a decade is a mild signal rather than a collapse. What it does
suggest is that the shape of in-house teams is unlikely to hold still for the
term of a hiring plan, which is awkward for a fixed cost you are committing to
for years.

Structural churn is already visible on the buying side.

> Two thirds of brands changed their agency model in the previous four years.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> 50 global brands, benchmarked against the same study run in 2021.

Two thirds inside four years is a category that has not settled. Whatever you
build now, the reasonable planning assumption is that you will revisit it before
the depreciation schedule on the equipment runs out, and that argues for keeping
the reversible half of the structure larger than instinct suggests.

<!-- SECTION: fixed vs variable -->

## Fixed, committed, and actually variable

The category collapses this into two categories when there are three, and the
middle one is where the surprises live.

**Fixed** is wages and benefits. They recur whether or not a campaign runs, and
they recur at full rate in your quietest month.

**Committed** looks variable and is not. Subscription floors recur too, and at
the top of the managed band they come with a term.

> The top published managed floor commits the buyer to $192,000 for the year: a
> $15,000 monthly minimum plus a $1,000 monthly software fee, on a twelve-month
> term.
> Source: arithmetic on a published rate card in this category, collected
> September 2026, no vendor named.

A twelve-month commitment is a fixed cost wearing a monthly invoice. It belongs
in the same column as headcount, not in the flexible one.

**Variable** is project-priced production, and it is the only genuinely
variable option of the three. Whether that is the right answer depends on
whether your demand is actually lumpy, which is a question about your calendar
rather than about the models.

| | Recurs when nothing ships | Term commitment | Scales down |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-house headcount | Yes | Notice periods | Slowly, and painfully |
| Subscription | Yes | Up to twelve months at the top band | At renewal |
| Project-priced production | No | None inherent | Immediately |

<!-- SECTION: when in-house is right -->

## When does in-house production make sense?

Often, and for reasons the cost argument usually misses.

In-house wins on proximity, institutional memory and speed on small things. A
team that sits in your building knows the product roadmap, has met the category
manager, and can turn a change around in an afternoon without a scoping call.
None of that shows up in a cost per hour, and it is why in-house adoption keeps
climbing.

> 82 percent of member advertisers reported running an in-house agency, up
> from 78 percent in 2018 and 58 percent in 2013.
> Source: US advertiser trade body in-house agency study, fielded February and
> March 2023, 162 respondents drawn from the body's own advertiser members.

That study was fielded in February and March 2023, on 162 respondents from one
body's own membership, so read it as a direction of travel among advertisers
who join trade bodies rather than as a current census. The multinational picture
is separately measured.

> Among major multinational advertisers, 66 percent report an in-house agency
> and a further 21 percent are considering one, up 16 percentage points on
> 2020, while 56 percent expect to move more digital production in-house.
> Source: global advertiser trade body in-housing research, December 2023, 45
> multinationals with an estimated 60 billion dollars of combined annual
> global ad spend.

But the same body found the workload arriving faster than the structure.

> In the same study, 88 percent of companies said the workload on their
> in-house shop had increased and 67 percent said it had increased a lot.
> Source: US advertiser trade body in-house agency study, fielded February and
> March 2023, 162 respondents drawn from the body's own advertiser members.

Workload up sharply, and the budget it comes out of moving the other way.

> Production averaged 19 percent of total marketing budgets, against 24 percent
> before the pandemic.
> Source: global advertiser trade body content production research, March 2025,
> 50 global brands across more than ten sectors, senior marketing procurement
> respondents.

That squeeze is the actual reason this question keeps being asked, and it is
also why the pure in-house answer strains: a fixed team sized for the average
week is under-sized for launch weeks and over-sized for the rest.

<!-- SECTION: hybrid -->

## What is the hybrid platform and studio model?

The third option, and the one the two-way framing hides. Keep a small senior
in-house team for direction, brand judgment and the work that benefits from
proximity. Put the volume, the surge and the physical capture outside, priced
per delivered asset so it disappears in quiet months.

That split maps onto the fixed and variable table cleanly: the fixed cost is
sized to your floor rather than your peak, and the peak is bought as it
arrives. It also concentrates in-house time on origination rather than on
trafficking and versioning, which is one of the eight unmeasured lines and,
anecdotally, the one that consumes in-house teams.

> Among in-house agency leaders, 65 percent are experimenting with AI and 71
> percent are partially implementing it, while 12 percent describe it as fully
> integrated and 40 percent report faster content production.
> Source: global advertiser trade body research on in-house agencies, November
> 2025, quantitative survey plus 30 in-depth interviews with in-house agency
> leaders.

Note that 12 percent against 71 percent. Most in-house teams are partway
through this, which is exactly the position where a hybrid is easiest to run and
hardest to admit to.

## Questions buyers ask about in-house and outsourced production

**Is an in-house studio cheaper than outsourcing?**

Not answerable from published data. Outsourced production publishes a
denominator, roughly $50 a creative hour at the managed subscription end in
September 2026. In-house has none: no agency, trade body or operator publishes
what share of a salaried creative's year reaches finished work. That is an
asymmetry in disclosure, not evidence that one is cheaper.

**What does a fully loaded in-house model include?**

Salaries, then a benefits load, then eight lines with no published benchmark:
recruitment, replacement on turnover, idle capacity, time on trafficking and
versioning, freelancer surge, software seats, space and equipment, and
management overhead. A four-seat unit at May 2025 national medians reaches
about $452,000 in wages and benefits before any of those eight.

**What utilization rate does an in-house team actually hit?**

Nobody has published one. The bands circulating at 60 to 85 percent come from
software vendors and agency consultancies with no sample or fielding date, and
they measure billable utilization at an agency, which an in-house studio does
not have. Treat any utilization number, including in your own model, as an
assumption you are making.

**When does in-house production make sense?**

When proximity and institutional memory matter more than unit cost, and when
demand is steady rather than spiky. A fixed team sized for the average week is
under-sized at launch and over-sized the rest of the year, which is why the
squeeze on production budgets, down to 19 percent from 24 percent
pre-pandemic, presses hardest on pure in-house structures.

**What is the hybrid platform and studio model?**

A small senior in-house team holding direction and brand judgment, with volume,
surge and physical capture bought outside and priced per delivered asset. The
fixed cost is sized to your floor rather than your peak, and it keeps in-house
time on origination rather than on versioning and trafficking.
