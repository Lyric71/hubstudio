# White-label creative production for agencies: NDA, contact rules, margin per asset

Confidentiality, contact rules, ownership of the work, ownership of any
trained model, disclosure of how the asset was made. Those five belong in the
supplier agreement before the first brief moves. Margin is the sixth question
and it belongs in your own numbers, so this page gives you a structure for it
and no percentage. No open benchmark in this category publishes one with a
method behind it.

A note before anything else. What follows describes production practice, drawn
from the published text of the instruments named in it, each re-read at source
in September 2026. It is not legal advice.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What does white-label creative production for agencies need in writing?

Two contracts, not one. The agency's agreement with its client, and the
agency's agreement with the studio. The second has to grant everything the
first promises: confidentiality, contact rules, ownership of the work and of
any trained model, and disclosure of how the asset was made. hubStudio runs
white-label production on that structure.

Most agreements in this category cover the first two and stop.

| What the client contract promises | What the studio contract has to grant | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| The work is confidential | Confidentiality, flow-down to the studio's own contractors, and the fact of the engagement itself | The studio's freelancers never signed anything naming your client |
| The agency is the only party in the room | A contact rule naming who may speak to whom, and when consent is needed | Nobody defined consent, so a producer emails the client to unblock a file |
| The client owns the deliverable | An assignment broad enough to pass through, not a label | The work-for-hire clause never reached that asset type |
| The client owns the brand's likeness and look | Ownership or a stated license for any model trained on their material | Nobody claimed the model, so the studio keeps it |
| The work can be used as the client intends | Disclosure of generated elements, in a form the client can act on | The client goes to register the asset and finds the gap |

The left column is already promised, usually in a contract signed months ago.
The right column is what has to exist in a second contract for any of it to
survive a production schedule.

<!-- SECTION: The NDA -->

## What should a white-label production NDA cover?

Start with what the word confidential actually covers, work outward to every
person who will touch the files, then decide what happens to all of it when the
job ends. One clause sits outside that sequence, and most NDAs skip it.

| Clause | What it has to say | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|
| Definition of confidential information | The client's name and the existence of the engagement, not just the files | Client identity is what a white-label deal is actually protecting |
| Non-disclosure of the relationship | The studio does not name you, your client, or the work in a deck, a case study, a portfolio, or a post | An NDA silent on this protects the brief and leaks the arrangement |
| Subcontractor flow-down | Advance notice of any subcontractor, identical obligations on them, and the studio liable for their breach | Production runs on freelancers, and an unflowed obligation is no obligation |
| Trade-secret immunity notice | The statutory whistleblower notice, in the studio's own contractor agreements | Without it, exemplary damages and attorney fees are off the table against the person who never got it |
| Personal data | A separate data agreement, named as separate, with the direction of travel stated | An NDA is a confidentiality instrument. It is not a lawful basis for anything |
| Return and destruction | A date, a method, and who certifies it | "On request" is not a retention policy |

That is the trade-secret notice. Short paragraph, real price.

> Notice of the trade-secret immunity must appear in any contract or agreement
> with an employee that governs the use of a trade secret or other confidential
> information, and "employee" includes any individual performing work as a
> contractor or consultant. Without the notice, exemplary damages and attorney
> fees are unavailable against the person who never got it.
> Source: 18 U.S.C. 1833(b)(3) and (b)(4), applying to contracts entered into
> or updated after 11 May 2016, statutory text.

Read that against the flow-down row. Your contract is with a company, so the
notice belongs in the agreements that company signs with the retouchers,
editors, and freelance designers who open the files, which almost nobody checks
during diligence. Ask to see one (redacted is fine). A studio running this
properly has it ready. A studio that has never thought about it hands you its
own NDA again.

Personal data gets its own line because direction matters. Talent captured on a
shoot in China, moving to an agency in Paris, is an export from China, and the
export has conditions.

> Sending personal information out of China requires one of four conditions, a
> regulator-organized security assessment, certification by a professional
> body, a contract on the regulator's standard form, or another condition set
> by law, plus separate consent from the individual and a specified set of
> disclosures about the overseas recipient.
> Source: Personal Information Protection Law of the People's Republic of
> China, Articles 38 and 39, adopted 20 August 2021 and in force 1 November
> 2021, statutory text on the regulator's own surface.

Files moving the other way sit under whichever regime the agency already
answers to, which will not be the same one. Budget for two instruments, a
confidentiality agreement and a data agreement, named for what each does.

## Can a white-label studio contact your client?

Only on terms you set in advance. Put them in a table, circulate it to
producers on both sides, and the question stops being a judgment call made at
eleven at night by whoever happens to be unblocking the file.

| Situation | Allowed | Needs written consent | Never |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joining a client call | Not by default | Yes, as your named team member | Introduced as a separate company |
| Emailing the client | Not by default | Yes, from an address on your domain, on a thread you opened | From the studio's own domain |
| Receiving client feedback | Yes, through your producer, in your tool | Yes, on a shared channel you opened and stay in | Directly, on a channel you cannot see |
| Naming the client internally | Yes, inside the siloed team | Not applicable | In a company-wide channel or an all-hands deck |
| Using the work as a credential | Never | Not available at any price | In any form, including an unbranded portfolio shot |
| Approaching the client for other work | Never | Not available at any price | For the term and an agreed period after it |

That last row is the non-solicitation. Write it as a behavior, not a promise:
say what the studio may not do, name the period, and attach it to a defined
list of clients introduced through the agreement, never to the whole world.
Whether a given wording survives a challenge is a question for counsel in the
governing jurisdiction, and this page does not answer it.

Watch what happens when you put the table in front of a supplier. Pushback on
consent timing, or on which channel feedback runs through, is ordinary and
negotiable. Pushback on the credential row is the one to take seriously. A
studio that needs your work in its portfolio is running a different business
from the one it has been describing to you.

Most of those rows come down to one decision: whose tooling the work runs
through. Brief in your own system and the rules enforce themselves, because
there is no channel for the studio to reach past you on. The hub4you platform
is one way to run that pipeline.

What a studio publishes about this is a fair proxy for how it behaves. The
agencies solutions page on this site commits to staying off the call, off the
credentials deck and off the announcement. Referrals run on a separate
instrument, the partner program, and folding the two into one agreement is how
contact rules get muddled.

## Who owns work made under a white-label agreement?

The two contracts decide, and plenty of them decide badly, because the phrase
every agency reaches for first does not reach most of what an agency buys.

> A specially ordered or commissioned work is a work made for hire only if it
> satisfies all four criteria: it falls within one of nine listed categories,
> there is a written agreement, the parties expressly agree it is a work made
> for hire, and all parties sign. "If a work fails to satisfy any of these
> requirements, it is not a work made for hire."
> Source: US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised
> August 2024, the registering office restating the statutory test.

The nine categories are narrow, and they are the whole test:

- a contribution to a collective work
- a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work
- a translation
- a supplementary work
- a compilation
- an instructional text
- a test
- answer material for a test
- an atlas

Now hold the list against what you actually commission. A film cut is in it. A key
visual, a product shot, a banner set, a packaging render and a social tile are
not, unless each happens to be a contribution to something collective, which is
a thin reed to hang a client promise on. So a clause reading "all deliverables
are works made for hire" does nothing at all across much of the roster.

The sentence after it carries the weight: a present assignment of all right,
title, and interest, with a fallback license if the assignment fails.

Then geography. If the studio sits in China, the default runs the opposite way
from the one most agency contracts assume.

> Ownership of a commissioned work is settled by the contract between the two
> parties. Where the contract does not clearly provide for it, or where there
> is no contract at all, copyright belongs to the commissioned party.
> Source: Copyright Law of the People's Republic of China, Article 19, third
> amendment adopted 11 November 2020, statutory text on the state council's own
> surface.

Silence there is not neutral. It hands copyright to the studio and leaves the
commissioning party holding a use right instead.

> Where copyright in a commissioned work sits with the creator, the
> commissioning party may use the work within the agreed scope, and where no
> scope was agreed, free of charge within the specific purpose of the
> commission.
> Source: Supreme People's Court interpretation on the application of law in
> copyright civil disputes, Article 12, adopted 12 October 2002 and in force
> 15 October 2002, judicial interpretation text.

A use right inside the original purpose is narrower than ownership, and much
narrower than what the agency promised its client.

One more layer, the newest. Generated material may not be property at all.

> "Copyright can protect only material that is the product of human
> creativity." Applicants "have a duty to disclose the inclusion of AI-generated
> content in a work submitted for registration", and AI-generated content that
> is more than de minimis "should be explicitly excluded from the application".
> Source: US Copyright Office registration guidance, 88 FR 16190, 16 March
> 2023, a statement of policy published by the registering office.

An assignment transfers what the assignor holds. It cannot manufacture rights
in material that carries none. So the honest structure is an assignment of
everything assignable, plus a record of which elements were generated and which
a person captured or drew. The disclosure audit trail article on this site
covers that per-asset record.

## The model trained on your client's brand

Nobody thought about it. That is the honest answer in most engagements, and it
resolves quietly in favor of whoever holds the weights.

Four questions settle it:

- who owns the fine-tuned model
- whether the studio may use it, or anything derived from it, for another client
- whether the client's material may train anything general
- what happens to the weights and the training set when the work ends

The one negotiated precedent in the open is a talent instrument, not a supplier
one, so borrow its shape and leave its text alone.

> "Producer may not use or authorize any other party to use the results and
> proceeds of services rendered under the Commercials Contracts to train GAI
> systems without consent of the Union."
> Source: the 2025 commercials agreement between the performers' union and the
> advertiser and agency bargaining body, Section XX.1, Training Data, agreement
> text re-read 21 September 2026.

The shape is consent as the default state, not permission by silence. That
agreement is good on consent generally: it wants a reasonably specific
description of intended uses, and treats any use beyond it as needing fresh
consent. Write the model clause the same way. "Improving our services"
describes nothing.

Deletion needs a date attached to it. The ecommerce design service page on this
site publishes a ninety-day purge of source files from working environments
after project close unless the client asks otherwise, which is studio policy
and not a legal requirement. Whatever your number is, put it in the same clause
as the weights.

## Setting margin without a benchmark to set it against

Not from a benchmark. There is no usable one, and that gap deserves a plain
sentence instead of an invented number.

> Across four buyer phrasings run in September 2026, every ranking result that
> published an agency margin percentage published it without a sample size, a
> fielding window or a population definition.
> Source: search-results audit, September 2026, publisher type and sourcing
> recorded for all 37 results, no domain named.

This is the state of the evidence, not a complaint about blogs. The one place
a real benchmark exists is closed to anyone who does not already feed it.

> The only creative and media agency hourly rate benchmark published by a trade
> body in this category goes to contributing members, in aggregated form.
> Source: the UK advertiser trade body's own published access condition, read
> September 2026.

So build the number from structure instead. The standard form the US
commercial production trade already uses shows where structure puts it.

> The standard commercial production bid form carries one Production Fee line,
> placed after the sub-total of sections A to O and after Insurance, with no
> percentage printed against it, and keeps Contingencies on a separate line
> marked not billed.
> Source: the US commercial production trade body's own bid form, 2023 update,
> summary page read directly.

One line. Named. No rate on it. Steal that design: the fee is visible and its
size is negotiable. Bury it inside the cost lines and a procurement review
eventually finds it there, where it reads as something worse than margin.

| What the line holds | What belongs in it | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier cost | The studio's invoice, at invoice | Anything you added |
| Agency production fee | Your scoping, briefing, review, QC, trafficking, and risk | The supplier's overhead, again |
| Pass-through | Licenses, stock, talent, clearance, at cost with receipts | A quiet uplift |
| Not billed | Contingency, held and released or returned | Contingency treated as revenue |

There is a test for whether the fee is set right, and it needs no benchmark.
Read the line back as a description of work to
someone who was not on the project. If the scoping, briefing, review, QC, and
trafficking behind it are real and specific, the number defends itself in a
procurement review. If the description is thin, no percentage saves it.

Per asset, the arithmetic never changes: the fee divided by what you actually
ship, not what you quoted. A volume deal where half the variants never get
trafficked carries a different per-asset margin from the one in the proposal,
and the difference is worth reconciling at the end of every campaign rather
than at the end of the year.

## Do your clients need to know AI was used?

Assume yes, and put the mechanism in the contract, not in a conversation six
months later. Who carries which duty, as between brand, agency, and tool vendor,
is generally treated as a matter of contract, not something regulation has
settled. Which is the argument for settling it in yours.

Three surfaces already ask the question, and each asks it somewhere different.

| Surface | When it asks | What it wants |
|---|---|---|
| Copyright registration | At the point of filing | Disclosure of generated content, and exclusion of anything more than de minimis |
| The UK advertising code | When the ad runs | No AI-specific rule. The code is media-neutral, so the existing rules apply and the advertiser stays responsible for any AI output used in its ads |
| China's labeling Measures | At the file | A label on the content and a label in the metadata |

Only the third of those reaches production, because it lands on the asset
itself and not on the paperwork around it.

> The labeling Measures set two forms of label. An explicit label is added in
> the generated content or the interface and presented through text, sound or
> graphics a user can perceive. An implicit label is a technical measure added
> to the file metadata of the generated content, and is not easily perceived.
> Source: Measures for Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content, issued by
> the Cyberspace Administration of China and three co-issuing bodies on
> 14 March 2025, in force 1 September 2025, instrument text.

None of it obliges an agency to name the tool it used. It does mean being able
to answer, per asset, in whatever form a regulator or a platform asks for. The
questions to ask an AI production partner article on this site is the version
of that conversation to have before signing.

## Questions agencies ask before signing

**What should a white-label production NDA cover?**

More than most templates carry. The client's name and the existence of the
engagement belong inside the definition of confidential information, not just
the files. The studio must be barred from naming the relationship anywhere, and
obligations have to flow down to its freelancers with the studio liable for
them. Then the trade-secret immunity notice, a separate data agreement, and
return or destruction with a date and a named certifier.

**Can a white-label studio contact my client?**

Only on terms set in advance, and the workable form is a table, not a clause.
Studio staff join calls as your team, email from your domain on threads you
opened, and take feedback through your producer. They never introduce
themselves as a separate company, and never take feedback on a channel you
cannot see.

**Who owns work made under a white-label agreement?**

Whatever the two contracts say, and a work-for-hire label is not enough on its
own. Only nine categories of commissioned work qualify, and most agency
deliverables sit outside them, so the assignment does the real work. If the
studio is in China, say so explicitly. Silence there leaves copyright with the
studio.

**How do agencies set margin on outsourced production?**

From structure, because no open benchmark in this category publishes a method.
Give the production fee its own named line, keep supplier cost and pass-through
at cost with receipts, hold contingency unbilled, and divide the fee by assets
shipped rather than assets quoted.

**Do my clients need to know AI was used?**

Assume yes and build the record. Copyright registration asks applicants to
disclose generated material, the UK advertising regulator treats its code as
media-neutral with the advertiser responsible, and assets running in China
carry a labeling instrument that reaches the file. Who holds which duty is
generally treated as a matter of contract.
