# The EU AI Act labeling duty: what brand content has to carry, and what Instagram, TikTok and YouTube ask for

Since August 2, 2026, a deep fake in a campaign seen in Europe has to carry a
visible AI label. Whoever decided how the AI was used applies it. The platform
labels cover part of that duty, and a different part on each network.

<!-- INTRODUCTION -->

## What does the EU AI Act require for AI-generated content labeling?

Article 50 of the EU AI Act splits the work in two. Providers of generative
engines mark their output in a machine-readable way. Deployers, the brands and
agencies that decide how AI is used, visibly disclose deep fakes and unreviewed
AI text on public-interest matters, from August 2, 2026. hubStudio describes
production practice here, not legal advice.

| Who | What they must do | From | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provider of a generative AI system | Mark audio, image, video and text output as AI-made, machine-readable and detectable | Aug. 2, 2026; Dec. 2, 2026, for systems on sale before Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(2), Art. 111(4) |
| Provider of a system that talks with people | Tell people they are dealing with an AI, unless it is obvious | Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(1) |
| Deployer making a deep fake (image, audio, video) | Disclose that it is AI-generated or manipulated | Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(4), first subparagraph |
| Deployer, in an evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work | Disclose that AI-made content is present, in a way that does not spoil the work | Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(4), first subparagraph |
| Deployer publishing AI text to inform the public on public-interest matters | Disclose, unless a person reviewed it and someone holds editorial responsibility | Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(4), second subparagraph |
| Every deployer above | Make it clear and distinguishable, at the latest at first exposure | Aug. 2, 2026 | Art. 50(5) |

A brand rarely builds the engine. The provider rows are a question for the
engine maker, and for your contract with it. The deployer rows are yours, or
your agency's.

> Article 50 of the EU AI Act applies from August 2, 2026. Providers of
> generative AI systems already on the market before that date have until
> December 2, 2026 to meet the machine-readable marking duty, under Article
> 111(4), added by the AI Omnibus. Content generated before August 2, 2026
> needs no label after the fact.
> Source: AI Act consolidated text, version of July 27, 2026, on the European
> Commission's AI Act Service Desk, read October 8, 2026; the Commission's
> guidelines on Article 50, July 20, 2026, paragraphs 153 and 154.

<!-- SECTION: dates -->

## Did the Digital Omnibus move the Article 50 dates?

One of them, and it's settled law now, not a proposal. The AI Omnibus is
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since July 27, 2026. It left the disclosure
date for deployers at August 2, 2026, and gave providers of engines already on
sale a short grace period for the machine-readable mark.

The negotiation matters only because older pages still print its first number.

> The Commission proposed a six-month grace period for the marking duty in its
> AI Omnibus of November 19, 2025. The Parliament's position cut it to three
> months. The trilogue agreement of May 7, 2026, set December 2, 2026, for
> systems on the market before August 2, 2026, and the Parliament approved the
> deal on June 16, 2026.
> Source: European Parliamentary Research Service, briefing on the Digital
> Omnibus on AI, PE 782.651, June 2026; Regulation number and entry into force
> from the European Commission's AI Act policy page, read October 8, 2026.

Pages that still give providers six months are quoting the proposal, not the
law.

Nothing else is pending on Article 50. A check of the Commission's AI Act pages
and of the open web on October 8, 2026, found no further proposal touching it.
What will move is the guidance around it. The Commission says the code of
practice will get a formal update at least every two years, and it will review
its guidelines as enforcement experience comes in.

<!-- SECTION: who is the deployer -->

## Who has to label: the brand or the agency?

Whoever has authority over how the AI is used. The Commission's guidelines of
July 20, 2026, define that authority as responsibility for the decision to
deploy a system and for the way it is used, outputs included. No technical
control is needed.

That puts the line somewhere many brand teams don't expect.

> A company that merely commissions an advertising agency, without deciding or
> controlling whether and how the agency uses AI, is not a deployer. Where the
> deployer is a company, an advertising company for instance, its staff are
> not separate deployers, and the company stays the deployer when it brings in
> freelancers under its control.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50, C(2026) 5054, July 20,
> 2026, paragraph 14.

Read the other way, a brand that directs the AI use itself (which engine, whose
synthetic face) is taking the decisions, and probably shares the role. Most
campaigns sit somewhere in between. That is why the label belongs in the
contract, down to who applies it and on which file. The guidelines point the
same way, telling deployers in long production chains to secure the label
through contract terms with the partners who distribute the work.

Being outside the EU doesn't help. A company established elsewhere is in scope
when it foresees its content being used in the Union, and the guidelines count
posting a deep fake on the open internet as foreseeing it. Their own example is
a company outside the EU running an AI deep fake of a celebrity in an ad shown
in Europe.

> Breaching Article 50 can draw fines of up to 15 million euros or 3 percent
> of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. For small and
> mid-sized companies, the lower of the two applies.
> Source: AI Act, Article 99(4) and 99(6), consolidated text of July 27, 2026,
> restated in the Commission's guidelines of July 20, 2026, paragraph 152.

## Which brand assets count as deep fakes?

More than faces. The Act defines a deep fake as AI-generated or manipulated
image, audio or video that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities
or events and would falsely appear authentic or truthful. The Commission's
guidelines read "objects" to include consumer goods and "persons" to include
realistic synthetic avatars. Intent doesn't matter. The foreseeable audience
does, children and older viewers included.

The advertising examples are the useful part.

| Asset | Deep fake under the guidelines? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| AI video of a celebrity influencer in a promotion | Yes | Listed as a deep fake example |
| Realistic synthetic avatar of the company's CEO | Yes | Listed as a deep fake example |
| AI product image that makes the product look better than it is | Yes | Can mislead on appearance, characteristics or use |
| Synthetic influencer testing a real sponsored product | Yes, with no creative-work discount | Named as outside the lighter regime |
| Real car shot against an AI-generated background | No, if the car is shown as it is | Named as a non-example |
| Mice arguing about cheese in a cheese ad | No | Not realistic, so it cannot pass as authentic |
| Color correction, re-scaling or an aesthetic background swap on a product shot | Usually not | Minor edits with little effect on how authentic it looks |

Does an ad count as a "creative work" with the lighter disclosure? Sometimes.
The guidelines say advertisements might qualify in certain situations and not in
others, and that where informative and creative content mix, the informative
side wins. A teleshopping-style demo with synthetic shoppers is named as not
creative. Plan for the full label.

Text is narrower. According to the guidelines, ad copy and product descriptions
fall outside the text duty unless they carry claims about health, consumer
safety or sustainability. A sustainability claim drafted by an engine and
published with nobody reviewing it is the case to watch.

## What does a compliant label look like?

The Act asks for "clear and distinguishable" at first exposure. The code of
practice, final since June 10, 2026, turns that into a working spec.

> The label's main element is the capital letters AI. It sits where nothing
> covers it (the code's example is the top right corner of an image or video),
> people see it without tapping anything, and on video it appears at the
> start, again at intervals where possible and at minimum after breaks.
> Audio-only deep fakes open with a short spoken disclaimer. AI text on
> public-interest matters is labeled near the headline.
> Source: Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, Section 2,
> Measures 1.1 and 1.2, final version of June 10, 2026.

The EU's own icon set comes in three versions: one for fully generated content,
one for modified content, and a basic mark. The icons are optional, the
Commission's icon page says, and the labeling is not. Creative works get more
room: the label can sit in the credits, in the notes that come with the work, or
beside the frame.

Two points trip teams up. The first is that the engine's invisible watermark
doesn't count.

> Deployers cannot meet the disclosure duty by relying on the machine-readable
> marking the provider embedded, because people cannot perceive it without a
> tool.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50, July 20, 2026,
> paragraph 117.

The second: the code is voluntary, and it is still the benchmark.

> The Commission and the AI Board judged the code an adequate voluntary tool
> for showing compliance, and about 190 companies and organizations had signed
> it by the end of July 2026.
> Source: European Commission, code of practice page and news release, July
> 31, 2026; the count is the Commission's tally of its own signatory register.

A deployer that doesn't sign is expected to show its own route to compliance, a
gap analysis against the code for instance, and may field more requests for
information from regulators.

## What do Instagram, TikTok and YouTube ask for?

Each platform has its own rule, written for its own reasons. None of the help
pages read for this piece mentions the AI Act, and all of them change without
notice; these were read on October 8, 2026.

| Platform | When a label is required | How it is applied | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram and Facebook posts | Photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio made or altered digitally; not required on images | "Add AI label" toggle when you create a post or reel; Meta also labels content that carries AI signals | Instagram and Meta help, read Oct. 8, 2026 |
| Meta ads | Advertiser disclosure required on social issue, election and political ads | Meta adds "AI info" when its own tools, or detected third-party AI tools, were used; shown in the About this ad menu | Meta help, read Oct. 8, 2026 |
| TikTok | Realistic AI-generated or significantly edited scenes or people | "AI-generated content" setting before posting; auto label from TikTok AI effects or C2PA Content Credentials | TikTok support and guidelines, read Oct. 8, 2026 |
| YouTube | AI used to meaningfully alter or generate photorealistic content | YouTube Studio, Attributes, "AI use": Yes; auto label from YouTube's own tools, C2PA data or detection | YouTube Help, read Oct. 8, 2026 |

> Meta requires a label on content with photorealistic video or
> realistic-sounding audio that was digitally generated or altered, including
> with AI, and does not require one on images, though images still get a label
> when its systems detect AI. It warns there may be penalties for content
> shared without a required label.
> Source: Instagram Help Center, "Label AI content on Instagram," and Meta
> Help Center, "How to identify AI content on Meta products," both read
> October 8, 2026.

> TikTok requires creators to label AI-generated or significantly edited
> content that shows realistic-looking scenes or people, and says unlabeled
> content may be removed, restricted or labeled by its team. A label TikTok
> applies automatically cannot be removed.
> Source: TikTok Community Guidelines, Integrity and Authenticity, and TikTok
> Support, "About AI-generated content," both read October 8, 2026.

> YouTube requires creators to disclose when they use AI to meaningfully alter
> or generate photorealistic content. The label shows in the player for
> photorealistic content and in the expanded description otherwise. Creators
> who consistently fail to disclose may get a label applied by hand, lose the
> content or be suspended from the YouTube Partner Program.
> Source: YouTube Help, "Disclosing use of GenAI content," read October 8,
> 2026.

TikTok adds a rule a label won't fix: it bans AI content that shows public
figures making an endorsement, and the likeness of private adults used without
their permission. The TikTok platform page and the Meta platform page cover how
each network handles brand content more broadly.

<!-- SECTION: where they diverge -->

## Where do the platform labels fall short of the EU duty?

In three places, and each one has a fix.

**Images.** Meta asks for no label on an AI image. The Act does, when the image
is a deep fake: a synthetic model in a real jacket, say, in a feed post seen in
Europe. Turn on Meta's label anyway, or put the AI mark in the picture.

**Menus.** On ads, Meta's "AI info" sits on the About this ad screen behind the
three-dot menu, and only sometimes next to the Sponsored tag. On organic posts
flagged as modified rather than generated, the information sits in the Options
menu.

> Information that is only included in a manual, hidden under layers of menu
> options or buried in terms of use is not provided in a clear and
> distinguishable manner.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50, July 20, 2026,
> paragraph 142.

For a deep fake, don't count on a menu label alone.

**Triggers.** They don't line up one to one. YouTube wants disclosure of "AI
generated extra footage of a real place, like a video of a surfer in Maui for a
promotional travel video," and lets an AI backdrop behind a moving car go
undisclosed. The EU test asks something else: would this falsely look authentic
to the people likely to see it? Run both tests and label to the stricter answer.

The platform tools still do real work.

> Where a very large online platform offers deployers a labeling tool that
> gives a clear and distinguishable disclosure, deployers can rely on it
> within that platform. The deployer keeps the responsibility.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50, July 20, 2026,
> paragraph 126.

The tool has an edge. The same file on a product page or on a screen in a train
station carries no platform label at all. A mark embedded in the master travels
with it.

Automatic labels come from metadata. TikTok and YouTube both read C2PA Content
Credentials, and on YouTube a label triggered that way can't be changed. Whether
those credentials survive your own pipeline is a separate question, taken up in
Content Credentials in a real pipeline.

## What should the workflow check on every asset?

One pass per asset, before it leaves production:

- **Name the deployer.** Who chose to use AI on this asset, and how: the
  brand, the agency, or both? Put it in the brief and the contract.
- **Run the deep fake test.** Does it resemble a real person, product, place
  or event, and could it pass as authentic to the people who will see it?
  Write down the answer and the reason.
- **Check the minor-edit exception.** Color, re-scaling or an aesthetic
  background swap on a real product shot is minor. A product made to look
  better than it is isn't.
- **Check the text.** Is AI copy going out on health, safety, sustainability
  or another public-interest topic with nobody reviewing it? Label it, or name
  the editor responsible.
- **Embed the mark in the master.** On stills, the AI mark in a clear corner.
  On video, at the start and after breaks. On audio-only, a spoken line first.
- **Set each platform's label as well.** Meta's toggle on realistic video and
  audio, and on deep fake images, which Meta doesn't ask for; TikTok's
  AI-generated content setting; YouTube's AI use answer.
- **Keep the engine's marks intact.** Avoid export steps that strip metadata
  you may need later.
- **Keep the record.** Engine, prompt, who approved which version, which label
  went where.

Some of this already sits inside the hubStudio app. On TikTok (Beta), the
publishing step carries TikTok's AI-generated content choice, ticked already
when the clip was rendered with AI in hubStudio. For YouTube, which you publish
by hand in YouTube Studio, the help guide includes answering YouTube's AI
question. History keeps every render with its prompt and engine, and Validation
keeps who approved which version on a thread nobody can delete. The Image editor
places a logo or watermark, enough to set the AI mark on a still. When our
studio makes the work, through the ad creative design service for instance, it
records provenance per asset.

Article 50 sets no record-keeping duty of its own. The record is how you answer
when a regulator, a platform or a client asks, and The AI disclosure audit trail
per asset lists the fields. One more thing a label won't change: copyright. The
guidelines say applying a label has no effect, as such, on whether a deep fake
can be protected, and the Copyright and AI resource covers who owns what.

## How does the EU rule compare with China's labeling rules?

China moved first and asks for more on the file itself.

> China's labeling measures require an explicit label on images and at the
> start of video, plus an implicit label in the file's metadata, and have
> applied since September 1, 2025.
> Source: Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, March 2025, in
> force September 1, 2025, full text read September 24, 2026.

The EU's visible duty is narrower, since it bites on deep fakes. A campaign that
runs in both markets builds to the stricter of the two on each asset, and China
AI labeling rules for production walks through that workflow file by file.

## Questions brands ask about EU AI labeling

**Does the EU AI Act require labels on all AI-generated images?**

No. Engine providers must mark all generated output in a machine-readable way. A
visible label is required from the deployer when the image is a deep fake,
meaning it resembles a real person, object, place or event and could pass as
authentic. A plainly fantastical image needs no visible label. The platform may
still add one.

**When does the EU AI Act labeling requirement start?**

Article 50 has applied since August 2, 2026. Providers of engines on the market
before that date have until December 2, 2026, for the machine-readable mark.
Deep fakes made before August 2, 2026, need no label after the fact, but AI text
generated earlier and published after that date does need one if it falls under
the text rule.

**Who has to label AI content, the brand or the agency?**

The deployer, meaning whoever decides whether and how AI is used. Under the
Commission's guidelines, an agency that makes those calls is the deployer, and a
brand that only commissions the work, with no say over AI use, is not. When both
decide, settle in the contract who applies which label to which file.

**Is a product image made with AI a deep fake?**

It can be. The guidelines treat an AI product image as a deep fake when it can
mislead about the product's appearance, characteristics or use. A real product
on an AI background, shown as it is, is not one, and neither are color
correction or re-scaling. Making the product look better than it is crosses the
line.

**Is the Instagram or TikTok AI label enough for the EU AI Act?**

Inside the platform, it can be, if the label is clear and distinguishable: the
guidelines let deployers rely on a platform's labeling tool there. It does
nothing off the platform, and Meta asks for no label on images at all. Embed the
AI mark in the master file and set the platform label as well.

**Do we have to use the EU AI icon?**

No. The Commission's icons are optional; the labeling is not. The code of
practice asks for a label whose main element is the capital letters AI, placed
where nothing covers it and visible without a tap. The EU set offers a fully
generated version, a modified version and a basic mark, free to use.

**What are the fines for not labeling AI content in the EU?**

Up to 15 million euros or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is
higher, and the lower of the two for small and mid-sized companies. National
market surveillance authorities enforce it. Platform penalties are separate:
removal, restriction or a label applied by the platform itself.
