competition is not weak, it is absent: every ranking page answers a different
purchase.

## R1. What has to be true before anything is looked up

The article is an instrument, so most of it is judgment rather than fact. Only
these claims carry a fact load, and each one is checked below or cut.

1. A software procurement questionnaire does not score a creative production
   studio. Argued from the SERP map above, not a figure.
2. Ownership of a custom-trained brand model is a contract term. It is not
   settled by copyright, because prompt-driven output is not protected.
3. A US registration application carries a duty to disclose AI-generated
   content, and that content is disclaimed rather than claimed.
4. Chinese law requires both a visible label and a metadata label on generated
   image and video, and the label has to survive export.
5. EU transparency duties on synthetic content have a date the buyer can hold a
   vendor to.
6. At least one US state statute puts a disclosure duty on the advertiser, with
   a penalty attached.
7. Consent for a digital replica of a living performer is governed by statute in
   at least one large US market, and by the Civil Code in China.
8. Personal data leaving China crosses defined thresholds that trigger a
   security assessment.
9. There is a published, certifiable management standard a vendor can point at
   instead of saying it takes AI governance seriously.
10. Brands routinely fail to ask who actually performed the work, and there is a
    survey that says so.
11. Production is a measurable share of marketing budget, so the buyer knows
    what is at stake.
12. There is no published benchmark for surge capacity, revision rounds or
    turnaround in creative production. Checked and confirmed absent. The
    capacity question therefore ships with no number attached.

Everything else in the piece is a question, a good answer, a weak answer and a
score. That is the format, and it does not need a citation to be honest.

### Ownership and copyright

> Copyright protects only material that is the product of human creativity, and
> where a generative system produces the expressive elements of a work, that
> material is not protected by copyright.
> Source: US Copyright Office, registration guidance for works containing
> material generated by artificial intelligence, 88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023, a
> statement of policy published by the issuing office.
> https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/html/2023-05321.htm

> Users do not exercise ultimate creative control over how a generative system
> interprets prompts. The guidance puts it this way: prompts "function more like
> instructions to a commissioned artist. They identify what the prompter wishes
> to have depicted, but the machine determines how those instructions are
> implemented."
> Source: US Copyright Office, registration guidance, 88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023.
> https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/html/2023-05321.htm

> Applicants have a duty to disclose the inclusion of AI-generated content in a
> work submitted for registration, and that content is entered in the Material
> Excluded field as generated by artificial intelligence rather than claimed as
> authorship.
> Source: US Copyright Office, registration guidance, 88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023,
> registration instructions section.
> https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/html/2023-05321.htm

> The Office concluded that copyright protection does not extend to the mere
> provision of prompts, that an output is protected where a human author has
> determined sufficient expressive elements, that using a generative system as an
> assistive tool or placing generated material inside a larger human-authored
> work does not defeat protection, and that "the case has not been made for
> changes to existing law to provide additional protection for AI-generated
> outputs."
> Source: US Copyright Office, announcement of Part 2 of its Copyright and
> Artificial Intelligence report, 29 January 2025.
> https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html
