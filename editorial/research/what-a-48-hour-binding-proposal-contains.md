**The bar:** the strongest ranking page (a production company guide, May 2026)
lists nine proposal sections plus assumptions and exclusions, recommends a
change-order process, and cites nothing. Five of the seven fetched pages give
at least one definition of quote, estimate or binding, and not one of the five
names a statute, a regulation, a court or a trade-body instrument. None gives a
revision count with a source. Table count on the ranking pages was not captured
by the fetch tool. To beat the set: the brief's 2,800 words, a decision table
whose every definition carries a dated primary source, a section-by-section
anatomy table, an exclusions checklist tied to the trade body's own bid
documents, the firm-bid-is-a-minimum finding, and the cost bands from the
ledger.

**The gap, in one sentence:** every ranking page tells a supplier how to write
a proposal and asserts what "binding" means, but no page shows a buyer the
published instruments (the federal definition of an offer and of a fixed price,
the trade body's firm bid against cost-plus, its overage rule) that decide what
a proposal actually commits the supplier to.

**H1 consequence.** The ranking pages all miss the definition of binding, so
"binding" belongs in the H1. The working H1, "What a 48-hour binding proposal
contains", does not carry the primary query and puts a hubStudio policy figure
in the headline, where no market source supports 48 hours as a norm. See Notes
for the writer.

## R6. Where sources conflict

**Conflict 1. What "binding" means depends on who is speaking, and the range
is the finding.**

| Source | What binds, and when |
|---|---|
| US federal procurement, FAR 13.004 and 2.101 | A quotation binds nobody. An offer binds the offeror if accepted |
| US household goods moving regulation, 49 CFR 375.403 | A binding estimate binds both parties, only for the services it identifies. Added services reopen it: reaffirm, reissue and sign, or convert to non-binding |
| US commercial production trade body, post-production guidelines, January 2026 | The bid is an estimate on the client's specifications. Once accepted, a firm bid is the minimum contract price, and approved overages sit on top |
| Software vendor blogs on the SERP (quarantined) | A quote is "usually" binding, or binding "once accepted", or not binding until the supplier also accepts. No source for any of the three |

Why they differ: they are describing different moments (before and after
acceptance) and different instruments (a quotation, an estimate written into a
bill of lading, a bid against specifications). What all the primary texts
agree on is narrower and publishable: a price document binds, if at all, only
to the scope it identifies, and a change of scope reopens the price through a
written change. The page must not collapse this into one legal definition.

**Conflict 2. "Firm bid" is not "firm fixed price."** The federal regulation's
firm-fixed-price contract is "not subject to any adjustment on the basis of the
contractor's cost experience". The trade body's firm bid for post-production is
"the minimum contract price", with actual hours (never below hours booked) and
approved overages added. A buyer reading "firm" as "capped" is reading the
wrong document. Publish both side by side. Scope note: the trade body text read
is its post-production guideline; its live-action bidding guide asks "firm
bid, cost-plus, or a blend?" but does not define the terms in the pages read.

**Conflict 3. hubStudio's revision-round wording differs across its own
surfaces.** The pricing page (public) says "three on finalized work, more on
retainer". The company information page (public) says "three revision rounds
are included" with no qualifier. The gated internal quoting tool says three
rounds of fixes per picture on images, and "two rounds of changes on the edit"
on video (src/lib/pricing/video-config.ts:108, `includedRevisionRounds: 2`).
The calculator is not published, so the site's public pages win under SPEC
"When to stop and ask" rule 5. The writer uses the pricing page's qualified
form. **Flag for the site owner:** the public three-round policy and the
internal two-round video default do not match.

**Conflict 4. Payment terms are models, not a norm.** The trade body prints a
75/25 split and in the same document calls it "not a recommendation". hubStudio
publishes deposit and balance on asset orders, milestones on projects, monthly
in advance on retainers, Net 30 on request. No survey of payment terms in
creative production was found. Publish the patterns, never a "typical" split.

**Conflict 5. Ownership defaults run the same way in two jurisdictions, by
different routes.** In the US a commissioned work is owned by the buyer as a
work made for hire only inside nine categories and with a signed written
agreement. In China a commissioned work belongs to the commissioned party
unless the contract says otherwise, and a transfer of economic rights needs a
written contract. Both put the burden on the written proposal or contract. UK
and other jurisdictions were not read (blocked). The page states only the two
read, as production practice, not legal advice.

### Quote, estimate, offer, fixed price

> In US federal purchasing, a quotation is not an offer and cannot be accepted
> to form a binding contract. The contract forms when the supplier accepts the
> buyer's order.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 13.004, effective 13
> March 2026, regulation text read at the official host; it governs federal
> procurement, not private contracts.

> The same regulation defines an offer as a response that, if accepted, would
> bind the offeror to perform the resulting contract.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 2.101 definitions,
> effective 13 March 2026, read at the official host.

> A firm-fixed-price contract carries a price that is not adjusted for what the
> work actually cost the contractor, which puts the cost risk on the contractor.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.202-1, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> Cost-reimbursement contracts pay allowable costs and set an estimate of total
> cost, which works as a ceiling the contractor exceeds at its own risk unless
> the buyer approves.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.301-1, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> A time-and-materials contract pays fixed hourly rates plus actual materials,
> provides no positive profit incentive for efficiency, and may be used only
> when the extent or duration of the work cannot be estimated with any
> reasonable confidence.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 16.601, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> Federal service contracts are told to describe the work in terms of the
> required results, not how it will be done or how many hours it will take, and
> to measure performance against stated standards.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, section 37.602, effective 13
> March 2026, read at the official host.

> Under the federal changes clause for fixed-price contracts, a change within
> scope is ordered in writing, an equitable adjustment is made to price,
> schedule or both, and the contractor has 30 days from the written order to
> assert its right to that adjustment.
> Source: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation, clause 52.243-1, dated August
> 1987, read at the official host; a government contract clause, shown as a
> pattern.

> The one US federal regulation read that defines a binding estimate, written
> for interstate household movers, requires it to say on its face that it binds
> both parties and covers only the services it names. Extra services reopen it:
> the mover reaffirms it, writes a new one the customer signs, or both agree in
> writing to treat it as non-binding.
> Source: 49 CFR 375.403, current eCFR text read 10 September 2026; a different
> industry, cited only to show what "binding" means where a regulator has
> defined it.
