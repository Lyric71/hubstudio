**The structural point the writer must not blur.** Section 927 does not
require anything. It withdraws enforceability from a clause that fails all
three conditions at once, and only as to a new performance fixed on or after
1 January 2025. The three conditions are cumulative. A clause with a
reasonably specific description survives even where the person had no counsel
and no union, and a clause negotiated by the person's own lawyer with the
commercial terms stated clearly and conspicuously survives even if the
description is thin. Write it as a test the clause has to pass, never as
"California requires X".

**What "reasonably specific" has been read to mean.** No California case
applying 927 was found in this session, and none is cited here. The only
authoritative reading located is the parties' own reading inside the union
commercials agreement, which uses the identical phrase and then defines it by
worked example. That is set out in the next block. Present it as what the
bargaining parties agreed the phrase means in their own agreement, not as a
judicial construction of the statute.

**The specificity test, as the parties themselves apply it.** The agreement
carries a worked example. A performer is engaged for a yogurt commercial. The
producer supplies this description 48 hours in advance:

"Performer agrees that Producer may create and use Performer's Digital Replica
to generate a future performance in which Performer's character floats in order
to represent the lightness of advertiser's yogurt."

The agreement then explains that a description is reasonably specific if it
conveys information consistent with what a performer would receive at the time
of casting and audition, including scripts and storyboards, and that
performances showing the person flying or riding a magic carpet, or other
visual or aural elements conveying floating, flying or lightness, are all
inside that description. Any use not consented to in the description requires
additional consent and a new description. Editing the finished commercial into
shorter, longer or same-length versions, permitted edits, paid edits or
addressable edits does not require fresh notice and consent, provided the
applicable edit fees are paid.

That example is the most concrete published reading of "reasonably specific"
found anywhere in this research. It is a bargained reading inside one
agreement, not a court's reading of the statute. Say so on the page.

**The range, published as a range, per R6.** The two jurisdictions verified on
primary text set the postmortem term at 70 years in California and 40 years in
New York, and they differ on the trigger: California asks whether the name,
voice, signature, photograph or likeness had commercial value at death; New
York adds a domicile condition, the person must have been domiciled in the
state at the time of death, and it conditions the claim on prior registration.
Other states set other terms. None of those other terms was verified on a
primary surface in this session, so the article publishes the verified span and
says the span is wider elsewhere without naming a number it did not read.

**Who holds the right.** In California the right is property, transferable by
contract, trust or will, and where it was never transferred it descends by the
statutory ladder. That is the operational point for a brand: the counterparty
for a deceased personality is whoever holds the transferred right or the
statutory successor, not the estate in the abstract, and in both verified
states there is a registration step in the picture.

**Why this belongs in a contract article.** Article 14 is the only provision in
this whole research file that makes a consent step a regulatory duty in the
tooling itself rather than a term the parties negotiate. It is aimed at
providers of face and voice editing functions, not at brands, and the article
must say so. What it means for the buyer is that a digital human built in China
from a real person's face or voice sits inside a regime where separate consent
is the expected artifact, so a release that bundles replica consent into a
general grant is out of step with the surrounding rules even where it is not
itself the addressee.

**China portrait and voice rights, already in the ledger.** Civil Code
Articles 1019, 1021 and 1022 were cleared for brief 013: a likeness may not be
made, used or published without consent; a disputed licence clause is construed
in favor of the person portrayed; an unagreed or unclear term is terminable on
reasonable notice. That construction rule is the single most useful sentence in
this file for a drafting article, because it tells a buyer that a vague grant
does not quietly expand in China, it collapses. Reuse the logged citation and

## The clause map, clause by clause

This is the article's spine. Each row states what the clause controls, what a
weak version leaves open, and which instrument or agreement the safety test
comes from. Rows marked "practice" have no instrument behind them and must be
presented as production practice.

| Clause | What it controls | What a weak version leaves open | Traceable to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grant and scope | Whether the brand may create a replica at all, and from what source material | A grant of "name, image and likeness" with no replica language leaves the replica route arguable in both directions | Labor Code 927(a)(1); union agreement Section XX.A definition; Exhibit 3 Section 1 creation routes |
| Reasonably specific description of intended uses | Everything. It is the field the statute and the agreement both turn on | "All media now known or hereafter devised" is the opposite of specific and is the clause 927 was written about | Labor Code 927(a)(2)(A) and the carve-out at (a)(2)(B); union agreement Section XX.C.2 and its worked example |
| Representation condition | Whether the specificity failure is curable | A release signed by an unrepresented person with a thin description fails all three conditions of 927 at once | Labor Code 927(a)(3)(A) and (B) |
| Term | How long the licence runs, separately from how long the model may exist | A term tied to "the campaign" with no outer date leaves the model alive after the ads stop | Union agreement Section XX.C.1.c, maximum period of use, in no event less than six months; Section XX.D.6.b retention |
| Territory | Which markets the replica may run in, which decides which disclosure regime attaches | "Worldwide" pulls in every labeling regime at once and the brand inherits all of them | practice, driven by the disclosure instruments in brief 024 and the China provisions above |
| Permitted uses and media | Whether a new placement is inside the grant or is a new use | Silence turns every new placement into a negotiation or a dispute | Union agreement Section XX.C.2, a use beyond the description needs new consent and a new description |
| New use trigger | What counts as a new performance rather than a version of an old one | Without it, an edit and a newly generated scene are treated the same | Union agreement worked example: shorter, longer and same-length versions, permitted, paid and addressable edits are not new consent events; a newly generated performance is |
| Alteration limits | Whether the brand may change the person's physical traits, state of dress, or put them in an offensive scene | An open alteration right is where a likeness deal becomes a reputation problem | Union agreement Section XX.C.3 |
| Training and retraining rights | Whether the captured material may be used to train or improve any model | Silence here is the single most valuable thing a weak release gives away, because the asset outlives the campaign inside a model | Union agreement Section XX.1.A.4, no training on results and proceeds without union consent |
| Prompting rights | Whether a real person's name, image or recording may be used in a prompt to build a supposedly synthetic character | The route by which a "fully synthetic" character quietly becomes a replica of someone | Union agreement Section XX.1.A.3, consent plus bargained payment at not less than minimum session fee |
| Derivative and transfer rights | Whether the replica may be assigned with the campaign, and on what conditions | An unrestricted assignment moves the person's likeness to a party they never met | Union agreement Section XX.D.6.c and the Transfer of Rights Assumption Agreement |
| Compensation model | Whether generation is paid like a performance or like a software licence | A one-time buyout for unlimited generation is the deal the union agreement specifically prices differently | Union agreement Section XX.D.1 to D.3: creation time is work time, generation pays 1.5 scale session fees per commercial, use and holding fees as if performed in person |
| Revocation and suspension | Whether consent can be withdrawn, and what happens to work in flight | Silence means consent is effectively permanent | Union agreement Section XX.C.5 suspends consent during a strike. Broader withdrawal rights are practice, not instrument |
| Security and access | Who may touch the model file | An unrestricted access right makes every downstream vendor a rights risk | Union agreement Section XX.D.6.a, legitimate business need plus commercially reasonable protection |
| Retention | How long the model may be kept after the last use | Without it the model persists by default | Union agreement Section XX.D.6.b: during the maximum period of use; written bargained consent to hold longer; 24 months where no commercial was made, extendable in further 24 month periods |
| Sunset and deletion | The end of the model, evidenced | "We will delete it" with no certificate and no deadline is not a deletion clause | Union agreement Section XX.D.7: permanent and irrevocable deletion of all copies, with written certification to the performer within 90 days, and no requirement to destroy commercials already produced |
| Survival after death | Whether consent continues, and who speaks if it does not | Silence turns a routine renewal into an estate negotiation | Union agreement Section XX.C.4; California Civil Code 3344.1(b), (d), (g); New York Civil Rights Law 50-f |
| Disclosure and labeling cooperation | Who applies the label, and who holds the evidence that it was applied | A perfect contract does not discharge a disclosure duty that attaches to the ad | New York GBL 396-b as amended; EU AI Act Article 50; China labeling Measures; China deep synthesis Provisions Articles 17 and 18 |

## Talent type: how the paperwork changes

| Type | What it is | What the paperwork has to carry | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scanned real person | A model built from a named individual's capture or prior performance | Full clause map. The specificity test, the representation condition, retention, deletion, survival after death | Treating the scan session as part of the shoot rather than as its own consent event with its own description |
| Composite | An output blended from more than one person's material, or from a person plus generated material | Every contributing person's consent, each with its own description, plus a record of which inputs went in | The composite is still "readily identifiable" as someone if the blend is thin, and the statutory definitions turn on identifiability, not on intent |
| Fully synthetic character | An asset that creates the impression of a human who is not recognizable as any identifiable person | No talent release for a person who does not exist. What it needs instead is proof of how it was made, and the disclosure duty that attaches to the ad | Prompting with a real person's name, image or recording. Under the union agreement that pulls the character back into consent and payment territory, and it is the mechanism by which a synthetic character stops being synthetic |
