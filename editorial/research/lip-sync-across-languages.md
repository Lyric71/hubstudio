**The bar:** vendor listicles and cost comparisons with tables of named tools.
None carries a timing threshold from a standards body, a peer-reviewed human
dubbing finding, a checklist a non-speaker can run, statute text on voice, or
arithmetic on a published price. Word counts were not measured, so no length
figure is claimed. To beat the set: four tables (failure modes, per-language
check, consent chain, cost bands), every threshold from a standards document,
every price band with its arithmetic, and zero named tools.

**The gap, in one sentence:** Thirty-three results across four buyer phrasings
are almost all published by companies selling AI dubbing or lip-sync tools, so
no ranking page gives a buyer the broadcast timing thresholds, the peer-reviewed
finding that professional dubbers treat mouth-shape matching as a soft
constraint, a per-language check someone who does not speak the language can
run, a consent chain built on statute text, or a cost figure with its arithmetic
shown.

**H1 consequence:** the approved H1, "Lip sync across languages: what breaks and
what it costs", already names the two things the set sells around rather than
answers. Keep it. The per-language check is the piece nobody publishes, so it
earns its own H2 in the buyer's words: "How to check lip sync in a language you
do not speak".

## R6. Where sources conflict

**Sound and picture timing: ITU against EBU.** The ITU recommendation gives
detectability at about +45 to -125 ms and acceptability at about +90 to -185 ms,
averaged from subjective tests described with tube cameras and a newsreader. The
EBU recommendation sets 40 ms sound-early and 60 ms sound-late at the broadcast
output, placed where lip-sync failure becomes perceptible to 50 percent of
observers, and notes that "particular care needs to be taken in HD production".
The two measure different things (an average detectability or acceptability
threshold, against the delay half of observers notice) at different times (1998
against 2007). Publish both, with the range: sound early 40 to 90 ms, sound late
60 to 185 ms, and state that both put the tighter tolerance on sound arriving
early. A slip of one frame at 25 fps (40 ms) already reaches the EBU sound-early
limit.

**Script length: interface strings against dubbed dialogue.** W3C reproduces
average expansion for English interface strings translated into European
languages, with short strings expanding the most, and one example where Korean
comes out shorter than English. The 2023 dubbing study measured spoken dialogue
lines adapted by professional dubbers and found German skewing longer and
Spanish skewing shorter, with most long lines moving by more than 10 percent in
either direction. They conflict because the populations differ: UI labels
cannot be rewritten to fit, dialogue is rewritten constantly. The page may not
publish a single expansion figure for scripts. Publish both, scoped, and make
the practical point that length changes in both directions by language.

**How much lip sync matters.** The broadcast standards treat a global timing
offset as a hard tolerance. The dubbing study shows professionals treat
mouth-shape matching as soft (12.4 percent same-viseme time, a lower bound). The
2020 human evaluation shows viewers preferred an unsynced original over a synced
version full of visual errors. These do not contradict each other: offset is
global and easy to see, viseme match is local and often forgiven, and visible
artifacts cost more than imperfect shapes. The page should say exactly that and
no more.

**Tool price pages against themselves.** One credit card gives 2,000 to 10,000
credits a dubbing minute in its FAQ and 13,500 in a tooltip. One per-second card
gives a flat $0.05 in its FAQ and a $0.025 to $0.16675 per-model table. One
minute card says enhanced lip-sync pricing is in beta and shows a countdown to a
price change. Publish the full spread, dated, and say the pages disagree with
themselves.

**Tool price pages against the ranking blogs.** A seller's March 2026 blog puts
AI dubbing at "$2 to $20 per minute" with no source; the four price pages read
put dubbing at roughly $0.33 to $3.00 a minute. Search summaries of other
seller blogs gave "$0.55 and $2.40 per minute" entry pricing, which happens to
match two derived figures above but was never read at source. The price pages
win. The blogs go under Do not publish.

### How lip sync is measured

> The standard automatic lip-sync scores trace back to a network trained on
> several hundred hours of broadcast news to judge whether mouth movement and
> speech belong together. Checked by hand against held-out clips, it landed
> inside the range a human cannot detect on 81 percent of single 0.2-second
> samples and on more than 99 percent of samples averaged over a whole clip.
> Source: University of Oxford Visual Geometry Group, "Out of time: automated
> lip sync in the wild", 2016. Self-supervised training on news footage,
> evaluated manually on several hundred held-out clips.
> https://www.robots.ox.ac.uk/~vgg/publications/2016/Chung16a/chung16a.pdf

> A 2020 peer-reviewed paper turned that network into the two scores most lip-sync
> research still reports: a distance between the mouth and audio
> representations, where lower means a closer match, and a confidence score,
> where higher means a closer match.
> Source: ACM Multimedia 2020 conference paper, October 2020. Scores computed
> with a pre-trained synchronization network on test sets drawn from three public
> lip-reading datasets plus a collected real-world set.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010

> Current research adds separate measures for what sync scores miss: a video
> quality score for temporal consistency, a similarity score to quantify whether
> the person still looks like themselves, and a success rate for clips that pass
> human verification.
> Source: NeurIPS 2025 conference paper, revised September 2025. Metric
> definitions stated in the paper's evaluation protocol.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21448

> People have tested this, not just software. In the 2020 study, 14 evaluators
> rated real-world clips, including dubbed video out of sync with its translated
> speech, for sync accuracy, visual quality and overall experience on a
> one-to-five scale. Earlier methods that left out-of-sync segments were
> preferred less than the untouched unsynced video, because the original still
> looked right.
> Source: ACM Multimedia 2020 conference paper, October 2020. Fourteen
> evaluators rating each class of video separately on 1 to 5 scales, plus a
> single-choice preference vote. https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010

### How much drift viewers notice

> Broadcast engineers measured how far sound and picture can drift apart before
> viewers notice. Subjective tests summarized by the international
> telecommunications standards body put the detectability threshold at about
> 45 milliseconds with sound ahead of the picture and about 125 milliseconds with
> sound behind it, and the acceptability threshold at about 90 and 185
> milliseconds.
> Source: International Telecommunication Union, Recommendation ITU-R BT.1359-1,
> November 1998, still listed in force in September 2026. Expert and non-expert
> assessors rated newsreader footage under the double stimulus impairment scale
> method. https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BT.1359/en

> The European Broadcasting Union sets a tighter limit at the broadcast output:
> sound no more than 40 milliseconds before the picture or 60 milliseconds after
> it, the delays at which a mismatch between lip movements and speech becomes
> perceptible to half of observers.
> Source: European Broadcasting Union, Technical Recommendation R37, February
> 2007. Limits set from subjective tests of the delay perceptible to 50 percent
> of observers under the union's standard viewing conditions.
> https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r037.pdf

> At 25 frames a second a single frame lasts 40 milliseconds, so an audio track
> that slips one frame early already sits at the European broadcast limit for
> sound arriving ahead of the picture.
> Source: arithmetic on European Broadcasting Union Technical Recommendation
> R37, February 2007. One second divided by 25 frames is 40 milliseconds.
> https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r037.pdf

### Why mouth shapes fail, and why some sounds are checkable by eye

> Mouth shapes do not map one-to-one onto speech sounds. "A phoneme falls into
> one viseme class but a viseme may represent many phonemes: a many to one
> mapping." The classic groupings put p, b and m in one visual class, lips
> closed, and f and v in another, lip against teeth.
> Source: Speech Communication, special issue on audio-visual expressive speech,
> 2017, peer reviewed. 120 phoneme-to-viseme maps compared by training lipreading
> classifiers on British English speakers from two audio-visual datasets.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02934

> Professional dubbing matches mouth shapes far less than buyers assume. Across
> 319.57 hours of professionally produced series dubbed from English into German
> and Spanish, only about 12.4 percent of on-screen speech time showed the same
> mouth shape on the original and dubbed tracks. The researchers call lip sync
> "a fairly soft constraint" and say the figure is likely a lower bound.
> Source: Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, volume
> 11, 2023, peer reviewed. Speech sounds aligned automatically, mapped to viseme
> classes, then compared between source and dub across 54 titles.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.12137

> In the same corpus, dubbed and original speech overlapped for a median 73.1
> percent of each line's speaking time, and on-screen lines were only 3.3 percent
> more tightly timed than off-screen ones. Forced to choose, professional dubbers
> "appear more willing to break timing constraints than vary speaking rate."
> Source: Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, volume
> 11, 2023. Overlap measured per dialogue line as shared speaking time divided by
> total speaking time of source and dub. https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.12137

> A 2025 peer-reviewed benchmark built 615 AI-generated clips specifically to
> stress lip-sync methods with "large facial movements, profile views, variable
> lighting, occlusions, and stylized characters," and reports that earlier
> methods "struggle with head pose variations, identity preservation, and
> artifact elimination."
> Source: NeurIPS 2025 conference paper, revised September 2025. A 615-clip
> benchmark of generated video plus the authors' visual comparison of existing
> methods; no failure rate is published.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21448

> The same paper names lip shape leakage, where "original lip movements from
> source videos persist in the synchronized output," and says the sounds hit
> hardest are those "requiring distinct mouth shapes (such as bilabial or
> labiodental sounds)," the p, b, m and f, v families.
> Source: NeurIPS 2025 conference paper, revised September 2025, appendix on lip
> shape leakage. The authors' qualitative analysis of generated examples.
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21448

> Sync and picture quality pull against each other. The 2020 study found that
> forcing accurate lip shapes "sometimes results in the morphed regions to be
> slightly blurry or contain slight artifacts," and the 2025 study found that
> stronger guidance toward the audio gave precise lips with texture artifacts,
> while weaker guidance kept the picture clean but the lips less precise.
> Source: ACM Multimedia 2020 conference paper, October 2020, and NeurIPS 2025
> conference paper, September 2025. Each is the authors' own observation from
> training and ablation. https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010 and
> https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21448
