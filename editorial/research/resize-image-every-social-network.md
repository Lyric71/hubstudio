**The bar:** tool pages carry a short block of copy around an upload box;
the cheat sheets are long pages built on one large size table with no source
column (lengths judged from the result pages, not counted). None carries a crop-or-fit decision table, a composition rule, a
per-network list of what the interface covers, or a pre-post checklist. Two
tables and one checklist with sources clears every result.

**The gap, in one sentence:** see the table above; the H1 is the composition
answer the tool pages skip, and the size table carries a source per row.

## R1. What has to be true (mapped before looking anything up)

1. Which sizes each network's own pages publish for a feed picture, a link
   picture, a vertical frame and a thumbnail.
2. Whether one shape can serve several feeds at once.
3. What each network does to a picture outside its range (crop, shrink,
   enlarge).
4. Where each network covers part of a picture, from its own pages.
5. File weight limits and accepted formats per network.
6. Every Image editor behavior the piece describes, from the help center.
7. The Image studio facts for making the master (Shape, resolution).
8. Arithmetic used to explain the master composition, labeled as derived.

## Cleared for use

> Instagram keeps a photo at its original resolution when it is between 320
> and 1,080 pixels wide and its ratio sits between 1.91:1 and 3:4. A ratio
> outside that range "will be cropped to fit a supported ratio," and a wider
> file is sized down to 1,080 pixels.
> Source: Instagram Help Center, image resolution article, read October 15, 2026.

> LinkedIn takes photos from 3:1 to 4:5, recommends 1,080 pixels wide and caps
> an upload at 5 MB. A taller photo "will be centered and cropped to fit the
> max ratio," and a photo can't be resized once it is posted.
> Source: LinkedIn Help, "Share photos on LinkedIn," read October 15, 2026.

> X shows a single photo in full when its ratio sits anywhere from 2:1 to 3:4,
> and says the composer shows what you will get. Photos stop at 5MB, in GIF,
> JPEG or PNG.
> Source: X Help Center, "How to post photos or GIFs on X," read October 14, 2026.

> For a shared link, Meta asks for at least 1,200 by 630 pixels, as close to
> 1.91:1 as possible, to show the full image in the feed without cropping.
> LinkedIn's Page help asks for 1.91:1 at 1,200 by 627.
> Source: Meta for Developers, "Images in Link Shares," and LinkedIn Help,
> "Image specifications for your LinkedIn Pages," read October 15, 2026.

> YouTube recommends custom video thumbnails at 3,840 by 2,160 pixels in 16:9,
> at least 640 pixels wide, JPG or PNG, under 2 MB from a phone or 50MB from a
> computer.
> Source: YouTube Help, "Add custom thumbnails on YouTube," read October 15, 2026.

> Meta's ads guide asks Reels ads for 9:16 at 1,440 by 2,560 pixels and
> suggests leaving at least 14 percent of the top, 35 percent of the bottom and
> 6 percent of each side free of text and logos.
> Source: Meta Ads Guide, Instagram Reels video ad specs, read October 15, 2026.

> On TikTok's standard in-feed template, a 720 by 1,280 frame, the caption and
> buttons cover 160 pixels at the top, 440 at the bottom and a column 120
> pixels wide on the right from 560 pixels down.
> Source: TikTok's In-Feed Standard template, linked from TikTok's auction
> in-feed ad specs, measured October 8, 2026.

> X warns that 60 pixels at the top and bottom of a 1,500 by 500 header could
> be cropped on some screens. LinkedIn asks for key details away from the
> edges of a Page cover, especially the lower-right corner. YouTube's banner
> keeps text and logos safe in 1,235 by 338 pixels at the 2,048 by 1,152
> minimum.
> Source: X Help Center, LinkedIn Help and YouTube Help, read October 8 to 15, 2026.

> Facebook caps a Page photo at 10MB and recommends keeping a PNG under 1MB
> "or the image may appear pixelated."
> Source: Meta for Developers, Page Photos reference, read October 15, 2026.
