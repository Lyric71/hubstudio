## Constraint banner, read before drafting

Four limits apply to everything below.

1. **No competitor, no vendor, no tool, no agency reaches the page.** The SERP
   tables below name domains because the spec's table shape requires it. Not
   one of those domains, brands, product names or agency names may appear in
   the article, in a caption, in the FAQ or in the commit message. The angle
   critiques the translation-pricing framing, so the temptation to name a
   translation or localization vendor is high. Work on the pattern. Say
   "a translation quote", "a per-word quote", "pages that price this query",
   never a company.
2. **No hubStudio rate, monthly figure or per-item price.** The article prices
   a market, not a studio. Send every hubStudio number to the calculator and
   the first call.
3. **Currency figures on the page: flagged, not settled.** The editorial rule
   says publish market figures and never hubStudio rate figures, and it gives
   a worked example that carries dollar bands. A standing project note also
   says strip dollar amounts and rate ranges from public hubstudio.ai pages.
   These two pull in opposite directions on a money page whose whole job is to
   price a stack. The reading applied here: a sourced, dated, category-level
   third-party band is publishable; anything that could be read as hubStudio's
   own price is not. Every currency figure cleared below is third-party,
   dated and attributed to a category. **Reviewer decides before publish.**
4. **Platform pixel specs are blocked.** The ledger's hard block on the Tmall
   file size cap and product fill ratio stands, and every Chinese source found
   for main-image dimensions in this session was a design blog restating the
   rule, not the rule. No pixel number, no file size cap, no fill ratio goes
   on this page. The re-crop argument is made from ratio arithmetic and from
   named legal instruments instead, both of which hold without a spec table.

### The verdict on the spec's SERP claim

**Confirmed, and one correction.** The spec says the SERP is translation SaaS
and game-localization vendors. Across five phrasings that is right for roughly
half the results. The other half is a category the spec did not name: **China
market-entry agency blogs that price media and retainers, not production.**
The correction matters, because those pages do carry currency figures, so a
reader who lands there believes they have seen a cost answer. They have seen a
media budget with the production line missing.

Not one page in forty-one results itemizes a production stack. Not one names
an aspect ratio, a portrait-right instrument, an absolute-terms rule, an AI
label duty, or a rework loop. Three of five phrasings return at least one page
older than 2023, and roughly two thirds of the pages carry no visible date at
all.

**The bar.** These pages run roughly 1,200 to 2,500 words, carry zero to one
table, and cite nothing dated. The brief's 3,000-word target with a decision
table, a cost-band section and a FAQ clears the field comfortably. Depth, not
length, is the differentiator: the bar to beat is one itemized table with
dated sources, and no page on this SERP has one.

**The gap, in one sentence.** Every page holding this query is published by a
company that sells words, prices the job per source word or per language pair,
and never itemizes the physical production lines a China adaptation actually
forces: new capture, model recasting under Chinese portrait-right law, a
separate layout per platform frame, on-image text rewritten to survive the
Advertising Law's absolute-terms rule, an AI label written into every
generated file, and the rework a rejected upload creates.

**Why this matters to a cost stack, and the exact limit of the claim.** These

article must not say that. The instrument constrains rights, not casting.

**How to use these.** They are arithmetic, not measurements, and the page must

without publishing a platform pixel spec the ledger blocks. The argument runs:
whatever the exact frame a given surface wants, a wide master surrenders half
to three quarters of its width to reach a tall one, so the subject placement,
the negative space that carried the headline, and the product's position in
frame were all composed for a picture that no longer exists.

## Why a Western master usually cannot be re-cropped, the four reasons that cleared

Ranked by how well they are sourced. The writer builds the section in this
order, strongest first.

| Reason | What it rests on | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| The typography has nowhere to live | Ratio arithmetic above. A master composed on thirds for a wide frame loses the negative space that held the headline when it is cut to a tall one, so the layout is rebuilt, not re-cropped | derived, stated as arithmetic |
| The on-image text is a legal object in China, not a design element | Advertising Law Article 9(3) plus Article 57's 200,000 to 1,000,000 RMB band, plus the 2023 SAMR enforcement guidelines and the 2024 Beijing implementing guideline. Superlative claims baked into a Western master have to come off the artwork, which means re-typesetting and re-exporting every size | primary |
| The ad has to declare itself | Internet Advertising Measures Article 9. A content-shaped asset carrying a purchase link must be conspicuously marked 广告. That mark is a layout element the master never had, and it changes the safe area on every frame | primary |
| The likeness rights do not travel | Civil Code Articles 1019, 1021 and 1022. Consent is required for use, ambiguity is construed against the buyer, and an unclear term is terminable. A Western release rarely names Chinese platforms, Chinese territory or a Chinese term, so the choice is re-clearance or recasting | triangulated |

Note for the writer: **casting expectation is deliberately not on this list.**
No source was found for the claim that Chinese audiences respond better to
locally cast models, so the recast line is argued on rights and on production
logistics, never on audience preference.

## Anchors for the two worked examples

The brief asks for two worked examples at different budget levels. Three
anchors cleared, and the examples are built from these and nothing else.

**Anchor 1: the capture line, priced in the Chinese market.** A Chinese
commercial photography studio's published 2026 rate card, dated 20 July 2026,
listing per-image bands from 80 RMB for flat lay to 300 RMB for in-studio
model work, retouching at 50 to 200 RMB per image, short video at 3,000 to
15,000 RMB per piece, on a five-style minimum, described by its publisher as
its own executing rates for the Guangzhou and Pearl River Delta region.
Single-source, so it is published as one published rate card with its region
and its date stated, never as a market average.

**Anchor 2: the layout line, published as a conflict.** Chinese rate cards for
ecommerce detail-page design disagree by roughly an order of magnitude, from
200 to 300 RMB at the low end of one 2023 page to 1,500 to 5,000 RMB per set
on a 2026 page. Per R6 the article publishes the range and says why they
conflict: the pages do not agree on what a set contains, none states a sample,
none states a method, and at least one is published by a seller of the cheaper
alternative. That disagreement is itself the story, because it is exactly what
a buyer hits when they ask for a quote.

**Anchor 3: the share of budget the whole stack comes out of.** Production
averaged 19 percent of total marketing budgets against 24 percent
pre-pandemic, from a 50-brand survey of senior marketing procurement
respondents, March 2025. This is the frame for both examples: a China
adaptation is drawn on the production line, which is the line that shrank.

**What the examples must not do.** They must not total to a headline number
that reads as a hubStudio quote. Build them as itemized stacks where each line
carries its own attribution, show the compliance and QA lines as structural
costs with no published benchmark, and let the reader add them up.
