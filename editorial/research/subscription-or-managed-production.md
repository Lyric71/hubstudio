### The bar

- **Length:** ranking pages run roughly 1,200 to 2,200 words. The brief's 2,700 clears it.
- **Depth:** shallow. Feature lists and tier tables, no arithmetic anywhere.
- **Tables:** most pages carry one feature-comparison table. Three substantive
  tables plus one worked calculation beats every page found.
- **Dates:** most ranking pages carry no visible publication date, which is
  itself an angle for a dated, methodful piece.

**The gap, in one sentence:** Every page across all four SERPs compares the two
models on features, price tags and speed, and none starts from the unit of
purchase, access to designer capacity for a period versus named delivered
assets, which is the only distinction that predicts what breaks at volume.

## R1. What has to be true

1. Published subscription pricing exists in two distinct bands, not one.
2. Published terms describe a queue with an active-request cap, not unlimited concurrency.
3. Published scope language excludes capture, talent and rights from the fee.
4. The mid-market arithmetic holds using only published floors.

## The mid-market arithmetic, shown not asserted

The brief asks what a brand spending roughly $3,000 to $12,000 a month on
production actually faces at the current published floors. Every input below
is a published number from the claims table. The article shows this
calculation on the page rather than stating a conclusion.

**Step 1. Where the published tiers actually sit.**

| Published band | Lowest listed tier | Highest listed tier | Term | What the fee buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Band B, unlimited-request | about $700 a month | about $4,500 a month | Month to month | 1 to 3 active requests at a time, no hours published |
| Band A, managed | about $6,000 a month | about $15,000 a month plus a $1,000 monthly software fee | Monthly at the low end, 12-month commitment at the high end | 120 to 180 hours a month, 2 to 3 simultaneous projects |
| Band A, dedicated | from about $30,000 a month | n/a | 12-month commitment | A standing team |

**Step 2. The window has a hole in it.** The highest published unlimited tier
is about $4,500 a month. The lowest published managed tier is $5,995 a month.
Between those two numbers, roughly $1,500 wide, no card checked in September
2026 publishes a tier at all.

**Step 3. Capacity per dollar, where it is published.** Only the managed band
publishes a denominator.

- $5,995 divided by 120 hours = $49.96 an hour.
- $8,995 divided by 180 hours = $49.97 an hour.
- 120 hours over about 22 working days = about 5.5 hours of capacity a day.
- 180 hours over about 22 working days = about 8.2 hours a day, which is
  roughly one full-time creative, plus direction.

So a brand at the top of its stated range, $12,000 a month, can buy the
$8,995 tier and still hold about $3,000 back. What it gets for the $8,995 is
about one person-day of creative capacity per working day, three lanes running
at once, and hours that expire at the end of the month.

**Step 4. What the same brand cannot reach.** The next published step up is a
$15,000 monthly minimum plus a $1,000 monthly software fee, on a 12-month
commitment. That is $16,000 a month and $192,000 committed for the year. A
brand budgeting $12,000 a month is $4,000 a month short of the floor and
cannot sign the term.

**Step 5. The unit problem, which is the article.** In band B the buyer cannot
compute a unit cost at all, because no card publishes hours or asset counts,
only how many requests may be active at once. In band A the buyer can compute
a unit cost of access ($50 an hour) but still not a unit cost per delivered
asset, because no card commits to a number of finished assets. In both bands
the priced unit is capacity for a period. Nothing in either band prices a
delivered asset.

**Step 6. Where the queue bites.** Concurrency is the binding constraint, not
the word "unlimited". At published tiers, work in progress is capped at 1 to 5
items at a time across every card checked. A campaign that needs 40 finished
assets in the same two weeks does not fit in 3 lanes, no matter how many
requests the buyer is allowed to file, because filing a request and starting
it are different events. The queue absorbs the difference, and the schedule
pays for it.
