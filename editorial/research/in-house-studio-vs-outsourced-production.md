> **Handling rule for this file.** No domain, brand or product name below ever
> reaches the article. Every figure on the page is attributed to the category,
> the institution type and the date. Vendor domains sit in "Do not publish" so
> the next writer can re-verify without re-searching.

> **The structural point of this piece, stated once so the writer does not lose
> it.** An in-house studio's cost has three layers. Layer one, salary, is
> published by a national statistical agency with a stated method. Layer two,
> the benefits load carried on top of salary, is also published by a national
> statistical agency with a stated method. Layer three is everything else:
> recruitment, replacement, idle capacity between campaigns, software seats,
> freelancer surge, and the share of a salaried creative's year absorbed by
> trafficking and versioning rather than making. **Layer three has no published
> benchmark anywhere.** Not from a trade body, not from a statistical agency,
> not from an academic source. Every number circulating for layer three is
> published by a party that sells one side of the decision. The article builds
> layers one and two honestly, names layer three as the hole it is, and gives
> the reader an instrument to measure it inside their own company.

### What the four SERPs share

Every page publishes a fully loaded annual number. The bands seen across the
four SERPs, none of them sourced: roughly $80,000 for one designer, $240,000
to $360,000 for a team of three, $250,000 to $350,000 for a minimum viable
unit, $280,000 to $420,000 before tools, $350,000 to $480,000 with tools, and
about $660,000 for five people. A benefits multiplier of 1.25 to 1.4 appears
repeatedly with no attribution. A benefits load of "about 30 percent of
salary" appears with no attribution. **Not one page on any of the four SERPs
cites a statistical agency, a trade body, or any source at all for those
multipliers.** Not one page states a utilization rate. Not one page publishes
a method. Not one page carries a visible publication date on the figure
itself.

**The bar:** the ranking set carries one or two illustrative tables, roughly
1,200 to 2,000 words, no citations, no dates and no method. Three sourced
tables, a dated national wage series, a dated national compensation series,
one worked model built only from sourced inputs, and an explicit map of what
is not published clears every page found, by a wide margin.

**The gap, in one sentence:** Every page ranking for this decision publishes a
fully loaded in-house number, and not one of them sources the two multipliers
that number depends on, the benefits load and the utilization rate, so the
whole category is arithmetic resting on a benchmark that does not exist.

Listed before looking anything up, per the gate. Each is marked with what
happened to it.

1. Salary levels for the named creative roles are published by a large-sample,

   May 2025 reference period.**

   March 2026 reference period.**
3. In-house agency adoption is measured by a trade body with a stated
   population and respondent count. **True, with a caveat on age. Cleared for

4. Production spend as a share of the marketing budget is published with a
   method, so the reader can size the pool the decision sits inside. **True.
   Already cleared in the ledger, reused, not re-sourced.**
5. A published utilization or billable-hours benchmark exists for salaried
   in-house creatives. **FALSE. Nothing found with a stated method. Cut from
   the outline as a claim, converted into the article's central question.**
6. A published benchmark exists for the share of an in-house creative team's
   hours absorbed by trafficking, versioning and coordination rather than
   origination. **FALSE. Nothing found. Cut as a claim.**
7. A published cost-per-hire or recruitment-cost benchmark exists for creative
   roles specifically. **FALSE for creative roles. Cut. Generic cost-per-hire
   figures exist but none is creative-specific with a published method, and the
   most-cited one is over a decade old.**
8. A published benchmark exists for idle capacity between campaigns. **FALSE.
   Nothing found on either side of the market.**
9. Software seat cost per creative seat is published by the vendors
   themselves. **Partly true, but excluded on the standing rule: it would mean
   naming software vendors and their rate cards, which is a company
   attribution. Handled as a named line with no figure.**

Four of the nine things the piece would ideally assert cannot be sourced.
That is the article.

## The arithmetic, shown not asserted

Every input below is a sourced row from the claims table. The article shows
the calculation on the page rather than stating a conclusion, exactly as the
sibling cost pieces do.

**Step 1. Salary is published. Start there and nowhere else.**

| Role | Published median annual wage, May 2025 | Published 10th percentile | Published 90th percentile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Graphic designer | $62,960 | under $39,520 | over $104,910 |
| Film and video editor | $75,420 | under $40,280 | over $148,550 |
| Camera operator, television, video and film | $74,990 | under $38,520 | over $135,180 |
| Art director | $114,850 | under $64,900 | over $213,050 |

The percentile spread is the first thing every ranking page hides behind a
single number. A graphic designer is a $39,520 role and a $104,910 role in the
same published series, a spread of more than two and a half times. Any model
built on one salary figure is a model built on a choice nobody declared.

**Step 2. The benefits load is published too, and it is not 1.25.**

March 2026, private industry, per hour worked: total employer compensation
cost $46.60, of which wages and salaries $32.60 and benefits $14.01. Benefits
are 30.1 percent of total compensation, which is the number the SERP pages
half-remember. Expressed the way a budget holder actually needs it, as a
markup on salary rather than a share of the total, $46.60 divided by $32.60 is
**1.43**.

So the SERP consensus multiplier of 1.25 to 1.4 sits below the published
figure at every point in its range. The pages that publish the friendlier
multiplier are the ones selling in-house tooling and staffing. The pages that
publish the fiercer one sell outsourcing. Neither cites the series that
settles it.

**Step 3. Wages plus benefits for a four-person unit, using only published
inputs.**

| Line | Published input | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Art director, median | May 2025 wage series | $114,850 |
| Film and video editor, median | May 2025 wage series | $75,420 |
| Graphic designer, median | May 2025 wage series | $62,960 |
| Graphic designer, median, second seat | May 2025 wage series | $62,960 |
| Salary subtotal | Sum | $316,190 |
| Salary plus benefits at the published private-industry ratio | 1.429448 multiplier, March 2026 series | $451,977 |

Rounded, a four-seat unit at published national medians costs about **$452,000
a year in wages and benefits alone**, before a single line of layer three.
That is the honest floor, and it is higher than most of the fully loaded totals
circulating on the SERPs for teams the same size, which is the tell that those
totals were built from a multiplier somebody guessed.

**Step 4. Layer three, named line by line, with no figure attached to any of
it.** This is the table the article must publish, and it publishes empty cells
on purpose.

| Line | Is there a published benchmark? |
|---|---|
| Recruitment and agency fees to fill each seat | No creative-specific benchmark found |
| Replacement cost when a seat turns over | No creative-specific benchmark found |
| Idle capacity between campaigns | None found on either side of the market |
| Share of the year on trafficking, versioning and routing rather than origination | None found |
| Freelancer surge at peak | None found |
| Software seats per creative | Published by the software vendors, so it cannot be cited here without naming companies |
| Studio space, equipment, storage and compute | None found as a per-seat benchmark |
| Management overhead above the unit | None found |

Eight lines. Zero published benchmarks. Every fully loaded model on the four
SERPs fills these eight lines with numbers, and none of them says where the
numbers came from.

**Step 5. The utilization question, which is where the whole comparison turns.**

Wages and benefits are paid on the calendar. Output happens on the campaign
schedule. The two do not line up, and no published source measures the gap.

What the article can do honestly is show the sensitivity rather than assert a
rate. Take the $452,000 floor from step 3 and divide it by output at different
utilization assumptions, labeling every assumption as the reader's input and
not a benchmark:

| Share of paid hours that produces originated output | Effective cost of the same $452,000 unit, per productive hour |
|---|---|
| 80 percent | about $68 |
| 60 percent | about $91 |
| 40 percent | about $136 |

Method for that column, which must appear on the page: four seats at 2,080
paid hours a year is 8,320 paid hours. $451,977 divided by 8,320 is $54.32 an
hour paid. Divide that by the utilization share to get cost per productive
hour ($67.91, $90.54 and $135.81, rounded on the page). **Every one of those
three rows is a hypothetical, not a benchmark.**
The article says so in the same breath, in the table caption, not in a footnote.

Only then does the outsourced comparison land: at the managed end of the
subscription category, the published cards price access at about $50 a creative
hour, and those hours are only billed when bought. That is a published number
against three hypothetical ones, and the article must be explicit that this is
an asymmetry in the evidence, not a verdict. The buyer knows the outsourced
denominator because a card publishes it. The buyer does not know the in-house
denominator because nobody publishes it, including the companies running the
studios.

**Step 6. The fixed against variable argument, which needs no new figures.**
Wages and benefits recur whether or not a campaign runs. The published
subscription floors recur too, on a twelve-month term at the top of the
managed band, which is $192,000 committed for the year. Only project-priced
production is genuinely variable. That is a three-way distinction the SERPs
collapse into two, and it needs no source beyond the published terms already
cleared.

## R6. The two conflicts, published as ranges with the reason

The gate requires that where sources disagree, the range is published and the
reason for the disagreement is stated. Two conflicts matter here, and in both
cases the reason for the conflict is more useful to the reader than any number
inside it.

### Conflict 1: the utilization benchmark that does not measure in-house teams

Searched September 2026 for a published utilization or billable-hours
benchmark for creative staff. Nine results, every one of them published by a
seller of time-tracking software, agency-management software or agency
financial consulting. The bands they publish:

| Band published | Population it names |
|---|---|
| 60 to 70 percent | "creative agencies" |
| 70 to 80 percent | "creative agencies", different publisher |
| 75 to 85 percent | "creative and marketing agencies" |
| 65 to 80 percent | "creative staff", attributed to a management institute |
| 60 to 70 percent | designers and strategists, UK |
| 75 to 85 percent | producers and delivery-impacting roles, attributed to a research firm |

Not one publishes a sample size, a fielding date or an instrument. But the
disqualifying problem is not the missing method, it is the population.
**Every one of these bands measures billable utilization at an agency.** An
in-house studio does not bill. It has no billable hours, no realization rate
and, in most companies, no timesheet against a client code at all. The number
being quoted at brands considering an in-house studio was measured on a
business model the in-house studio does not run.

That is the publishable finding: the utilization benchmark the buyer will be
shown is measured on the wrong entity. **The bands themselves stay off the
page.** The article states that agency billability benchmarks exist, that they
disagree with each other across a fifteen-point spread, that none publishes a
method, and that none of them measures an in-house team. Then it hands the
reader the arithmetic to measure their own.

### Conflict 2: cost per hire, three medians from one publisher

The most-cited cost-per-hire source is a US human-resources membership body.
Three different medians attributed to it are circulating in September 2026:
about $1,300 for non-executive roles, about $4,700 as an internal cost per
hire, and about $5,475 as a non-executive benchmark. All three are relayed
through recruitment-vendor blogs rather than read at the publisher, the
reference years differ, and the definitions of what the figure counts differ
with them. None is creative-specific.

**Cut, all three.** A recruitment line belongs in the model because it is
real, but it goes in with no figure attached, like the other seven layer-three
lines. The conflict is worth one sentence on the page as evidence for why the
line is empty: even the most widely quoted staffing benchmark in the market
appears as three different numbers in the same year, none of them measured on
a creative role.

## Notes the writer must not lose

1. **The asymmetry is the story, and it must be stated as an asymmetry.** The
   outsourced side publishes a denominator. The in-house side does not. That is
   a fact about disclosure, not proof that one model is cheaper. Any sentence
   that slides from the first to the second gets cut.
2. **Every utilization figure on the page is the reader's input.** Label it in
   the same sentence, in the table caption, and in the FAQ answer. Three
   labels, not one.
3. **The 1.43 multiplier is US private-industry data.** The audience is
   international. Say the geography in the same sentence every time, and say
   that a reader outside the US must substitute their own national series. Do
   not present it as a global constant.
4. **The wage series is May 2025, the compensation series is March 2026.** They
   are different reference periods and the article must not blend them into
   "current". State each date where the figure appears.
5. **The 82 percent figure is three and a half years old.** The brief asks for
   it and it is properly sourced, so it runs, but only with "fielded February
   and March 2023" in the same sentence. Pair it with the December 2023
   multinational figure so the reader sees two independent trade bodies rather
   than one number carrying the whole claim.
6. **When in-house is genuinely right does not need a new figure.** It falls
   out of steps 5 and 6: high, steady, predictable volume of adaptation work
   with a stable brand system, where utilization stays high and the calendar is
   full. The moment volume is spiky or the work is originated rather than
   adapted, the fixed cost is carried through the troughs. Argue it, do not
   source it.
7. **The hybrid model is the third option, not a compromise.** Platform for the
   steady adaptation volume, studio for originated capture. Reference hub4you,
   the consulting solutions page and the quotation calculator by name in body
   copy, per the brief. No markdown links.
8. **No hubStudio rate, monthly figure or per-item price.** Every dollar figure
   on the finished page is either a national statistical series or a
   category-level published band. Search the draft for the dollar sign and
   check every hit.
9. **No competitor named, described, compared to or alluded to.** The
   comparison is in-house against outsourced, as models. The SERP audit is
   published as a publisher-type count, never as a list.
