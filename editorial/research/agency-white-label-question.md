**Publisher-type counts.** 31 results across four queries. 26 are published by
a seller of white-label services, agency software or agency finance services;
2 are encyclopedia entries (1 off-intent); 1 is a personal post; 1 is a review
platform; 1 is a service page whose body did not load. Zero of 31 come from a
trade body, a government IP office, a bar association, a law school or a court.
Fourteen pages were fetched: 11 served a body, 1 served a title only, 2
refused (404 and 403).

**The bar:** 450 to 4,500 words, zero to two tables, no instrument cited on any
page. The NDA pages assert that NDAs are standard without saying what goes in
one. The contact page with the most structure gives four contact models and one
table. The margin pages either treat outsourced work as pass-through at cost or
print unsourced markup bands. No page covers the ownership chain across two
contracts, trained model ownership, or AI disclosure to the end client. The bar
to beat is 2,000 words with an NDA clause checklist, a contact rules table, an
ownership chain table, and a margin section that shows its structure and says
plainly that no method-stated figure exists.

**The gap, in one sentence:** Every page ranking for these phrasings is
published by a seller of white-label services or agency software, so the
category promises invisibility and quotes unsourced markups, and not one page
maps the NDA, the contact rules, the ownership of work and trained models, and
AI disclosure onto the two contracts an agency actually signs, or admits that
no method-stated markup figure exists.

**H1 consequence:** the ranking pages sell the arrangement and skip the paper.
The working H1 already names the three documents buyers search for (NDA,
contact rules, margin per asset) and can stay. The first H2 should restate the
query and say it is about two contracts, the agency's with its client and the
agency's with its supplier, because that frame is what no ranking page has.

**R4 note.** The subject is not China-specific, but the supplier may be. One
Chinese-language search was run for trade association guidance on generative
AI in advertising and disclosure (query: 中国广告协会 生成式人工智能 广告 应用 指引 披露). It
returned association homepages, a lecture notice, a talent standard and an
academic PDF, and no published disclosure guidance. The China-side ownership
rule (Copyright Law Article 19 on commissioned works) and the labeling
instrument are reused from the ledger, where both were read on primary Chinese
surfaces. The session's search budget was exhausted before a second Chinese
pass on outsourcing confidentiality could run; that is recorded as a gap, not
as an absence.

## R6. Where sources conflict

**Markup on outsourced production: no range is publishable, and that is the
finding.** Seller pages print a "standard" production markup of 25 percent with
a 20 to 35 percent spread (search extracts only, never fetched), white-label
markups of 30 to 50 percent and 30 to 100 percent (one software vendor, no
source), and a pass-through share rule of thumb from one consultant. The US
commercial production trade body, in its own guidelines revised April 2020,
describes the production fee as a markup percentage and then declines to set
one: fee and percentage amounts are "strictly a matter of independent
determination by each production company". The advertiser trade body's
compensation reports, the only buy-side survey series found, returned 403 on
two pages and 404 on a mirror, so no figure from them was read. Why they
conflict: the numbers come from parties selling the service or software they
describe, and the trade body with a method has chosen, on the face of its own
document, not to publish a number. The page publishes the structure and the
absence, never a band.

**NDA duration: three readings, no conflict of fact.** The UK IPO guidance says
3 or 5 years is common. Its own example NDAs leave the term as a choice between
"indefinitely" and a number of years. hubStudio's terms of service set two years
after an engagement ends. The WIPO guide says a trade secret holder should
prefer a permanent obligation. These describe different interests (a discloser
of a trade secret, an inventor, a studio's standard terms), so the page presents
duration as a field the parties set, and never sets hubStudio's two years
against the IPO's sentence as if one were a benchmark for the other.

**Contact with the end client: two models that point opposite ways.** Seller
pages say a white-label supplier never contacts the client. The US production
trade body's guidelines tell a production company, where payment protection is
weak, to obtain the advertiser's billing and contact details, copy the
advertiser on invoices, and notify the advertiser when the agency misses a
payment date; its standard agreement names the advertiser on the face of the
contract and makes agency and advertiser jointly and severally liable. Why they
conflict: the trade body writes for a disclosed principal, where the supplier
knows who the advertiser is. White-label is built on the advertiser not knowing
the supplier. The page must say that the protections suppliers rely on in the
disclosed model disappear in the undisclosed one, and that the contact rules
therefore have to be written into the supplier contract rather than assumed
from industry practice.

**Ownership timing: a condition, not a conflict.** The US standard agreement
vests copyright in the contracting client (section 5) and says ownership does
not transfer until full payment (section 12). hubStudio's terms say the same of
its own work. Across a white-label chain this stacks: the agency cannot pass to
its client what it does not yet hold from its supplier.

**Who owns a commissioned work by default: the two jurisdictions invert.** In
the US a commissioned work is a work made for hire only within nine categories
and only with a signed written agreement; otherwise the creator is ordinarily
the author. Under China's Copyright Law Article 19, copyright in a commissioned
work belongs to the commissioned party where the contract is silent. Both point
the same practical way (write it down), from opposite defaults, and the page
says so without stating what a court would do with a particular contract.

### Ownership of work and trained models

> A work is "made for hire" in two situations: when an employee creates it
> within the scope of employment, or when it is specially ordered or
> commissioned in one of nine categories and "the parties expressly agree in a
> written instrument signed by them that the work shall be considered a work
> made for hire." The nine categories are a contribution to a collective work,
> part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a
> supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer
> material for a test, and an atlas.
> Source: US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised August
> 2024; the Office's circular read in full.
> https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf

> The Office lists four criteria a commissioned work must meet: it falls within
> one of the nine categories, there is a written agreement, the agreement
> expressly says the work is made for hire, and all parties sign it. "If a work
> fails to satisfy any of these requirements, it is not a work made for hire."
> Ordinarily, the Office notes, the author is the person who actually created
> the work.
> Source: US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised August
> 2024; the Office's circular read in full.
> https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf

> Copyright protects only material that is the product of human creativity, and
> where a generative system produces the expressive elements of a work, that
> material is not protected by copyright.
> Source: US Copyright Office, registration guidance for works containing
> material generated by artificial intelligence, 88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023, a
> statement of policy published by the issuing office.
> https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/html/2023-05321.htm

> Users do not exercise ultimate creative control over how a generative system
> interprets prompts. The guidance puts it this way: prompts "function more like
> instructions to a commissioned artist. They identify what the prompter wishes
> to have depicted, but the machine determines how those instructions are
> implemented."
> Source: US Copyright Office, registration guidance, 88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023.
> https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/html/2023-05321.htm

> Under China's Copyright Law, ownership of a commissioned work is set by the
> contract, and where the contract is not explicit or there is no contract,
> copyright belongs to the commissioned party. A transfer of economic rights
> requires a written contract.
> Source: the Copyright Law of the People's Republic of China as amended

## Notes for the writer

### The frame that makes the page different

Every ranking page sells the arrangement. This page reads the paper. The spine
is two contracts: the agency's contract with its client (call it the client
contract) and the agency's contract with its supplier (the supplier contract).
Every must-include is a question of what each contract says and what happens
when they disagree. Say early that the page describes production practice, not
legal advice, and that a lawyer drafts both contracts.

The single most useful insight in this file, and no ranking page has it: the
published US production standard is built for a disclosed principal. The
advertiser is named on the supplier's contract, agency and advertiser are
jointly and severally liable, and the supplier's trade body tells it to copy
the advertiser on invoices when payment is at risk. White-label removes the
advertiser from the supplier's view. Invisibility therefore costs the supplier
its usual payment protection, and the contact rules and payment terms that the
disclosed model supplies by default have to be written into the supplier
contract instead.

### Table content guidance

**NDA clause checklist.** Suggested columns: Clause, What it settles, Where the
standard comes from, White-label note. Rows: parties and direction (mutual);
permitted purpose; what counts as confidential, written to include the end
client's identity, the brief, unreleased work and the existence of the
arrangement itself; exclusions; onward disclosure to freelancers and
subcontractors only on equivalent terms; security; return or destruction with
certification; no licence to intellectual property; reel, portfolio, credit and
case-study use; client material not used to train shared models; duration;
governing law; signed before the first brief. Mark rows that come from practice
rather than a cited source as practice. "Existence of the arrangement" and
"client material not used to train shared models" are practice rows: the first
is supported only by hubStudio's own published policy, the second by
hubStudio's pricing page. No printed ordinal in the table.

**Contact rules table.** Columns: Allowed, Needs written consent, Never. These
are contract terms the agency writes, not an industry standard, and the table
caption must say so. Suggested content. Allowed: supplier works only with the
agency's named producer; deliverables go to the agency under the agency brand.
Needs written consent: supplier joins a client call, and in what role; supplier
receives client contact or billing details; supplier uses the work on a reel or
in credentials; supplier subcontracts or assigns; AI-generated content goes into
materials for the client. Never, as written terms: supplier approaches or
pitches the client during the term and an agreed tail; supplier names the
client or the agency's client list; supplier discusses the agency's pricing or
margin with the client. Put the non-solicitation caveat directly under the
table: the clause is only as strong as the law that governs it, BPC 16600 is
one example of a statute that voids restraints on trade to the extent they
apply, and whether a given clause holds is a question for counsel in the
governing jurisdiction. Do not say it is enforceable or unenforceable.

**Ownership chain table.** Rows: finished deliverables; raw capture, out-takes
and working files; prompts and generation records; a custom model trained on
the brand, including its weights; the brand references used to train it; the
supplier's own pre-existing tools and methods. Columns: Supplier contract
should say, Client contract should say, If either is silent. The "if silent"
column states defaults only as the instruments state them (US: not a work made
for hire unless all four criteria are met; China: commissioned party owns
absent contract) and never predicts a court outcome. Carry the payment
condition across both links: the agency cannot hand over what it has not yet
paid for.

**Margin structure, no numbers.** A per-asset build the reader can fill in:
supplier cost; agency handling (brief translation, review, account time,
revision exposure); risk the agency now carries because the supplier cannot see
the client (payment float, indemnity, insurance, rework); margin. Then two
decisions with no figures: cost-plus against firm bid, and whether the client
contract requires the markup and any rebates to be disclosed. Do not print a
percentage anywhere in this section except inside the 17.65 and 15 arithmetic
and the two 2017 survey figures.
