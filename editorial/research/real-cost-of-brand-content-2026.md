**What the four result sets share.** The phrase "brand content cost" splits
three ways on the SERP: content marketing retainers priced per month, creator
sponsorship fees priced per follower or per thousand views, and video
production priced per minute or per film. No page separates asset production
from media, management or talent fees. The figures that recur across pages
(about $3,500 a minute of video, a "2026 survey of 350+ businesses", 30 to 40
percent AI savings) are relayed from page to page without an instrument. Every
fetched page mixes units (per month, per project, per piece, per minute) inside
one list, and none defines an asset before pricing it.

**The bar.** Six pages fetched: all ungated as articles (one offers a gated
report beside the article), all publish absolute figures, none states a
collection method or sample for its own bands, five of six are published by a
seller of the work. Lengths were not measured in this pass. To clear the field:
a master table by asset type with a unit and a date on every row, a visible
inclusion rule, confidence labels in the table itself, a decision table, and an
explicit statement of which of the four tests the page itself fails.

**The gap, in one sentence.** Of 34 results across four buyer phrasings, 28 are
published by a party selling the work being priced and none by a statistical
agency or trade body, and not one states how its bands were collected or which
asset and unit a band covers.

**H1 consequence.** No H1 change needed. The working H1 already carries the
answer ("from published figures"). Because "brand content" collides with
creator sponsorship and paid editorial on this SERP, the first 100 words and
the first H2 should say "production", and the opening should state in one
sentence that the page prices making assets, not creator fees, media or
content marketing retainers. The approved title and meta stay accurate.

## The inclusion rule and exclusions, written to print

Cleared as written for the method box. The writer may tighten the sentences
but may not add a claim.

**What counts as a published figure.** A figure is on this page only if it
meets all four of these conditions. It is an absolute amount, in the currency
its source publishes, against a unit the source states. It was read directly on
the surface that publishes it: a seller's own pricing page, a national
statistical release, or a trade body's own instrument. It carries a date. And
the way it was produced can be stated in one sentence. A rate card is evidence
of what one seller publicly charges on the day it was read, never of a market
average. Where two or more independent cards agree, the band is a range. Where
only one card carries a figure, the table says single-source. Where a number is
arithmetic on published inputs, the inputs and the operation are shown.

**Collection window.** Pricing pages were read on 9 and 10 September 2026 and
the most load-bearing ones read again on 10 September 2026. Three Chinese rate
cards carry their own dates, from June 2023 to 20 July 2026. Statistical series
carry their own reference periods: US wages May 2025, US employer compensation
March 2026, UK earnings for the tax year ending 5 April 2025, Chinese wages for
2024. Trade body instruments are dated January 2023 (bid form), 2026 (bidding
guide) and March 2025 (content production research). One practitioner
disclosure dates from April 2024 and is labeled as such.

**What was excluded, and why.**

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| Savings percentages ("cut costs 30 to 40 percent", "up to 80 percent", "about 40 percent offshore") | A percentage of an unstated baseline cannot be checked or compared, and every one found was published by a party selling the cheaper option |
| Per-ad claims ("$1 to $5 an AI ad") | No publisher in the chain states a sample, an instrument or what a finished ad includes, and each sells generation. Quoted only as a labeled market claim |
| Figures published by the party they flatter, with no method | A seller pricing both sides of its own comparison wins it by construction |
| Aggregator and roundup figures | They restate unnamed sources, so the figure cannot be traced to the surface that set it |
| Gated figures | A reader cannot check a number behind a membership, an email form or a priced book |
| Estimates of a price a seller does not publish | An estimate is not a price |
| Survey figures with no sample, instrument or fielding date | Nothing to test the figure against |
| Figures found in search summaries or secondary pages but not on the source they cite | Includes two "2025" commercial production survey figures that cite a survey discontinued in 2014 |
| Monthly content marketing retainers and creator sponsorship fees | They price management, media or audience reach, not the making of an asset |
