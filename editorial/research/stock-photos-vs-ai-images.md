## R1. What has to be true (mapped before looking anything up)

1. What a stock license actually buys: royalty-free against rights-managed,
   standard against extended, editorial-use-only, the print or copy cap, the
   indemnity or legal-protection cap, exclusivity.
2. What a stock license costs, at category level, with a method; cut if no
   clean method.
3. Who owns a generated image: the US Copyright Office's own conclusions,
   prompts alone, and what happens when your own photo is the input.
4. Whether the human-authorship rule is settled in court (appellate opinion,
   Supreme Court status).
5. Whether an AI product image has to be labeled, and when an edited product
   photo is or is not a deep fake under the EU rules.
6. That a picture standing in for the product can mislead (regulator
   precedent).
7. App facts for the Image studio: jobs, source pictures, price before the
   run, failed runs, History. From the help center and
   hubstudio-positioning.md only.
8. Speed and cost of generation in money: cut from the outline at R1. No
   market per-image AI price has a method behind it (see Do not publish), and
   no hubStudio amount is ever printed.

## Cleared for use

> Copyright "does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material
> where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements,"
> and "prompts do not alone provide sufficient control."
> Source: US Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2:
> Copyrightability, January 29, 2025, executive summary.

> When "a human inputs their own copyrightable work and that work is
> perceptible in the output, they will be the author of at least that portion
> of the output." That copyright "would not extend to the AI-generated
> elements standing alone."
> Source: US Copyright Office, Part 2: Copyrightability, January 29, 2025,
> Section II.E, page 24.

> The Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first
> instance by a human being." The Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal on
> March 2, 2026.
> Source: US Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, Thaler v. Perlmutter, No.
> 23-5233, March 18, 2025; Supreme Court docket No. 25-449.

> Standard licenses cap print runs at 500,000 and cap the library's legal
> protection at $5,000 to $10,000; one library raises that to $250,000 per
> image with an extended license.
> Source: four published stock license agreements and three plan pages, read
> October 12, 2026, category level, no library named.

> Entry stock subscriptions start at about $29 to $30 a month, which works
> out to roughly $3 to $10 a standard image.
> Source: lowest monthly plans on three stock libraries' public price pages,
> collected October 12, 2026; per-image figures as printed, or the monthly
> price divided by the images it includes.

> An AI image of a product that can mislead "as to the actual product
> appearance, characteristics or use" counts as a deep fake. A real product
> "shown in an advertisement against an AI-generated background" does not, as
> long as the ad does not mislead about the product.
> Source: European Commission guidelines on Article 50 of the AI Act,
> C(2026) 5054, July 20, 2026, examples after paragraph 116.

> A stock photo with readable number plates added, shown as footage from the
> advertised dash cams, was "misleading, because it was not taken from
> footage from any of the advertised dash cams."
> Source: UK advertising regulator ruling, June 5, 2024.

> "If using pre- or post-production techniques, images should not be altered
> in a way which exaggerates what the product can do."
> Source: UK advertising regulator guidance on misleading imagery, September
> 11, 2025.
